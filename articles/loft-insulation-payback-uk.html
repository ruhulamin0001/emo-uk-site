Loft Insulation Pays Back In Four Years. Unless You Already Have Some, In Which Case It Is Thirty-Five.

Loft insulation is the example everyone reaches for when they want to say an energy improvement pays for itself, and from an empty loft it does, fast. What the advice rarely says is that the same job on a loft that already has 120mm of the stuff saves £17 a year against a £600 professional bill. We took the Energy Saving Trust's own cost and saving tables, by house type, and turned them into the only number that matters: how many years until the money comes back.

The short version

  • An uninsulated roof loses about a quarter of a home's heat, and insulation lasts around 40 years, both Energy Saving Trust figures.
  • From nothing to 270mm, a semi costs about £750 professionally installed and saves about £190 a year in Great Britain, a payback of about 4 years. Detached: £1,100 to save £360, about 3 years. Mid-terrace: £650 to save £140, about 4.6 years.
  • Topping up from 120mm to 270mm in a semi costs about £600 and saves about £17 a year, a payback of 35 years. In a mid-terrace it is 38 years.
  • Over the full 40-year life the top-up returns £680 on £600, so it is not a loss, just a very poor investment when done professionally.
  • Done yourself, the top-up is a different sum, because the material is a small fraction of the installed price. Check your loft depth with a tape measure before you get a quote.
  • EST figures are at July 2026 energy prices. They rise and fall with the gas price cap, and so does every payback here.

1. The Energy Saving Trust figures, by house type

The Energy Saving Trust publishes typical professional installation costs and annual bill savings for loft insulation, split by house type and by how much insulation is already there. Its page was last updated on 8 May 2026 and the savings are based on fuel prices as of July 2026, for a gas-heated home in England, Scotland or Wales. These are its figures, unchanged.

Detached bungalow£1,200£350£800£35

Two things about the source. The Energy Saving Trust is an independent charity, not a seller of insulation, so it has no reason to flatter the numbers. And the costs are for unsubsidised professional installation, which the EST says will vary; your quotes will not match to the pound. The top-up figures are labelled as based on 2022 data in the EST's chart title while the from-nothing figures are 2024, so treat the smaller numbers as slightly older.

The EST also gives two facts that frame everything else. An uninsulated home loses around 25% of its heat through the roof. And loft insulation, installed correctly, lasts about 40 years. So a job done once will still be working when the boiler has been replaced twice.

2. Payback in years: from nothing versus a top-up

Payback is the installed cost divided by the annual saving. This is our arithmetic on the EST's figures.

Detached house3.1 years22.9 years£1,400 on £800
Semi-detached house3.9 years35.3 years£680 on £600
Mid-terrace house4.6 years38.5 years£520 on £500
Detached bungalow3.4 years22.9 years£1,400 on £800

Read across the semi-detached row, because it is the most common house in Britain. From an empty loft, £750 comes back in under four years and then saves £190 a year for another 36. That is roughly £7,600 of gas over the life of the material for a one-off £750, and there are very few things you can do to a house that come close.

From 120mm, the same trade with the same rolls charges £600 and the bill falls by £17. Thirty-five years. Over the entire 40-year life the top-up returns £680, so it is technically not a loss, but a savings account would have beaten it comfortably, and you would have had the money.

Bungalows and detached houses do better on both rows simply because they have more roof per room. Terraces do worst because a mid-terrace shares two walls and its roof is a smaller share of its heat loss.

3. Why the top-up saves so little

Insulation works on diminishing returns. The first 100mm of mineral wool stops most of the heat that was leaving through the ceiling. The next 100mm stops most of what is left, which is already a much smaller amount. By the time you are adding the last 150mm on top of 120mm, you are chasing a fraction of a fraction.

That is why building regulations ask for 270mm on new work, because on a new house the extra material is cheap at the point of building and the house will stand for a century. It is not because 120mm is failing. A loft with 120mm is already doing most of the job, which is exactly what the EST's £17 says.

So the first thing to do, before any quote, is get a tape measure into the loft. If you can see the joists, which are usually 100mm deep, and the insulation is level with them or below, you are in the from-nothing to from-100mm territory and the job pays quickly. If the insulation is above the joists and you cannot see them, you probably have 150mm or more and the top-up is a comfort purchase, not a money one. The Department for Energy published a printable ruler for this, linked from the EST page.

4. DIY, grants, and the things that change the sum

Doing it yourself changes the top-up sum completely. The £600 professional figure is mostly labour, access and margin. The rolls themselves are a fraction of it, priced per roll at any DIY chain; we are not quoting a figure because it moves, but check the price of enough 170mm top-up roll to cover your loft floor and divide by £17. For many lofts it will be a payback of a few years rather than thirty-five, for a Saturday's work. The EST says DIY is fine if the loft is easy to access, free of damp and not a flat roof. Lay the second layer at right angles to the joists, do not squash it, do not cover the eaves vents, and insulate the pipes and tank once the loft goes cold.

Boards on top need a raised floor. If you store things up there, squashing 270mm of wool under boards laid straight on the joists throws away much of the benefit. Loft legs or a raised deck add cost, and that cost belongs in your payback sum.

Grants exist and they change everything for the eligible. The Great British Insulation Scheme and ECO4 fund loft insulation for households on certain benefits or in lower council tax bands with poor EPC ratings, and the EST says fully funded support under the Warm Homes Plan is coming for low-income homes with low- and zero-interest loans for others. If any of that applies to you, the cost side of the sum is zero and the payback is immediate. The EST keeps a grant finder by postcode.

Damp first, insulation second. A cooler loft is a loft where condensation is more likely. The EST is blunt that existing damp must be fixed before any insulation goes in, and that spray foam sold as a fix for a leaking roof is not one. If a salesperson offers to insulate over a damp problem, that is the end of the conversation.

5. Worked example: the Patel family in Leicester

1960s semi, gas central heating, loft last touched when they bought the house

The Patels have a quote for £620 to bring the loft up to 270mm. Before signing, they get a tape measure into the hatch.

Finding A: the insulation is level with the joists, about 100mm. That is close to the EST's 120mm row. Professional top-up: £620 to save about £17 a year, payback 36 years. They decline the quote, buy top-up rolls for the loft floor themselves, and lay them across the joists over a weekend. If the rolls cost them a tenth of the quote, the payback is under four years. Over the 40-year life the £17 a year adds up to £680 either way; the only question was how much of it went to the installer.

Finding B: there is nothing up there but dust and a water tank. Now they are on the from-nothing row. £620 to save about £190 a year is a payback of about 3.3 years, and because they would want the tank jacketed and the pipes lagged at the same time, the professional quote is the sensible route. Over 40 years that is around £7,600 of gas for £620.

Either way, the saving is in gas. At the October 2026 cap rate of 7.97p per kWh, £190 a year is about 2,400 kWh of gas, roughly a fifth of the 11,500 kWh Ofgem treats as typical. That is the scale of the from-nothing job: a fifth of the heating bill, for the price of a weekend away.

6. The honest verdict

If your loft is empty or has only a thin old layer at or below the joists, insulate it to 270mm now, professionally if you want the tank and pipes done properly. On the Energy Saving Trust's figures it is a three to five year payback and then decades of savings, and if you qualify for a grant it is free.

If your loft already has 120mm or more, do not pay a professional to top it up for the savings. At £17 a year in a semi it is a 35-year payback, the worst return of any common home energy job. Do it yourself for the price of the rolls if you want the last few pounds and a warmer landing, and put the £600 you saved towards something with a shorter payback.

Frequently asked

How much does loft insulation save per year? Energy Saving Trust figures at July 2026 prices: from an uninsulated loft to 270mm, about £140 a year in a mid-terrace, £190 in a semi and £360 in a detached house in Great Britain. Topping up from 120mm saves £13 to £35 depending on house type.

How long does loft insulation last? Around 40 years if installed correctly, according to the Energy Saving Trust, which is why even the slow top-up eventually returns more than it cost.

Can I get loft insulation free? Possibly. The Great British Insulation Scheme and ECO4 fund it for eligible households, and the Warm Homes Plan is due to add fully funded support for low-income homes and cheap loans for others. Use the EST grant finder with your postcode.

Should I top up loft insulation myself? If the loft is easy to reach, dry and not a flat roof, the EST says yes. The material is cheap compared with the installed price, which is what turns a 35-year payback into a short one.
