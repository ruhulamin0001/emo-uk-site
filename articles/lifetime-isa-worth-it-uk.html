Is a Lifetime ISA Worth It? The 25% Bonus Is Real, and So Is the Bit Where You Lose Your Own Money

The Lifetime ISA is the only savings account in Britain where the government tops up your money by a quarter. It is also the only one where taking your money out for the wrong reason costs you more than the top-up was worth. Both facts are in the rules, and the Treasury has now said in writing that the product is not working well for many. Here is who should still open one, who should not, and the sums.

The short version

  • You can pay in up to £4,000 a year and the government adds 25%, so up to £1,000 a year. You must open it before 40 and can pay in until 50. The £4,000 counts inside your £20,000 ISA allowance. Source: GOV.UK, Lifetime ISA, read 4 September 2026.
  • You can take the money out without charge for three reasons only: a first home costing £450,000 or less bought with a mortgage at least 12 months after your first payment, reaching 60, or terminal illness. Anything else is an unauthorised withdrawal.
  • The unauthorised withdrawal charge is 25% of the amount withdrawn, not 20%. GOV.UK's own example: £800 in, £200 bonus, £1,000 pot, £250 charge, £750 back. You lose £50 of your own money, or 6.25%.
  • The £450,000 cap has not changed since the product launched in April 2017. The Treasury's June 2026 consultation says more LISA holders have lost part of their original savings than have used one to buy a home, and unauthorised withdrawals hit 8% of accounts opened in 2024-25.
  • A replacement First Time Buyer ISA is being consulted on. No launch date is set. Existing LISAs continue, keep earning the bonus, and can be used alongside the new product for the same purchase. Do not close one because of the announcement.
  • Our verdict: worth it if you are confident you will buy a first home under £450,000 and can leave the money alone. Not worth it as a rainy-day account, and usually beaten by a workplace pension for retirement saving.

1. The rules as they stand today

The Lifetime ISA, usually shortened to LISA, launched on 6 April 2017 and the core rules have not moved since. You must be 18 or over and under 40 to open one, and you must be resident in the UK (or a Crown servant or armed forces member, or their spouse). You can pay in up to £4,000 in each tax year until the day before your 50th birthday, and the government adds a 25% bonus on whatever you paid in, so a maximum of £1,000 a year. The bonus is paid monthly by HMRC into the account, so it starts earning interest or investment growth almost straight away.

The £4,000 is not on top of your ISA allowance. It sits inside the £20,000 annual ISA limit for 2026 to 2027, so someone maxing a LISA has £16,000 left for cash and stocks and shares ISAs. You can hold cash, investments, or both. From 6 April 2027 the separate cash ISA limit for under-65s drops to £12,000, which we covered in our cash ISA changes guide, but that change does not touch the LISA's own £4,000.

There are exactly three ways to take the money out without a charge. First, buying your first home: the property must cost £450,000 or less, you must be buying with a mortgage, a solicitor or conveyancer must handle the purchase and receive the funds directly, and the purchase must complete at least 12 months after your first payment into the account. Second, reaching 60. Third, being terminally ill with less than 12 months to live. If you die, the account ends on that date and there is no charge on what is passed on. Everything else, from a car to a wedding to an unexpected bill, is what GOV.UK calls an unauthorised withdrawal.

2. The withdrawal charge, and why 25% means 6.25% of your money

This is the part most people get wrong, and the arithmetic is worth doing slowly because the number is not what it looks like. The government adds 25% on the way in. The charge is 25% on the way out. Those sound like they cancel. They do not, because the 25% on the way out is charged on a bigger number.

GOV.UK's own worked example, quoted here because it is the official one: you save £800, the government adds £200, and you have £1,000. You withdraw the lot for an unauthorised reason. The charge is 25% of £1,000, which is £250. You walk away with £750. That is £50 less than the £800 you paid in. In percentage terms you have lost 6.25% of your own money, before counting any interest you would have earned in an ordinary account.

The second official example is subtler. If you need £120 in cash to cover a bill, you cannot withdraw £120. You have to withdraw £160, of which £40 is taken as the charge, to end up with £120 in your hand. Every emergency withdrawal costs a third more than the emergency.

3. The £450,000 cap and the people it quietly excludes

The cap is the same £450,000 it was on launch day in 2017, and it applies across the whole UK. The Treasury's consultation document, published 23 June 2026, notes that the Treasury Select Committee argued the cap ensures the support goes to people who need it most, and that raising it would come with a cost. Read that as: it is not going up.

The practical consequence is a trap that is invisible until exchange of contracts. Someone who opened a LISA at 25 where £450,000 buys a family house, then moves for work to a part of London or the South East where it does not, has a pot that cannot be used for the home they are actually buying. The choice is the 25% charge or leaving the pot until 60.

The consultation's own numbers show how common the exit route has become. Unauthorised withdrawal charges rose year on year to 8% of all accounts opened in 2024 to 2025, and, in the Treasury's words, more LISA holders have lost a part of their original savings than have used it to purchase a house. That sentence is the reason the product is being replaced, and it is the reason the account needs to be opened with a clear plan rather than as a general savings pot.

4. A worked example in Leicester

Amara is 29, rents in Leicester, and expects to buy a two bedroom house locally in about four years. She opens a cash LISA and pays in £333 a month, close to the £4,000 annual maximum. We will ignore interest so the mechanics are clear.

After four years she has paid in £16,000 and the government has added £4,000, so the pot is £20,000. She finds a house at £215,000, well under the cap, with a mortgage. Her solicitor draws down the full £20,000 as deposit. The £4,000 bonus is hers outright. On our arithmetic she has earned a guaranteed 25% on four years of saving, which no savings account, Premium Bond or cash ISA will match, and it is tax free on top.

Now the other branch. In year three her circumstances change: a relationship, a job in another city, a decision to keep renting. She wants the money for a deposit on a rental and furniture. She withdraws the full pot, which by then is £15,000 (£12,000 of her own money plus £3,000 bonus). The charge is 25% of £15,000, which is £3,750. She receives £11,250. That is £750 less than she paid in.

The same £12,000 drip-fed into an easy access account paying, say, 4% would have been worth roughly £12,700 after three years. So the true cost of picking the wrong account is closer to £1,450 on our arithmetic: the £750 of capital lost plus the interest never earned.

5. Using it for retirement: pension first, nearly always

The LISA's second purpose is saving to 60, and the 25% bonus is arithmetically identical to basic rate tax relief on a pension: £80 becomes £100 in both. Withdrawals from a LISA after 60 are entirely tax free, while only 25% of a pension can usually be taken tax free and the rest is taxed as income. On that comparison alone the LISA looks competitive for a basic rate taxpayer.

What the comparison leaves out is the employer. Under automatic enrolment an employer must contribute at least 3% of qualifying earnings when the employee pays in, which we explained in our salary sacrifice guide. Money paid into a LISA instead of a workplace pension forfeits that employer contribution entirely. A higher rate taxpayer also gets 40% relief on pension contributions against the LISA's flat 25%.

So the retirement case is narrow: a basic rate taxpayer already taking every pound of employer matching, who expects to stay basic rate in retirement. That is a real group, but a small one, and for most readers the pension comes first.

6. The replacement product, and what to do while you wait

On 23 June 2026 HM Treasury opened a consultation on a First Time Buyer ISA, which will be offered in place of the Lifetime ISA. The consultation ran to 18 August 2026 and the outcome had not been published when we checked. The proposed design pays the government bonus at the point of the home purchase rather than monthly, which removes the need for any withdrawal charge: if your circumstances change you take your own money out without penalty. It would be open to anyone over 18 with no upper age limit, and available as cash or stocks and shares. It would only be for a first home, so the retirement use disappears.

Three things the consultation says about existing LISA holders matter. You will not be able to transfer a LISA into the new product, because you have already received a bonus. You will be able to use funds from both an existing LISA and a new FTB ISA for the same purchase. And you will be able to hold both, but only pay into one in the same tax year. The property price cap for the two products will be aligned.

No launch date is in the consultation document. MoneySavingExpert's Martin Lewis guessed at April 2027 at the earliest; treat that as a guess, not a date. For a decision today: if you are under 40 and expect to buy a first home under £450,000 within a few years, opening a LISA now still earns the 25% bonus on this year's £4,000, and nothing announced takes that away. If you are wavering, the no-penalty design is worth waiting for, and the honest cost of waiting is one or two years of bonus.

7. The honest verdict

Open one if you are under 40, will buy a first home under £450,000, and can leave the money alone for at least 12 months. The 25% bonus is the best guaranteed return on offer to a UK saver and it is tax free.

Do not open one as a general savings account. The 25% withdrawal charge takes 6.25% of your own money, and the Treasury's own data says more people have paid that charge than have bought a house with the account.

For retirement, fill the workplace pension first. The employer contribution beats the LISA bonus for almost everyone. If you already have a LISA, keep it: the replacement product does not touch it.

Frequently asked

Can I have a Lifetime ISA and a normal ISA in the same year? Yes. The £4,000 LISA limit sits inside your £20,000 overall ISA allowance, so you can put the other £16,000 into cash or stocks and shares ISAs in the same tax year.

I opened a LISA years ago and now the house I want is over £450,000. What happens? You cannot use the LISA for that purchase without the 25% charge. Your options are to pay the charge, to leave the money until 60, or to wait and see whether the new First Time Buyer ISA's cap is set differently. The consultation says the caps will be aligned, so do not count on a higher one.

Is the Lifetime ISA closing? Not yet. The government has said it will be replaced by a First Time Buyer ISA, but no date has been set, and existing accounts will continue to accept payments and earn the bonus after the new product launches.

Can my partner and I both use a LISA on one house? Yes, if you are both first time buyers and both meet the conditions. You can each use your savings and bonus towards the same property.
