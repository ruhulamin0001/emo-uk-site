The Jobs Data Says Two Opposite Things This Month. Here Is Which One Applies to You

The ONS says vacancies have fallen to a level last seen in 2014. KPMG and the REC say permanent hiring stopped shrinking for the first time in 45 months. Both statements are true, they were published eight days apart, and they are measuring completely different things. The one that affects your next job move is not the one that got the headlines.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Vacancies are 707,000 for May to July 2026, down 6,000 on the previous quarter. Outside the pandemic, the last time the figure was that low was September to November 2014. That is the ONS release of 18 August 2026.
  • They have also barely moved all year. Vacancies are down just 11,000 since January to March 2026, which is 1.5%. The fall already happened. What we have now is a plateau, not a collapse in progress.
  • Permanent hiring stopped falling in July for the first time in 45 months, according to KPMG and the REC on 10 August 2026. Temporary vacancies rose for the first time in two years.
  • Private sector regular pay is growing 2.8% a year. CPI in the year to July 2026 was 2.9%. On those two numbers the average private sector worker is now going backwards.
  • Public sector regular pay is growing 6.1%. That 3.3 percentage point gap is doing all the work in the "real pay is still rising" headline.
  • There is nothing to buy here. This is a read of published statistics, not a product.

1. Why the two headlines contradict each other

On 18 August 2026 the ONS published its monthly labour market overview. The line every outlet ran was that vacancies had fallen again, to 707,000 for May to July 2026, down 6,000 on the February to April quarter. The ONS notes that outside the coronavirus period, the last time there were 707,000 or fewer vacancies was September to November 2014, when there were 703,000.

Eight days earlier, KPMG and the Recruitment and Employment Confederation published their monthly Report on Jobs, compiled by S&P Global from about 400 UK recruitment consultancies. Their finding was that permanent placements stabilised in July, ending a 45 month decline, the first month without a fall since 2022.

Both are accurate. They contradict each other only if you assume they measure the same thing, and they do not.

A vacancy count is a stock. A placement count is a flow. The stock is the water in the bath. The flow is the tap.

Vacancies are the number of open roles sitting there on a given day. Placements are the number of people actually being hired in a month. You can empty a bath for three years and then turn the tap back on, and for a while the bath is still nearly empty while the tap is running. That is roughly where the UK labour market is in August 2026.

If you already have a job and you are wondering whether your employer is about to cut headcount, the stock is the number you want. If you are looking for work, the flow is the number that decides whether anyone calls you back. Only one of those got the coverage.

The rest of the ONS release leans the same way. Unemployment for April to June 2026 was 4.9%, down 0.1 percentage points on the quarter. The employment rate for 16 to 64 year olds was 75.1%, up 0.1 on the quarter. The Claimant Count for July fell on both the month and the year, to 1.665 million. Payrolled employees stood at 30.3 million, down 94,000 on the year but essentially flat on the month.

One derived figure worth sitting with. Divide the Claimant Count by the vacancy count and you get roughly 2.4 claimants for every open role. That is our arithmetic on two published ONS numbers, and it is not the same as unemployed people per vacancy, because the two are defined differently. But it explains why "the market has stopped getting worse" and "I have sent 80 applications" are both true at once.

2. The pay figure almost nobody split in two

The pay headline from the same release was that regular pay grew 3.5% in the year to April to June 2026, and that after inflation this was real growth of 0.7% on the CPI measure. That reads as mildly good news.

The ONS publishes the split, and the split is the story:

Public sector regular pay growth: 6.1%.

Private sector regular pay growth: 2.8%.

CPI inflation in the year to July 2026: 2.9% (up from 2.6% in June). CPIH was 3.1%.

Most UK employees work in the private sector, and for them the average pay rise is now running below the most recent inflation reading. The 0.7% real growth in the headline is an average across both sectors, held above zero by the public sector figure. The ONS is explicit that public sector pay growth this year is affected by the timing of pay awards, so the gap may narrow. It has not narrowed yet.

Two honest caveats. The pay figures cover April to June and the 2.9% CPI figure is for July, so the periods are not perfectly aligned. And a sector average is not a forecast of your pay rise. It includes people who got nothing and people who got 15%.

3. Worked example: Amara in Luton, on £34,000

Amara works in logistics administration for a private employer in Bedfordshire. She earns £34,000 and pays English income tax rates. Suppose her employer awards exactly the private sector average.

The rise. £34,000 × 2.8% = £952, taking her to £34,952.

What she needed to stand still. £34,000 × 2.9% = £34,986. She is £34 short before tax.

What she actually keeps. The rise sits above the £12,570 personal allowance and below the £50,270 upper earnings limit, so it is taxed at 20% and carries 8% employee National Insurance. She keeps 72p in the pound: £685.44 a year, or £57.12 a month.

If she has a Plan 2 student loan (threshold £29,385, deducted at 9%), the marginal rate becomes 37% and she keeps £599.76 a year, or £49.98 a month.

Now the same salary on the public sector average of 6.1%. That is £2,074, taking a counterpart to £36,074. The cash gap after one year, on identical starting salaries, is £1,122.

All of the 2026/27 tax figures above come from HMRC's rates and thresholds guidance. The personal allowance and the £50,270 threshold are frozen, which is why a pay rise that only matches inflation still quietly increases the share of your income that is taxed.

4. Who the good news is actually for

The recruiters' report is granular, and the detail matters more than the headline because the recovery is not evenly spread.

  • Temporary and contract work turned first. Temp vacancies rose in July for the first time in two years, at the quickest rate since August 2023, and temp billings have now risen four months running. Blue collar and engineering led.
  • London and the Midlands are hiring permanently again. London recorded a near four year high in permanent staff hiring. The South and the North of England both recorded further declines in the same month.
  • Nursing, medical and care saw the steepest growth in demand for permanent staff. Retail and hotel and catering saw the sharpest falls in vacancies.
  • Pay for movers is improving. Starting salary inflation hit a six month high and temp wage growth a 26 month high. The recruiters attribute this to a shortage of suitably skilled candidates, not to generosity.
  • Candidate availability is still rising sharply, and has been for nearly three and a half years, driven by redundancies. There are more of you chasing each role than before, even as demand improves.

So here is the part worth saying plainly. If you work in retail or hospitality in the North or the South of England, none of the good news in this month's data is about you. Your sector had the sharpest vacancy falls and your region is still contracting. That is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to treat "the jobs market is recovering" headlines as somebody else's news and plan accordingly.

The honest verdict

The UK labour market in August 2026 is not collapsing and it is not recovering. It is flat at a low level, with the first genuine signs of a turn showing up in the fastest moving indicators. Vacancies down 11,000 in seven months is a plateau, and a 45 month decline in permanent placements ending is a real inflection, even if it is only one month of data.

The pay picture is worse than the headline suggests, and that is the thing most coverage missed. If you are in the private sector, the average pay award is currently behind the most recent inflation figure. Asking for more than the average is not greedy this year, it is the only way to stand still.

Nothing here requires a decision today. The next ONS labour market release is 15 September 2026, and one month of stabilising placements is not a trend. If you were going to move jobs anyway, the data is mildly more encouraging than it was in the spring, particularly for temporary and contract work. If you were not, there is no urgency in any of this.

Frequently asked

Is 707,000 vacancies really the worst since 2014? Outside the pandemic period, yes, on the ONS Vacancy Survey. The ONS names September to November 2014 as the last comparable point, at 703,000. It is worth remembering that the workforce is larger now than in 2014, so the same vacancy count represents fewer openings per worker.

Why are the two surveys allowed to disagree? They are different instruments. The ONS Vacancy Survey counts open positions at businesses. The KPMG and REC report is a diffusion index built from about 400 recruitment consultancies, where 50 means no change on the previous month. A diffusion index tells you direction of travel, not level. The ONS count tells you level, not momentum.

Does the ONS say why small firms are not recruiting? It reports feedback from its own Vacancy Survey that some small firms may not be recruiting because of increases in labour costs and other operating expenses. That is the ONS quoting its respondents, not an ONS judgement on policy.

Should I ask for a pay rise now or wait? If your last review was more than a year ago, waiting has a measurable cost, because inflation is running slightly ahead of the average private sector award. The recruiters' data showing starting salaries at a six month high is the useful lever: what an employer would have to pay to replace you has gone up.
