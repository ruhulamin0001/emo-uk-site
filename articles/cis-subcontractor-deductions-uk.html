Ten Points of Difference, Free to Fix, and Most People Never Do

The gap between a registered and an unregistered CIS subcontractor is ten percentage points of every payment, and registering costs nothing. The second habit in this guide is worth as much again, and it is just a line on an invoice.

The short version

  • Registered subcontractors have 20% deducted. Unregistered subcontractors have 30%. Registering is free and takes minutes, so the 10 point gap is the most avoidable money loss in construction.
  • On £40,000 of labour in a year that gap is £4,000 of your cash flow, held by HMRC until you file.
  • The deduction is not a tax. It is an advance payment against your tax and National Insurance bill, and anything overpaid comes back after your Self Assessment.
  • Deductions come off the labour element only. Materials, plant hire you paid for, and VAT are excluded, so how you itemise your invoice changes what is deducted.
  • Gross payment status removes deductions entirely and is the third option almost nobody on 20% has looked at.

1. Twenty, thirty or nothing

Under the Construction Industry Scheme, a contractor takes money from what they pay you and sends it to HMRC. GOV.UK sets out three positions.

  • Registered, net payment status: 20%. This is the standard position for a registered subcontractor, also called payment under deduction.
  • Not registered: 30%. Contractors must deduct at the higher rate from anyone they cannot verify.
  • Gross payment status: 0%. You are paid in full and settle everything through Self Assessment or corporation tax.

Registration is free. Subcontractors are not obliged to register, but the only consequence of not registering is the extra 10 points. There is no upside to it.

2. The part that decides the size of the deduction

This is where real money is won and lost, and it is nothing to do with the rate.

CIS deductions are taken from the labour element of a payment only. The contractor takes off the cost of materials you supplied, plant you hired, and VAT, and applies the percentage to what is left.

That means an invoice that says “supply and fit, £4,000” can have 20% taken from the whole £4,000, which is £800. The same job invoiced as £2,500 labour and £1,500 materials has 20% taken from £2,500, which is £500. Same job, £300 difference in the cash you take home that month.

Nothing is avoided by doing this. The final tax bill is identical, because materials were never taxable income in the first place. What changes is how much of your own money HMRC holds for up to a year.

Itemise labour and materials separately on every invoice. It changes nothing you owe and a great deal about when you have it.

3. The comparison, on £40,000 of labour a year

Take a self-employed subcontractor invoicing £40,000 of labour plus £15,000 of materials across a tax year, so £55,000 invoiced in total. Their actual tax bill depends on profit after expenses, so assume profit of £30,000 after £10,000 of allowable costs. On 2026/27 rates, income tax on that is £3,486 and Class 4 National Insurance is £1,046, giving a genuine liability of £4,532.

Look at the last column rather than the deduction column, because the refunds are the point. Every row ends with the same £4,532 of tax. What differs is how much of your money was sitting with HMRC in the meantime, and for how long.

The worst row, unregistered and not itemising, has £16,500 taken during the year against a real bill of £4,532. That is £11,968 of your own cash locked up for up to eighteen months depending on when in the year you earned it. For a subcontractor buying materials and running a van, that is the difference between managing and not.

The best row for cash flow, gross payment status, hands you everything and leaves you to pay £4,532 by 31 January. That is superb if you set money aside and dangerous if you do not.

4. Gross payment status, the option nobody checks

Gross payment status means contractors pay you in full with nothing deducted. You apply to HMRC and, if accepted, you are reviewed annually. The tests are broadly about your business being genuine, your turnover being above a threshold, and your tax affairs being up to date and filed on time.

It is worth applying if your cash flow is tight and your filing is reliable. It is a bad idea if you struggle to put money aside, because the entire year’s tax then arrives as a single bill with no credit already paid against it.

There is a second, quieter reason to look at it. Chasing a refund every spring means your working capital is decided by how quickly HMRC processes your return, which is outside your control. Gross payment status hands that control back. The price of taking it is that the discipline has to come from you instead, and a business that has never managed to hold a tax reserve rarely starts doing it the month the deductions stop.

Worked example: Tomasz in Doncaster

Tomasz is a self-employed plasterer. He invoiced £40,000 of labour and £15,000 of materials last year and was not registered for CIS, so contractors deducted 30%. He also invoiced as a single figure per job.

What was taken. 30% of the full £55,000, which is £16,500.

What he actually owed. After £10,000 of allowable expenses his profit was £30,000, so £3,486 income tax and £1,046 Class 4 National Insurance, a total of £4,532.

The refund. £11,968, which arrived in the spring, nine months after he had earned much of it.

The two changes he made. He registered for CIS, moving to 20%, and he started splitting labour and materials on every invoice. On the same work this year, deductions fall from £16,500 to £8,000.

What that is worth. Not a penny in tax, because he was always getting the difference back. What it is worth is £8,500 of cash in his own account during the year instead of HMRC’s, which is roughly £708 a month. He stopped using a credit card to buy materials, which had been costing him real interest.

5. Frequently asked

Is CIS a separate tax? No. It is a collection mechanism. The deductions count as advance payments towards your income tax and National Insurance.

Who has to register as a contractor? Anyone who pays subcontractors for construction work, and also any business that does not do construction itself but has spent more than £3 million on construction in the 12 months since its first payment.

What work is outside the scheme? Architecture and surveying, scaffolding hire with no labour, carpet fitting, making materials, delivering materials, and work on a site that is clearly not construction such as running a canteen.

How do I prove what was deducted? Contractors must give you a payment and deduction statement. Keep every one, because your refund depends on them.

What if I work through a limited company? Deductions are set against the company’s PAYE and National Insurance liabilities through the payroll rather than through your personal Self Assessment.

6. The honest verdict

If you are an unregistered subcontractor, register today. It is free, it takes minutes, and it moves you from 30% to 20% with no downside at all. On £40,000 of labour that is £4,000 of cash flow.

Then split labour and materials on every invoice. Deductions apply to the labour element only, so an itemised invoice keeps money that a lump sum hands to HMRC for a year. On the numbers above that is another £3,000 or £4,500.

Look at gross payment status only if your filing is genuinely reliable and you are disciplined about setting tax aside. It is the best option for cash flow and the worst one to get wrong.
