The Heat Pump Grant Is £7,500, Or £9,000 Off The Gas Grid. Whether You Save A Penny Turns On One Number Nobody Quotes

Every heat pump quote you will be sent leads with the grant. Almost none of them lead with the seasonal performance factor, which is the only number that decides whether your running costs fall, stay flat, or quietly go up. At the capped rates that start on 1 October 2026, the break even point is an SPF of 2.81. The largest UK field trial measured an average of 2.8.

The short version

  • The grant amounts, confirmed by Ofgem: £7,500 off an air-to-water, ground source or water source heat pump. £9,000 if the property is heated by oil or LPG and has no mains gas connection, available until 31 March 2027. £5,000 for a biomass boiler. £2,500 for air-to-air, a category only added on 28 April 2026.
  • Break even is an SPF of 2.81. At the October 2026 capped rates, electricity costs 3.30 times what gas does per kWh. A condensing gas boiler realistically delivers about 85% of the fuel you buy as heat. Multiply and you get 2.81. Below that your heat pump costs more to run than the boiler it replaced.
  • The government's own field trial averaged 2.8. The Electrification of Heat Demonstration Project monitored 427 UK air source installations and recorded a mean seasonal performance factor of 2.8. That is a coin flip, not a saving.
  • Good installations do much better. Open source monitoring of 169 well optimised UK systems found an average of 3.86. The gap between 2.8 and 3.86 is worth roughly £200 a year on a three bedroom semi. The gap is the installer, not the machine.
  • Cash cost after the grant. A typical air source install runs about £13,500 before the grant, so roughly £6,000 after it. A straight combi boiler swap has a median quote of about £2,300. The real decision is whether the extra £3,700 is worth it.
  • The one date to diarise is 31 March 2027. That is when both the £9,000 uplift and the zero rate of VAT on electricity are due to end. If VAT returns at 5%, break even moves from an SPF of 2.81 up to 2.95, and every heat pump in Britain gets worse overnight without anything changing physically.
  • Update, 5 September 2026: the Energy Saving Trust has now run 1.1 million heat pump, battery and tariff combinations across 546 monitored homes. Its finding is that the tariff decides the outcome: a heat pump on a standard flat rate costs the same or more to run than an efficient gas boiler, a dedicated heat pump or dynamic tariff saves up to £330 a year, and on its own arithmetic (£11,700 average install, less £7,500 grant, less the £3,700 boiler you no longer buy) the extra £500 pays back in two to four years. 1. What the Boiler Upgrade Scheme actually pays in 2026

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme covers England and Wales, is administered by Ofgem, and has been extended to 2030. Version 5 of the property owner guidance came into force on 28 April 2026 and changed three things that matter.

First, the grant levels as they stand today:

  • £7,500 off an air-to-water heat pump, a ground source heat pump or a water source heat pump.
  • £9,000 off an air source or ground source heat pump where GOV.UK's two conditions are both met: the property is heated by oil or LPG, and there is no mains gas connection at the property. That is the standard £7,500 plus a £1,500 uplift, and it runs until 31 March 2027. It exists specifically to move rural households off heating oil.
  • £5,000 off a biomass boiler, rural off gas grid properties only.
  • £2,500 off an air-to-air heat pump, residential properties only. This category is new as of 28 April 2026 and is the one to be most careful with, for reasons in section 7.

Second, the mandatory EPC requirement was removed in April 2026. You no longer need a valid Energy Performance Certificate with no outstanding loft or cavity wall insulation recommendations. Where there is no EPC, your installer supplies alternative evidence such as proof of your current heating fuel. This removed the single biggest reason applications used to fail, and it is a genuine improvement.

Third, and this has always been true but is worth stating plainly: you never touch the money. An MCS certified installer applies on your behalf and the grant appears as an upfront discount on your quote. If a company asks you to pay the full amount and claim the grant back yourself, that is not how the scheme works and you should walk away.

Northern Ireland is not covered. Scotland runs its own separate scheme through Home Energy Scotland.

2. SPF: the number every quote leaves out

A gas boiler turns roughly 85 pence of every pound of gas into heat in your rooms. It cannot exceed 100%, and in practice a condensing boiler with return water temperatures that are too high often does worse than its badge suggests.

A heat pump does not burn anything. It moves heat from outside air into your house, and it can therefore deliver more heat energy than the electrical energy it consumes. How much more is measured two ways, and the difference between them is where most of the disappointment in this market lives.

  • COP, coefficient of performance, is a snapshot at one set of conditions. Manufacturers quote 4.0 to 5.0. These are laboratory numbers at mild outdoor temperatures and low flow temperatures.
  • SCOP, seasonal COP, averages across a standardised heating season. Better, but still a test standard.
  • SPF, seasonal performance factor, is what your actual installed system actually achieved in your actual house over a real year, measured at the meter. This is the only one that spends your money.

The machine is rarely the problem. The flow temperature, the radiator sizing and the commissioning are the problem.

The evidence on real UK performance is unusually good, because the government paid for it. The Electrification of Heat Demonstration Project monitored 427 air source heat pump installations across Britain and recorded a mean SPF of 2.8. Separately, published open source monitoring of more than 169 well optimised real world systems found an average annual SPF of 3.86, around 38% higher than the trial mean.

Those two figures describe the same hardware installed with different levels of care. That is the whole story of this technology in Britain, and it is why "which heat pump should I buy" is close to the wrong question.

3. The break even calculation, done properly

Here is the arithmetic in full, using Ofgem's published capped rates for 1 October to 31 December 2026, direct debit, the England, Scotland and Wales average.

Electricity: 26.32p per kWh, standing charge 54.83p a day. No VAT, because domestic electricity is zero rated from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027.

Gas: 7.97p per kWh, standing charge 29.68p a day. Includes 5% VAT.

Price ratio: 26.32 ÷ 7.97 = 3.30. Electricity costs 3.3 times as much per unit as gas.

Break even SPF: 3.30 × 0.85 (boiler efficiency) = 2.81.

Read that last line slowly, because it is the point of this entire guide. A heat pump achieving an SPF of exactly 2.81 costs precisely the same to run as the gas boiler it replaced. Not cheaper. The same.

The field trial average was 2.8.

There is one genuine adjustment in the heat pump's favour that this ratio misses. If you remove your gas connection entirely, you stop paying the gas standing charge, which at 29.68p a day is £108.33 a year whether you burn a single unit or not. That is a real, guaranteed saving that arrives regardless of how well the heat pump performs, and it is the reason the worked example below still shows a small saving at an SPF of 2.8.

It is also, at an average performance level, essentially the entire saving.

4. Cost per kWh of heat, four systems compared

The fairest way to compare heating systems is cost per kWh of heat delivered into the room, not cost per kWh of fuel bought. Fuel prices are the October 2026 capped rates for gas and electricity, and the BoilerJuice UK average kerosene price of 92.93p per litre including VAT as at 25 August 2026, at 10.35 kWh per litre.

SystemFuel priceEfficiencyCost per kWh of heat
Gas condensing boiler7.97p per kWh85%9.38p
Oil boiler, kerosene8.98p per kWh85%10.56p
Heat pump, field trial average26.32p per kWhSPF 2.89.40p
Heat pump, well optimised26.32p per kWhSPF 3.866.82p

Three conclusions fall straight out of that table.

Against mains gas, an average heat pump is a dead heat. 9.40p against 9.38p is a rounding error. Anyone quoting you large running cost savings on a gas home is either assuming an SPF well above the national average, or has not done this sum.

Against heating oil, an average heat pump already wins, by about 11%, and the grant on offer is £9,000 rather than £7,500. This is a materially better proposition and it is not close.

A well commissioned heat pump beats everything, by 27% against gas. That saving is real and it is achievable. It is simply not automatic, and nothing about the grant guarantees you get it.

5. Worked example: the Hussain household in Stevenage

A 1960s three bedroom semi on the gas grid with cavity wall insulation and 250mm of loft insulation. Two adults, two children. Their bills show 12,000 kWh of gas and 3,100 kWh of electricity over the last twelve months. Their 14 year old combi is working but noisy, and they have been quoted £2,300 to replace it.

At 85% efficiency, those 12,000 kWh of gas deliver 10,200 kWh of useful heat into the house. That is the number a heat pump has to reproduce.

Option A, replace the combi boiler.
Gas: 12,000 × 7.97p = £956.40. Gas standing charge: 365 × 29.68p = £108.33.
Electricity: 3,100 × 26.32p = £815.92. Electricity standing charge: 365 × 54.83p = £200.13.
Annual running cost: £2,080.78. Capital cost: £2,300.

Option B, heat pump at the field trial average, SPF 2.8.
Heat required 10,200 ÷ 2.8 = 3,643 kWh of electricity for heating.
Total electricity: 3,100 + 3,643 = 6,743 kWh × 26.32p = £1,774.76, plus £200.13 standing charge.
Gas connection removed, so no gas bill and no gas standing charge.
Annual running cost: £1,974.85. Saving: £105.93 a year.

Option C, heat pump well commissioned, SPF 3.5.
10,200 ÷ 3.5 = 2,914 kWh. Total electricity 6,014 kWh × 26.32p = £1,582.88, plus £200.13.
Annual running cost: £1,783.09. Saving: £297.69 a year.

Option D, heat pump badly commissioned, SPF 2.4.
10,200 ÷ 2.4 = 4,250 kWh. Total electricity 7,350 kWh × 26.32p = £1,934.52, plus £200.13.
Annual running cost: £2,134.65. That is £53.87 a year MORE than the gas boiler.

Now the capital side. A typical air source install for a house this size is around £13,500 before the grant, so £6,000 after the £7,500 discount. But the Hussains were going to spend £2,300 on a boiler anyway. The genuine incremental cost of choosing a heat pump is therefore £3,700.

Payback on that £3,700:

  • At the field trial average SPF of 2.8, saving £105.93 a year: 34.9 years. The unit will not last that long.
  • At a well commissioned SPF of 3.5, saving £297.69 a year: 12.4 years. That is inside the lifespan and is a defensible decision.
  • At SPF 2.4: there is no payback. It never breaks even and costs more every year.

The spread between those three outcomes is not the machine. It is the flow temperature the system is designed around, whether the radiators were upsized, and whether the installer bothered to commission it properly. The grant is identical in all three cases.

6. Why the off gas grid case is completely different

Everything above assumes mains gas, which is the hardest case for a heat pump to win. If you heat with oil, the sums change in three directions at once and all three favour the heat pump.

The grant is 20% larger. £9,000 rather than £7,500, available on air source and ground source in eligible off gas grid properties from 21 July 2026 until 31 March 2027.

The fuel you are replacing is dearer. Kerosene at 92.93p a litre works out at 10.56p per kWh of delivered heat, against 9.40p for an average heat pump. An average installation already saves you money before you count anything else.

You have no gas standing charge to keep paying. Oil households already pay only the electricity standing charge, so the comparison is cleaner.

Run the same 10,200 kWh of heat demand through those numbers and an oil household at the trial average SPF of 2.8 saves about £119 a year on fuel, rising to roughly £382 at an SPF of 3.86. Against a net cost that is £1,500 lower because of the uplift. That is a completely different investment case from the mains gas one, and it is why the government targeted the extra money exactly there.

One honest caveat: heating oil is volatile. It averaged 82.4p a litre in July 2026 and rose to roughly 89p across August, driven by Middle East supply disruption. A calm year could push it back down and narrow the gap. Nobody, including me, knows where it goes next.

7. Myths that cost people money

Myth one: "The COP is 4.5, so it will use a quarter of the energy."
The 4.5 on the brochure is a laboratory figure at mild ambient temperatures and a low flow temperature. The measured UK field average is 2.8, which is 38% worse. On the Hussain household that difference is the gap between saving £106 a year and saving £450 a year. Ask for the design SPF at your intended flow temperature, in writing, not the headline COP.

Myth two: "The £2,500 air-to-air grant is a cheap way in."
Air-to-air units blow warm air and, in most British homes, do not heat your water at all. Hot water is typically 15% to 20% of a household's total heat demand, so on the Hussains' 10,200 kWh that is roughly 1,800 kWh a year still needing a source. If that falls to an immersion heater at 26.32p per kWh with no heat pump multiplier, you are paying about £474 a year for water that the gas boiler heated for around £169. The grant is smaller and the trap is bigger.

Myth three: "The grant covers most of the cost, so it is nearly free."
£7,500 against a typical £13,500 install leaves about £6,000 to find. More to the point, the honest comparison is not £6,000 against zero, it is £6,000 against the £2,300 you would have spent on a boiler anyway. The real number you are deciding about is £3,700, and if a salesperson only ever says "£7,500 off", they are steering you away from the arithmetic that matters.

8. What to actually ask an installer

Get three MCS certified quotes. That is not a formality, because the spread in this market is enormous and MCS certification is a condition of the grant. Then ask these five things and put the answers in writing.

  1. What flow temperature is the system designed around? Anything at or below 45C is a good sign. 55C or above means the installer has designed around your existing radiators to keep the job cheap, and your SPF will suffer for the life of the system.
  2. What is the predicted SPF at that flow temperature, in this house? If the answer is a brochure COP, that tells you what you need to know about the quote.
  3. Which radiators are being replaced and why? Upsizing radiators is usually what buys you the difference between 2.8 and 3.5. A quote with no radiator work on an older house is a quote that has quietly chosen the cheap option.
  4. Is a heat loss survey included, and can I see it? A properly sized system is usually smaller than people expect. Oversizing causes short cycling, which wrecks efficiency.
  5. What is the grant value shown as, and is it deducted before I pay? It must appear as an upfront discount on the quote.

Before any of that, spend the small money. Draught proofing, loft insulation topped up to 270mm and a hot water cylinder jacket reduce the heat demand your heat pump has to meet, which makes a smaller and cheaper unit viable and improves the SPF at the same time. Doing this after installation is doing it in the wrong order.

9. What the Energy Saving Trust's million-combination study changes

On 12 August 2026 the Energy Saving Trust published the results of a study that does at scale what section 3 of this guide does by hand. Its experts took measured performance data from 546 real homes, combined heat pumps, solar panels and batteries on their own and together, ran each combination against standard, specialist and export tariffs, and worked through around 1.1 million combinations using a full year of data. Read again on 5 September 2026, the page carries four figures that bear directly on the decision above.

First, the tariff, not the machine, decides whether you save. The Trust found that most heat pumps on a standard flat-rate tariff cost the same or even more to run than an efficient gas boiler, which is exactly what the SPF 2.81 break-even in section 3 predicts at the national field average of 2.8. Move the same heat pump onto what the Trust calls a heat pump appropriate tariff, meaning a dedicated heat pump tariff or a dynamic tariff whose price changes every half hour, and the saving against gas is up to £330 a year. That figure is the difference between the standard-rate case in the Hussain example, which saved the gas standing charge and nothing else, and the good-installation case.

Second, the Trust's own payback arithmetic. It takes an average heat pump cost of around £11,700, subtracts the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant, and then subtracts the around £3,700 a replacement gas boiler would have cost when the old one eventually failed, on the reasoning that a heat pump owner never buys that boiler. That leaves an additional cost of £500, and at up to £330 a year on the right tariff the Trust puts the payback at two to four years. The logic is sound and the numbers are close to ours (section 3 used £13,500 and £2,300, which gives a £3,700 gap rather than £500, because we priced a straight combi swap rather than an average boiler replacement). The difference between a two year payback and a twelve year one is entirely which boiler you compare against and which tariff you sit on, and both are choices the reader makes, not facts about heat pumps. Our boiler replacement article has the boiler side of that comparison by house type.

Third, the gas standing charge. A home that moves both heating and cooking off gas can have the gas meter removed and stop paying the standing charge altogether, which the Trust values at around £105 a year at July 2026 prices. That is on top of the £330, and it is what turns a marginal case into a clear one for the roughly half of homes that still cook on gas and are willing to change the hob.

Fourth, batteries pair better with heat pumps than with solar. The Trust's most surprising finding is that a home battery charged from the grid overnight on a cheap window and then discharged into the heat pump during the day beats the same battery paired with solar panels, because export tariffs are now good enough that selling surplus solar earns almost as much as storing it. A heat pump, solar and battery together on the right tariffs cut bills by around £800 a year in the general case and £1,000 or more in the best, with heating bills down almost 80 per cent. Our solar payback article covers the panels on their own; the point here is that the heat pump is the piece that makes the battery pay.

The Trust's own caveats apply and are worth repeating: every saving is scenario-based, outcomes depend on the home, the installation and the tariff, and even the best tariff cannot rescue a badly performing heat pump. The Trust is also candid that there is no easy way in the UK to find and compare these tariffs, and has published a guide to which tariff types suit which technologies. The grant figures were re-checked on GOV.UK on 5 September 2026: £7,500 for air source and ground source, plus £1,500 until March 2027 for oil or LPG heated homes with no mains gas connection, £5,000 biomass, £2,500 air-to-air. Nothing there has moved since this guide was written.

The honest verdict

Updated 5 September 2026. The Energy Saving Trust's million-combination study confirms the shape of this verdict and sharpens one part of it: on a standard flat-rate tariff a heat pump mostly does not beat a gas boiler, and on a dedicated heat pump or dynamic tariff it saves up to £330 a year and pays back its £500 extra cost in two to four years. If you install one, the tariff switch is not optional. Section 9 has the figures.

This clearly helps you if you are off the gas grid on heating oil. £9,000 until 31 March 2027, a fuel that already costs more per kWh of heat than an average heat pump, and no gas standing charge in the comparison. If that is your house, get three MCS quotes this autumn rather than next spring, because the uplift has an end date and installer diaries fill up before winter.

It probably helps you on mains gas if, and only if, you are willing to buy a good installation rather than a cheap one. At an SPF of 3.5 the Hussain household saves £298 a year and pays back the £3,700 difference in 12.4 years. At the national field average of 2.8 the saving is £106, which is the gas standing charge and nothing more, and payback runs past the life of the equipment.

It does not help you if your boiler still has years in it and your radiators are small and original. Replacing a working boiler early to chase a grant means you have paid £6,000 to avoid a £2,300 bill you were not facing yet. Spend a few hundred pounds on insulation and draught proofing instead and revisit this when the boiler actually fails.

Two dates are worth writing down. 31 March 2027 ends the £9,000 uplift, and it is also when the zero rate of VAT on electricity is currently due to end. If 5% VAT returns, the break even SPF moves from 2.81 to 2.95, and every heat pump in the country gets slightly worse without a single component changing. That is a policy risk sitting inside a twenty year decision, and it deserves to be said out loud rather than left in a footnote.

Frequently asked

Do I still need an EPC to get the grant? No. The mandatory EPC requirement was removed in April 2026 under version 5 of the Ofgem guidance, in force 28 April 2026. Where there is no EPC, your installer provides alternative evidence such as proof of your current heating fuel.

Can I apply myself? No, and you should not want to. Your MCS certified installer applies on your behalf and the grant is deducted from your invoice. You never handle the money.

Does the grant cover Scotland or Northern Ireland? No. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme is England and Wales. Scotland has its own scheme via Home Energy Scotland with different amounts and rules. Northern Ireland is separate again.

How long does the £9,000 last? It is available from 21 July 2026 to 31 March 2027 for eligible off gas grid properties. The standard £7,500 continues, and the scheme as a whole has been extended to 2030.

Will my house be cold? Not if the system is sized and commissioned properly. A heat pump runs at a lower flow temperature for longer periods rather than short hot blasts, so the radiators feel cooler to the touch while the room reaches the same temperature. If a quote does not include a heat loss survey, that is the risk you are taking.

What about solar panels and a battery? They change the sums by lowering your effective electricity price, which is the single biggest lever in the break even calculation. That is a large enough subject to deserve its own guide rather than a paragraph here, and the honest answer is that it depends heavily on your roof and your usage pattern.
