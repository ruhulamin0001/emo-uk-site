A Heated Airer Costs 20p A Load. A Condenser Dryer Costs 92p. Here Is The Whole Sum.

Every winter the same argument: the tumble dryer is expensive, the heated airer is slow, and nobody has the numbers. We took the wattage from Lakeland's own instruction manual, the annual energy figures from Hotpoint's own spec pages, and the unit rate from Ofgem's October 2026 cap, and did the sum per load. The airer wins on cost, but not by the margin the adverts suggest against a heat pump dryer, and there is one household where it loses.

The short version

  • Electricity is 26.32p per kWh from 1 October 2026 under the Ofgem cap for Direct Debit customers. Every figure here uses that rate.
  • A 300 W heated airer running five hours dries two loads for about 39p, so roughly 20p a load. The wattage is from Lakeland's own manual for the Dry:Soon 3-tier.
  • A condenser dryer uses about 3.5 kWh a full load, about 92p. That is Hotpoint's weighted annual figure of 561.1 kWh divided by the 160 cycles the label assumes.
  • A heat pump dryer uses about 1.66 kWh a load, about 44p. Hotpoint's figure for its 8 kg heat pump model is 265 kWh a year.
  • At 160 loads a year the airer saves about £116 against a condenser and about £38 against a heat pump dryer. A £140 airer pays back in about 14 months against the first and about 3.7 years against the second.
  • An unheated clothes horse costs nothing to run and beats all three, provided you can deal with the moisture it puts into the room.

Two notes on those. The airer wattage is a plate rating, so it draws 300 W whenever it is switched on and warm, and its running cost is simply hours multiplied by 0.3 kWh. The dryer figures are the opposite: they are energy per cycle averaged over the standard cotton programme at full and partial loads, so they already reflect a real mix of loads rather than a flat-out draw. Dividing 561.1 by 160 gives 3.5 kWh per average load for the condenser, and 265 by 160 gives 1.66 kWh for the heat pump. Those two numbers are the whole argument.

We are not quoting purchase prices for any of these. They change weekly and a stale price on a money page is worse than none. Check the current price at the link, and read the rating plate or the energy label before you buy, because that is where the figure that decides your running cost actually lives.

2. Cost per load: airer, condenser, heat pump, clothes horse

The airer needs one assumption the dryers do not: how long you leave it on. Lakeland's 3-tier holds about two wash loads. With the cover on, in a normal room, five hours will dry a mixed load of cottons; without the cover, or with heavy towels and jeans, it can be eight. We will run both.

All of that is our own arithmetic from the inputs in section 1. A few things stand out. The condenser is more than four times the cost of the airer per load, which is the number the airer adverts lean on. The heat pump dryer is only about twice, and if you leave the airer on for eight hours rather than five, the gap shrinks to 12p a load, or about £19 a year. That is real money but it is not a life-changing sum, and it is the figure the adverts do not mention.

The clothes horse row is there because it is the honest baseline. It costs nothing. Its problem is not money, it is time and moisture: a load of washing straight from a 1,200 rpm spin still holds a litre or two of water, and every drop of that goes into the air of the room. In a well-ventilated house in September that is fine. In a small flat in January it is condensation on the windows and, eventually, black mould on the cold wall behind the wardrobe. The heated airer does not change the amount of water, it just gets it into the air faster, so the same warning applies.

3. The payback sum, and the household where it fails

Payback is the purchase price divided by the annual saving. We will use £140 for the airer, which is the sort of money a branded three-tier model costs; if you pay less, the payback is shorter.

So the case for buying an airer is strong if you currently run a condenser or vented dryer, moderate if you have a heat pump dryer, and non-existent if you already dry on a rack and can live with the drying time.

The household where the sum fails is the one that does not actually stop using the dryer. If the airer becomes the place you dry the delicates while the dryer still runs for towels and bedding, you have added £32 a year of airer to whatever the dryer already costs, and the payback is negative. Be honest about which household you are before you buy. A family doing five loads a week in a three-bed semi with one radiator in the utility room may find that an airer holding two loads a day is a bottleneck, not a saving.

The other honest caveat is that 160 loads a year is the label's assumption, not yours. A single person doing two loads a week is at about 100. A family of five with a school uniform rota is nearer 300. Scale the annual figures up or down in proportion, because the per-load costs do not change.

4. The costs the per-load figure hides

Lifespan and warranty. Lakeland gives three years on the airer. Hotpoint gives one year labour and ten years parts on the dryers. Bosch is currently offering five years on its heat pump dryers bought between 1 April 2026 and 31 March 2027 if you register within three months. A heated airer is a simple resistive element and a switch; there is little to fail. A heat pump dryer is a refrigeration circuit plus a motor plus electronics, and when it does fail out of warranty the repair bill is a large fraction of a new one. That does not change the per-load maths but it changes what you actually spend over ten years.

Your time. A condenser cycle on the Hotpoint model above is 142 minutes for the standard cotton programme, the heat pump 209 minutes. The airer is five to eight hours and you have to hang and turn things. If you value your time at anything, the airer is not free.

The heat is not wasted, in winter. Every one of the 300 W the airer draws ends up as warmth in the room, and so does most of what a condenser dryer uses. Between October and March that is heat you would otherwise have bought from the boiler, at about 8.9p per kWh of heat from gas at 7.97p and 90% efficiency. So the true winter cost of the airer is nearer 20p minus the gas it displaced, which is a few pence. In summer, the same heat is simply unwanted. This is a small effect and we have left it out of the tables, but it slightly favours the electric options in winter and the clothes horse in summer.

The moisture, again. If you end up buying a dehumidifier to cope with the airer, that machine has its own running cost, typically 5p to 6p an hour for a compressor model, and its own purchase price. Add it to the sum if that is your situation.

5. Worked example: Farida and Tom in Hitchin

Two adults, a toddler, four loads a week, a condenser dryer bought in 2019

Farida and Tom do about four loads a week, roughly 200 a year, and until now everything went through a vented condenser dryer in the garage. They are on a standard variable tariff, so the Ofgem rate applies.

Now. 200 loads at 3.51 kWh is 702 kWh a year. At 26.32p that is £185 a year in electricity for drying.

With a heated airer for everything except bedding. Say 170 loads go on the airer in 85 fills of five hours, and 30 loads of bedding and towels still go through the dryer. Airer: 85 fills at 1.5 kWh is 127.5 kWh, £34. Dryer: 30 loads at 3.51 kWh is 105 kWh, £28. Total £62 a year, a saving of £123. A £140 airer pays back in about 14 months.

If instead they replaced the dryer with a heat pump model. 200 loads at 1.66 kWh is 332 kWh, £87 a year, a saving of £98. Against a purchase of several hundred pounds that is a payback of four to six years, with no change to how they do the laundry.

The combination. Airer for 170 loads plus heat pump dryer for 30 is £34 plus £13, £47 a year. That is the cheapest arrangement, but it means buying both, and the extra saving of the heat pump dryer over keeping the old condenser for 30 loads is only £15 a year. On that basis they should buy the airer now and replace the dryer only when it dies.

6. The honest verdict

If you own a condenser or vented tumble dryer and you would genuinely use an airer for most loads, buy one. At 20p a load against 92p it pays for itself inside a winter and a half, and after that it is about £100 a year in your pocket for as long as it lasts.

If you own a heat pump dryer, the saving is about £38 a year and the airer takes nearly four years to pay back. That is a marginal purchase, and a reasonable person could decide their time is worth more than that.

If you already dry on a rack and your home does not suffer from damp, do not buy a heated airer to save money, because it will cost you £32 a year you are not spending now. Buy one only for the speed, and know that is what you are paying for.

Frequently asked

Is a heated airer cheaper than a tumble dryer? Per load, yes: about 20p against 92p for a condenser and 44p for a heat pump dryer at the October 2026 cap rate, using manufacturer wattage and energy label figures. Per year it depends entirely on whether you stop using the dryer.

How much does a heated airer cost per hour? A 300 W model costs 300 divided by 1,000 multiplied by 26.32p, which is 7.9p an hour at the October 2026 rate. A model with a different wattage on its plate scales in proportion.

Does a heated airer cause damp? It puts exactly the same water into the room as a clothes horse, just faster. Open a window, use it in a room with a trickle vent, or run a dehumidifier alongside it in a small flat.

Is a heat pump dryer worth it instead? Against a condenser it roughly halves the per-load cost with no change to how you do laundry. The premium over a condenser is the question, and we cover that separately.
