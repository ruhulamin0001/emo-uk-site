1. Find the wattage before you do anything else

Electric blankets vary more than almost any other appliance in this series, and the marketing rarely leads with the number. UK blankets run from about 40 watts to 150 watts, and oversized king models can reach 250.

Rough guide, and check yours against it: single 40 to 60W, double around 80W, king 100 to 150W, oversized king up to 250W. Heated throws, the ones you put over you on a sofa, are usually 100 to 120W.

The wattage on the label is the maximum, on the highest setting. Most blankets have a thermostat or a low setting that cycles, so real average draw over an evening is often well below the plate figure. This page uses the full plate figure throughout, which makes every number here a worst case. Your actual bill will be lower.

The other thing the label tells you is whether it is an underblanket or an overblanket. Underblankets go beneath the sheet and are what you want, because the mattress underneath them is an insulator and reflects the heat upwards into you rather than into the room. Overblankets lose more of it upwards.

3. What the blanket does not do, and the point where it stops being enough

A blanket heats a bed. It does not heat a room, and there are limits to how far you should push that.

It does not protect the building. A bedroom that drops below about 16 degrees for long periods is where condensation forms on cold surfaces, and that is how you get mould in the corners and behind the wardrobe. If you switch the bedroom radiator off entirely and rely on a blanket, keep the room ventilated and watch the walls. Mould costs far more to fix than heat costs to run.

It does not suit everyone. NHS and Age UK guidance is that older people, very young children and anyone with a condition affecting circulation or sensation should keep bedrooms warmer, around 18 degrees, rather than relying on localised heat. If that is your household, treat the blanket as a supplement, not a replacement.

It does not fix a cold house. If the room is losing heat through an uninsulated loft or single glazing, the blanket is a very cheap patch on a much more expensive problem. Our pages on loft insulation payback and thermal curtains against blinds cover the fabric side, and both pay back faster than most people expect.

Safety, briefly, because it matters. Electric blankets should be checked every three years and replaced after about ten. Never use one that is creased, scorched, frayed at the flex, or wet. Do not use an underblanket with an adjustable bed unless it is made for one, and never fold one while it is on. Many fire services run free electric blanket testing in autumn.

4. Worked example: Iwona in Peterborough

Iwona rents a two-bedroom flat with gas central heating she controls from a single programmer. Her habit has been to leave the heating on low overnight because the bedroom is cold at the far end from the boiler.

Running the heating overnight at roughly 4kW of output for eight hours is 32 kWh of useful heat, so 35.6 kWh of gas at 7.97p, which is £2.84 a night. Over 150 nights that is £426.

She buys a double underblanket for £42, sets the heating to go off at eleven and come back on at six, and uses the blanket to preheat the bed for thirty minutes then leaves it on the lowest setting.

Her blanket costs about £9.48 a winter on the worst-case arithmetic above. Her heating now runs seven fewer hours a night, cutting roughly £2.48 a night, or £372 a winter.

Net saving: about £362. Payback on the £42 blanket: 42 nights.

She keeps the bedroom radiator open on its thermostatic valve set to the lowest mark rather than closing it, so the room does not fall cold enough to condense, and she opens the window for ten minutes each morning. That last habit is free and it is the reason her landlord's walls stayed dry.

The saving is unusually large because leaving central heating on all night is an unusually expensive habit. If you already turn the heating off at bedtime, expect the eleven month payback from the table rather than six weeks.

5. The honest verdict

Buy the blanket. At about £45 for a double it pays back inside a single winter against a gas radiator, in under two months against a plug-in electric heater, and in about six weeks if you are currently leaving the central heating on overnight. There is almost nothing else in a house with that return.

Do not use it as an excuse to let the bedroom go properly cold. Leave the radiator valve cracked open on its lowest setting, ventilate in the morning, and keep the room above about 16 degrees. The cost of that is small and the cost of mould is not.

Check the flex, check the age, and if it is over ten years old replace it before you use it again.

Frequently asked

Is it safe to leave an electric blanket on all night? Only if it is designed for it. Blankets are labelled either as preheat only, which must be switched off before you get in, or as safe for all night use with a thermostat. The label and the manual say which. Never assume.

How old is too old for an electric blanket? Fire services generally advise replacing at ten years and testing every three. If the flex is frayed, the fabric is scorched or discoloured, or the wires can be felt as hard ridges, stop using it now rather than at the next test.

Do heated throws cost more to run than underblankets? Usually a little, because they are typically 100 to 120W against 80W for a double underblanket, and because they sit on top so more heat escapes upwards. They are still trivially cheap against any form of room heating, and they work in a sitting room where a blanket does not.

Can I use one on a memory foam mattress? Check the mattress manufacturer's guidance first. Some memory foam responds badly to sustained heat and some warranties exclude it. If in doubt use an overblanket or a heated throw instead.
