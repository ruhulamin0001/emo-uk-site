Your Fuse Box Swap Became a Rewire Quote. Here Is Why

A consumer unit replacement is quoted at a few hundred pounds and lands at a few thousand often enough that people assume they are being fleeced. Usually they are not. The board is the cheap part, and the expensive part is what the board reveals. Here is the mechanism, and how to know in advance.

The short version

  • A straightforward board change typically runs £450 to £900 including certification, on an installation in reasonable condition.
  • The reason quotes escalate is testing. A new consumer unit must be certified, which means every circuit it feeds is tested, and old circuits fail tests that an old board never applied to them.
  • The usual failures are low insulation resistance on old cable, no earth at lighting points, and missing main protective bonding. The first of those is the one that leads to a rewire.
  • You can find out before you commit. An EICR at £150 to £300 tells you what the board change will uncover, and it is a fraction of the cost of finding out halfway through.
  • Consumer unit replacement is notifiable work under Part P. A registered electrician self-certifies. Nobody else should be touching it.

1. Why anyone replaces a board at all

There are four honest reasons, and one dishonest one.

The board is a rewirable fuse carrier. Ceramic holders with fuse wire, usually in a wooden or bakelite enclosure. No residual current protection of any kind. These are genuinely obsolete and there is no argument for keeping one.

You are adding something the existing board cannot feed. An electric shower, an induction hob, an electric vehicle charge point or a heat pump each need a dedicated way, and a full board has nowhere to put one.

There is no residual current protection where it is now required. RCD or RCBO protection is what disconnects a circuit fast enough to prevent electrocution, and there are circuits in modern practice where it is expected.

The enclosure is combustible. Since the 2015 amendment to BS 7671, consumer units in domestic premises are required to have a non-combustible enclosure, which in practice means steel. Plastic boards are not automatically unsafe and do not have to be ripped out, but it is a relevant consideration when one is being replaced anyway.

And the dishonest reason: "your board is old, it needs upgrading to the latest regulations." An installation does not have to comply with the current edition of BS 7671 because a new edition has been published. Existing installations are assessed on safety, not currency. Anyone leading with that line should be asked which specific defect they have found.

2. The mechanism that turns a swap into a rewire

This is the part nobody explains, and once explained the escalating quote stops looking like a scam.

When an electrician installs a new consumer unit, they are taking responsibility for it. That means issuing an Electrical Installation Certificate, and that certificate covers not just the box but the circuits it protects. So before they can sign it, every circuit is tested: continuity, polarity, earth fault loop impedance, and crucially insulation resistance.

Insulation resistance is the test that catches out old houses. It measures how well the insulation around the conductors is still doing its job. Rubber-insulated cable from the 1950s and 1960s becomes brittle and crumbles, particularly where it has been run near heat or bent hard at a fitting. It has been quietly failing for decades while the old board, which never tested anything, sat there happily.

Put a modern board with RCD protection onto a circuit with degraded insulation and one of two things happens. Either the test fails at installation, or it passes marginally and then the RCD trips repeatedly in use, because the leakage the old system tolerated is exactly what an RCD is designed to detect.

So the electrician who quoted £600 and then said the house needs rewiring is usually telling you something true and unwelcome: the fault was always there, and the new board is what found it.

Two lesser versions of the same thing. No circuit protective conductor at lighting points, common in pre-1966 wiring, which means no earth at the light fittings and a partial rewire of the lighting circuits. And missing main protective bonding to incoming gas and water services, which is a few hundred pounds rather than a few thousand and is very frequently found.

4. How to find out in advance for under £300

Book an Electrical Installation Condition Report before you book anything else, from an electrician who is not the one quoting for the board.

The EICR does the same testing the board change would trigger, and produces a coded list of what it found. That tells you, before you have committed to anything and before anyone has removed your existing board, whether you are in the £600 world or the £5,000 world.

Three things to ask for when you book it. That the insulation resistance results are given as actual values in megohms, not just a pass or fail, because a circuit sitting just above the limit is one that will deteriorate. That the report states the cable type found, since rubber or lead-sheathed cable changes the whole conversation. And that any recommendations are separated by observation code, so mandatory work and improvements are not blended into one figure.

Take that report to two or three electricians for quotes. You will get comparable prices for defined work, rather than three different guesses about a box nobody has opened.

Worked example: the Adeyemi household, Luton

1962 semi, original-looking board with plug-in fuse carriers, buying the house and wanting an electric vehicle charge point fitted.

First quote, from the EV installer: £1,350 for the charge point including a board change, subject to survey. The phrase "subject to survey" was doing a great deal of work.

What they did instead. Booked an EICR from an independent electrician for £210.

What it found. Insulation resistance on the ring circuits was fine, cable was PVC throughout, so no rewire. But the lighting circuits had no circuit protective conductor, coded C2, and there was no main protective bonding to the incoming water, also C2.

The defined quote. Board change £720, main bonding £240, lighting circuits rewired £1,480. Total £2,440, plus the charge point at £900.

The value of the £210. They renegotiated £2,000 off the purchase price on the strength of the report, which is roughly ten times what the report cost, and they went into the work knowing the final figure rather than discovering it in stages.

5. What should be in the new board, and what should not

Two boards at the same price can be very different products, and the difference is in how the protection is arranged.

Split load with two RCDs is the cheaper arrangement. Circuits are divided into two banks, each behind one residual current device. It works, and it has one irritating consequence: a fault on any circuit in a bank takes out every circuit in that bank. A fault on the freezer takes the kitchen sockets and half the lighting with it, at two in the morning, while you are away.

RCBOs on every way gives each circuit its own combined overcurrent and residual current protection. A fault disconnects one circuit and nothing else. It costs perhaps £150 to £300 more on a domestic board, and on any house where a freezer, a home office or a medical device matters, it is the specification to ask for.

Surge protection is now commonly fitted and is a reasonable addition at modest cost where there is electronic equipment worth protecting. Arc fault detection is a further option and is genuinely useful in some situations, though it is not required in most domestic installations and it adds cost.

What should not be in the quote is a suggestion that the board will reduce your electricity bill, or any device sold on that basis. Distribution equipment does not change consumption, and anything marketed as an energy-saving addition at the consumer unit deserves a very hard look.

6. The honest verdict

Do not book a board change blind on a house wired before about 1970. Book an EICR first, from someone who is not quoting for the work, and spend the two hundred pounds to find out which conversation you are actually having.

If the tests come back clean, a board change is a straightforward day's work at a few hundred pounds and you should simply get it done, particularly if there is a rewirable fuse carrier in there.

And if an electrician tells you the whole house needs rewiring, ask to see the insulation resistance readings circuit by circuit. A competent one will show you without being asked, and the numbers either support the recommendation or they do not.

Frequently asked

Is it illegal to have an old fuse box? No. There is no legal requirement for a homeowner to upgrade an installation simply because standards have moved on. The duty is different for landlords, who must hold a satisfactory EICR, and an old board with genuine defects will produce an unsatisfactory one.

Can I change a consumer unit myself? No. Consumer unit replacement is notifiable work under Part P of the Building Regulations in England and Wales, and it involves working at the origin of the installation where the supply cannot be isolated by you. Use a registered electrician.

How long does a board last? Twenty-five years or more is normal. Boards are usually replaced because requirements or loads have changed rather than because the board has failed.

Will a new board make my electricity cheaper? No. A consumer unit distributes and protects, it does not affect consumption. Anyone claiming an energy saving from a board change is either mistaken or selling something else.
