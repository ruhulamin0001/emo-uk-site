Most Septic Tank Owners Have Never Read the Rules That Govern Them

If you have a septic tank in England you are legally its operator, and you can run it without a permit only if you meet every one of the Environment Agency's General Binding Rules. One of those rules catches a great many rural households by surprise: a septic tank that discharges straight into a stream or ditch does not comply, and the Agency expects you to fix it, usually within about twelve months.

The short version

  • No permit is needed if you meet all the General Binding Rules. Miss any one of them and you need an Environment Agency permit instead.
  • Septic tank straight into a watercourse is the big one. The Environment Agency says you must connect to a public sewer, replace the tank with a small sewage treatment plant, or install a drainage field, with plans in place usually within 12 months.
  • You must empty it at least once a year, or in line with the manufacturer's instructions, and the company taking the sludge away must be a registered waste carrier.
  • Ask the tanker driver for the waste carrier's certificate. The Environment Agency's own guidance says you can ask for a copy. That is your evidence, and it costs nothing.
  • Selling the house: you must tell the buyer in writing. Not a courtesy, a requirement, with a defined list of details to hand over.
  • Emptying is roughly £200 to £400 a year. Replacing a tank with a treatment plant is £5,000 upwards. The rule decides which of those you are facing, not your budget.

1. What the General Binding Rules actually require

Everything below is from the Environment Agency's guidance on the General Binding Rules for a small sewage discharge to a surface water, read on 13 September 2026. It applies to England. Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales each have their own regime and their own regulator, so if you are not in England, the numbers on this page are not yours.

The structure is simple. If you are the operator of a septic tank or small sewage treatment plant, you do not need a permit provided you meet all of the rules that apply to you. Fail any one and you must either fix it, connect to a public sewer, or apply for a permit.

The rules that apply to every discharge include:

  • Volume. Discharge no more than 5 cubic metres, which is 5,000 litres, a day to surface water. Above that you must connect to the public foul sewer where it is reasonable to do so, or hold a permit.
  • Domestic sewage only. From a toilet, bathroom, shower or kitchen.
  • No pollution. The discharge must not cause pollution of surface water or groundwater.
  • The correct treatment system. Discharging to a watercourse requires a small sewage treatment plant, which treats to a higher standard than a septic tank.
  • British Standard. Your system must meet the standard in force when it was installed. Systems installed before 1983 are exempt from this one, because there was no standard then.
  • Correct installation, operation and capacity, in line with the manufacturer's specification.
  • Regular maintenance, by a competent person, with repair or replacement if the system is not in good working order.
  • Regular emptying. Desludge before the sludge exceeds maximum capacity, at least once a year or per the manufacturer's instructions, using a registered waste carrier.
  • Tell the new owner in writing if you sell.
  • Decommission properly if you stop using it, removing anything that could cause pollution.

Discharges that started on or after 1 January 2015 carry extra rules, including that you cannot rely on the General Binding Rules if there is a public foul sewer within 30 metres of any boundary of the premises your system serves, and that the discharge must not be in or within 500 metres of protected sites such as biological SSSIs, special protection areas, special areas of conservation, Ramsar wetlands, designated bathing water or protected shellfish water. Tighter distances apply near aquatic local nature reserves at 200 metres and chalk rivers at 50 metres.

Discharges that started on or after 2 October 2023 carry a further rule again: the new discharge must not be within 50 metres of any other small sewage discharge operating under the General Binding Rules, and must not share an outlet where the combined volume would exceed the limit.

What the rules do not require, and this is worth stating plainly because a good deal of marketing implies otherwise. There is no requirement to register your septic tank with the Environment Agency in England if you meet the rules. There is no periodic licence to renew. There is no legal requirement to have your tank emptied by any particular company, only that whoever takes the waste is a registered waste carrier. And there is no rule that says a septic tank as such is illegal. It is the destination of the discharge that matters.

2. The rule that catches people out

Here is the sentence that changes lives in rural England, paraphrased closely from the guidance:

If your septic tank discharges directly to a watercourse, as soon as possible you must connect to a public foul sewer, replace the tank with a small sewage treatment plant, or install a drainage field.

And the Agency adds that you must have plans in place to do this work within a reasonable timescale, usually twelve months.

A septic tank does primary settlement. It separates solids and lets partly treated liquid out. That liquid is fine soaking away through a properly designed drainage field, where the soil finishes the treatment. It is not fine going straight into a stream, and that is why the rule exists.

An enormous number of older rural properties do exactly this, because it was normal practice when they were built and nobody has looked since. If you do not know where your outfall goes, that is the single most valuable thing you can find out about your house this year, and it costs nothing but an afternoon and a wet weather walk downhill from the tank.

The three lawful fixes, and they are not equal.

  1. Connect to a public foul sewer. Best outcome if one is near. Also worth asking your sewerage undertaker about first time sewerage, which is the duty that can arise where more than one property needs to replace or upgrade.
  2. Install a drainage field. Often the cheapest fix and it keeps the existing tank, but it needs suitable ground, enough land, and it must meet the rules for discharges to ground, which are a separate set.
  3. Replace the tank with a small sewage treatment plant. The default answer that most contractors will quote, because it always works. It is also the most expensive and it introduces an electrical running cost the septic tank never had.

Ask about the drainage field before you accept a quote for a treatment plant. Whether it is possible depends on ground conditions and space, so it may genuinely not be, but a contractor who sells treatment plants has no reason to raise it.

One further point most quotes leave out: installing a new sewage treatment plant needs building regulations approval, and may need planning permission. The Environment Agency guidance says so directly. The same certificate problem covered in our guide to rewiring a house applies here, and it applies at the moment you sell.

4. Buying and selling a property with a tank

This is where non compliance stops being theoretical and turns into money.

If you are selling, the guidance is explicit: you must tell the new operator in writing that a sewage discharge is in place, and include a description of the treatment plant and drainage system, the location of the main parts and the discharge point, details of any changes made, details of how it should be maintained together with the manual if you have one, and maintenance records if you have them.

Start assembling that folder now rather than during a sale. Emptying invoices, the installation paperwork, any service records, and a sketch of where the tank and the outfall actually are. A seller who can produce that answers a conveyancing enquiry in a day. A seller who cannot invites a retention.

If you are buying, the guidance says you should agree with the seller who will be responsible for replacing or upgrading a system that discharges directly to a watercourse, and agree it as a condition of sale. That is a negotiating point worth several thousand pounds and it exists only before contracts are exchanged.

The three questions to ask, in this order: where does the discharge go, when was it last emptied and by whom, and does the system meet the British Standard in force when it was installed. If the answer to the first is a stream and the tank is a septic tank, you have found a five figure liability and you found it in time.

5. Worked example: the Trelawney cottage in Devon

A stone cottage outside Okehampton, built in the 1890s, septic tank believed to be from the 1970s. The owners have it emptied every eighteen months or so, when the smell reminds them, at about £280 a time. They are thinking of selling in two years.

They follow the outfall pipe on a wet day and find it discharging into a ditch that feeds a stream forty metres away.

Current compliance. Two rules failed: emptying is not at least annual, and a septic tank discharges to a watercourse.

Fix one, free. Move to annual emptying. £280 a year instead of about £187 a year averaged. Costs £93 a year more, and fixes a rule.

Fix two, the real one. Two quotes. A drainage field using the existing tank, in the paddock, at £4,200. Or a new small sewage treatment plant at £7,800.

Drainage field over 25 years. £4,200 ÷ 25 = £168 a year, plus £280 a year emptying. No electricity.

Treatment plant over 20 years. £7,800 ÷ 20 = £390 a year, plus service and emptying at around £400, plus electricity at roughly £150. About £940 a year.

The drainage field is less than half the annual cost, and it was the option only one of the two contractors mentioned. Whether it is available depends on percolation testing of that paddock, which is a small cost worth paying before deciding.

The sale argument is the decisive one though. Doing nothing for two years and then selling means disclosing, in writing, a septic tank discharging to a watercourse. A buyer's solicitor will read the same guidance this article quotes and will ask for a price reduction of at least the cost of the fix, probably more, and at a moment when the Trelawneys have no leverage. Spending £4,200 now, with time to get competing quotes and to test the ground, is cheaper than having £8,000 taken off the price later.

Frequently asked

Do I have to register my septic tank? In England, if you meet the General Binding Rules you do not need a permit and there is no general registration requirement for compliant discharges. If you cannot meet the rules, you must apply for a permit. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have different arrangements, including registration in some cases, so check your own nation's regulator.

How do I know if my system met the British Standard? The guidance gives three checks: a CE mark, a certificate of compliance in the manual or documentation, or presence on British Water's list of approved equipment. You can also ask the installer to confirm. And if it was installed before 1983, this rule does not apply to you at all.

What counts as a competent person for maintenance? The guidance points to British Water's list of accredited service engineers, and says there may be other competent engineers too. It is not a closed list, but that list is the defensible starting point.

What happens if I ignore it? The guidance says that if your discharge causes pollution you may be committing an offence, that the Environment Agency will first give advice to help you fix the problem, and that it may take enforcement action if pollution continues. In practice the more common consequence for a household is not enforcement at all. It is the sale.

Is this the same as a cesspit? No. A cesspit or cesspool is a sealed tank with no discharge at all, which has to be emptied far more often and is correspondingly expensive to run. The rules discussed here concern systems that discharge.

The honest verdict

Find out where your discharge goes. If it soaks away through a drainage field and the tank is emptied annually by a registered waste carrier, you are very probably compliant, the running cost is £200 to £400 a year, and there is nothing to buy.

If a septic tank discharges straight into a ditch, stream or any other watercourse, you have a real legal obligation and a real liability, and it will surface at the worst possible time, which is when you sell. Deal with it while you have time to get three quotes.

When you do, ask about a drainage field before you accept a quote for a treatment plant. It keeps the tank you already own, it has no electricity bill, and on the numbers above it can cost less than half as much a year. It is not always possible, but it is almost never offered.
