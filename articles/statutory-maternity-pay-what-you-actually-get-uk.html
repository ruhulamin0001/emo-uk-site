Statutory Maternity Pay Is 39 Weeks, Not 52, and Only Six of Them Are at 90%. What a £28,000 Salary Actually Receives

Maternity leave lasts up to a year. Maternity pay does not, and the part most people picture, 90% of salary, lasts six weeks. The rest of the paid period is a flat £194.32 a week in 2026/27. Here is how the rule works, who qualifies, what the fallback is for those who do not, and what the money actually adds up to on an ordinary salary.

The short version

  • SMP is paid for 39 weeks: 90% of your average weekly earnings for the first six, then £194.32 a week or 90% of average earnings, whichever is lower, for the next 33. The £194.32 rate applies from 5 April 2026. Source: GOV.UK rates and thresholds for employers 2026 to 2027.
  • Statutory Maternity Leave is 52 weeks, so the final 13 weeks are unpaid unless your employer offers more. Tax and National Insurance come off SMP like ordinary wages.
  • To qualify you must have worked for the same employer for 26 weeks continuously into the qualifying week, the 15th week before the week the baby is due, and earn at least £129 a week on average in the eight weeks before it.
  • Miss those tests and the fallback is Maternity Allowance from the DWP: £194.32 a week or 90% of earnings if lower for 39 weeks if employed, and between £27 and £194.32 a week if self-employed, depending on Class 2 contributions.
  • On a £28,000 salary, SMP over 39 weeks comes to £9,320.25 gross on our arithmetic, against £21,000 you would have earned in the same 39 weeks. The full year off costs £18,679.75 in gross pay.
  • Your employer reclaims 92% of SMP from HMRC, or 109% if it is a small employer. So an employer refusing to pay SMP is not saving much, and if it is insolvent HMRC's Statutory Payment Disputes Team pays instead.

1. The shape of the 39 weeks

Statutory Maternity Pay comes in two blocks. For the first six weeks it is 90% of your average weekly earnings before tax, with no upper limit. For the following 33 weeks it is the lower of £194.32 a week or 90% of your average weekly earnings. The £194.32 is the 2026 to 2027 rate, in force from 5 April 2026, up from £187.18 the year before. It usually rises each April with the September inflation figure.

That makes 39 weeks of pay. Statutory Maternity Leave, the right to be away from work and come back to the same or an equivalent job, lasts 52 weeks. So anyone taking the full year has 13 weeks at the end with no statutory pay at all. Many people plan the year around the 90% weeks and are surprised by week 40.

SMP is paid by your employer through payroll in the same way as wages, so income tax and National Insurance are deducted from it. It is not a benefit, and it is not affected by savings or a partner's income. It can start any time from 11 weeks before the expected week of childbirth, and it starts automatically if you are off work with a pregnancy-related illness in the four weeks before the due date.

Average weekly earnings, the number everything else is calculated from, are worked out over roughly the eight weeks before the qualifying week. That matters because a bonus, overtime or a pay rise falling in that window lifts the 90% weeks, and a period of reduced hours or unpaid leave in it lowers them. The £194.32 flat rate is the same for everyone.

2. Who qualifies: the qualifying week and the £129 floor

The qualifying week is the 15th week before the week your baby is due. To get SMP you must have been employed by the same employer continuously for at least 26 weeks running into that week. Counting back, the qualifying week falls around the 25th week of pregnancy, and 26 weeks before that is around the time of conception. In practice you need to have started the job before you became pregnant, give or take a week or two. Start a new job once you already know you are pregnant and you will not get SMP from it, however long you work there before the birth.

You must also earn at least the lower earnings limit on average, which is £129 a week for 2026 to 2027, in the eight weeks up to the qualifying week. That is about £6,708 a year. Part-time workers below it, and people on zero-hours contracts with quiet weeks in the window, can miss SMP on earnings alone.

You have to give the correct notice, at least 28 days before you want pay to start, and provide proof of the pregnancy, normally the MATB1 certificate your midwife issues from around 20 weeks. Miss the notice deadline without a good reason and the employer can refuse.

If you do not qualify, your employer must give you form SMP1 within seven days of deciding, explaining why. Keep it, because it is the document the DWP asks for when you claim Maternity Allowance instead.

3. A worked example on £28,000 in Derby

Chloe works full time for a logistics firm in Derby on £28,000 a year. Her average weekly earnings are £28,000 divided by 52, which is £538.46. She has been there three years, so the 26 week test is met, and she is well above the £129 floor.

Her first six weeks are at 90% of £538.46, which is £484.62 a week, or £2,907.69 for the six weeks. Her next 33 weeks are at the lower of £194.32 or 90% of earnings; £194.32 is lower, so she gets £194.32 a week, or £6,412.56 for the 33 weeks. Her total SMP over 39 weeks is £9,320.25 gross on our arithmetic.

Had she stayed at work, those 39 weeks would have paid £21,000 gross (39 times £538.46). So SMP replaces about 44% of her gross pay across the paid period, and about 36% of the 90% headline that most people remember. If she takes the full 52 weeks, the last 13 are unpaid and the year's gross shortfall is £18,679.75.

The net position is a little better, because tax and National Insurance on £194.32 a week are small. At that level she is below the £242 a week primary threshold for employee National Insurance, so nothing is deducted for NI in the flat rate weeks, and with her personal allowance spread over the year her income tax is modest too. On the other side, her workplace pension contributions during leave are normally based on the SMP she actually receives, while her employer's contributions must stay at the level based on her normal salary. That is a rule worth checking on your own scheme before you go.

4. If you do not qualify: Maternity Allowance

Maternity Allowance is the DWP's benefit for people who cannot get SMP: the self-employed, people who changed jobs too late, people who earned under £129 a week, and people who recently stopped working. It is claimed on form MA1, from 26 weeks of pregnancy, and can be paid from 11 weeks before the due date.

For employees and the recently employed the amount is £194.32 a week or 90% of average earnings if lower, for up to 39 weeks. Note what is missing: there are no six weeks at 90%. On Chloe's salary that difference is £2,907.69 minus six times £194.32, which is £1,741.77 less over the year on our arithmetic. To qualify you need to have been employed or self-employed for at least 26 of the 66 weeks before the due date, and to have earned at least £30 a week in any 13 of those weeks.

For the self-employed the rate depends on Class 2 National Insurance. Pay enough Class 2 contributions for the 13 weeks before the claim and you get the full £194.32; pay none and it is £27 a week. HMRC lets you pay the Class 2 needed at the point of claim, and the DWP tells you how much when it processes the MA1. It is one of the few places where a small voluntary National Insurance payment buys a large return, and it is routinely missed.

5. Enhanced pay, keeping in touch days and the things that do not stop

Many employers pay more than SMP, often called enhanced or occupational maternity pay: a common pattern is full pay for a number of weeks followed by SMP. It is contractual, so it is in your staff handbook rather than on GOV.UK, and it often comes with a clawback if you do not return for a set period afterwards. Read that clause before relying on the money.

You can work up to 10 keeping in touch days during maternity leave without ending your leave or your SMP. Both sides have to agree, and the pay for those days is a matter for the contract, not the statute. Work an eleventh day and you lose SMP for that week.

Your rights while on leave continue as if you were at work: holiday accrues, pay rises apply, and you are entitled to be offered a suitable alternative job ahead of redundancy. Your employer can reclaim 92% of the SMP it pays from HMRC, or 109% if it paid £45,000 or less in Class 1 National Insurance in the last complete tax year, so the money is largely not the employer's. If your employer will not pay or cannot, because it has gone under, HMRC's Statutory Payment Disputes Team can decide the case and pay you directly.

6. The honest verdict

Plan on 39 weeks of pay, not 52, and on £194.32 a week after the first six. On a £28,000 salary that is £9,320.25 gross across the paid period, and a £18,679.75 shortfall across a full year off.

The two tests that catch people are timing and earnings: 26 weeks with the same employer into the 15th week before the due date, and £129 a week on average. Miss either and Maternity Allowance is the route, with no 90% weeks.

If you are self-employed, check your Class 2 position before you claim. The gap between £27 and £194.32 a week is the biggest single lever in this whole system.

Frequently asked

Is Statutory Maternity Pay 90% for the whole time? No. It is 90% of average weekly earnings for six weeks only. The next 33 weeks are £194.32 a week or 90% of earnings if that is lower. The last 13 weeks of a 52 week leave are unpaid.

I started a new job at 14 weeks pregnant. Can I get SMP? Not from that employer, because you will not have 26 weeks' continuous service by the qualifying week. You can claim Maternity Allowance instead, which pays the flat rate for 39 weeks.

Is SMP taxed? Yes. It goes through payroll and income tax and National Insurance are deducted in the normal way, though at £194.32 a week the deductions are small.

What if my employer says it cannot afford SMP? It reclaims 92% or 109% from HMRC, so affordability is rarely the real issue. If it refuses or is insolvent, contact HMRC's Statutory Payment Disputes Team, which can pay you directly.
