Your Provider Claims the First Twenty Percent. Nobody Claims the Second Twenty for You

This is one of the few pieces of unclaimed money in the UK tax system that HMRC tells you about openly on its own website. If you pay tax above the basic rate and your pension is a relief at source scheme, part of your relief is sitting there waiting for you to ask for it.

The short version

  • Two automatic routes exist, and only one of them finishes the job. Net pay, where your employer takes contributions before Income Tax, gives full relief automatically. Relief at source, where the provider claims 20% and adds it to your pot, does not.
  • GOV.UK states the gap plainly. You need to make a claim if you pay Income Tax at a rate above 20% and your pension provider claims the first 20% for you.
  • What you can claim, England, Wales and Northern Ireland: 20% up to the amount of income you paid 40% tax on, and 25% up to the amount you paid 45% tax on.
  • Scotland has four figures, not two: 1% against income taxed at 21%, 22% against 42%, 25% against 45% and 28% against 48%.
  • GOV.UK's own example. Earn £60,270, pay £12,000 into a relief at source scheme, and you can claim extra relief on £10,000, the part that was taxed at 40%. Nothing extra on the remaining £2,000.
  • Route depends on Self Assessment. If you file a return you must claim through it. The online service is for claiming through your tax code for the current tax year only.

1. Two schemes, two very different outcomes

Every UK pension contribution gets tax relief, but the mechanism differs, and the mechanism decides whether you have anything left to do.

GOV.UK describes the two automatic routes: either "your employer takes workplace pension contributions out of your pay before deducting Income Tax", or "your pension provider claims tax relief from the government at the basic 20% rate and adds it to your pension pot ('relief at source')".

Net pay. The contribution comes out of gross pay, so you never pay Income Tax on it at any rate. A 40% taxpayer gets 40% relief automatically and there is nothing to claim.

Relief at source. The contribution comes out of pay that has already been taxed. The provider then reclaims 20% from the government and adds it to the pot. If you are a basic rate taxpayer that is the whole relief. If you pay tax above 20%, it is not.

GOV.UK sets out the consequence directly in a list of situations where you have to claim yourself. The first item: "you pay Income Tax at a rate above 20% and your pension provider claims the first 20% for you (relief at source)".

That sentence is the entire article. A higher rate taxpayer in a relief at source scheme is being given half the relief they are entitled to until they ask for the rest.

2. How to tell which one you are in

You cannot tell from the amount, and you should not guess. Two checks settle it.

Look at your payslip. If your taxable pay is lower than your gross pay by the amount of the contribution, it came out before tax, which is net pay. If it is deducted from pay that has already been taxed, it is relief at source.

Look at your pension statement. In a relief at source scheme you will see money arriving from the provider that you did not pay in. That is the 20%. In a net pay scheme you will not, because the relief happened in the payroll.

GOV.UK notes that relief at source applies "in all personal and stakeholder pensions, and some workplace pensions", so a SIPP or personal pension alongside a workplace scheme is almost certainly relief at source even if the workplace scheme is not. Check each pot separately. That is the commonest way the extra relief goes missing.

3. What you can actually claim

The figures are published and they are not a single percentage.

England, Wales and Northern Ireland. GOV.UK: you can claim additional relief of "20% up to the amount of any income you have paid 40% tax on" and "25% up to the amount of any income you have paid 45% tax on".

Scotland. Four figures, because the rate bands differ: "1% up to the amount of any income you have paid 21% tax on", "22% up to the amount of any income you have paid 42% tax on", "25% up to the amount of any income you have paid 45% tax on" and "28% up to the amount of any income you have paid 48% tax on".

The words doing the work in every one of those lines are up to the amount of any income you have paid that rate of tax on. You are not claiming extra relief on the whole contribution. You are claiming it on the slice of your income that was actually taxed at the higher rate.

There is an overall ceiling too: relief is available on contributions "worth up to 100% of your annual earnings", and GOV.UK says HMRC can ask you to pay back anything over that limit. A separate annual allowance also applies, which this page does not cover.

4. The two routes, and which one you must use

This is where people waste time, because the choice is not free. From HMRC's claim service page, last updated 2 March 2026:

"If you complete a Self Assessment tax return, you must claim through your tax return (for the current tax year and any previous years). Use this service if you are claiming tax relief through your tax code for just the current tax year."

So:

Your situationRoute
You file Self AssessmentClaim on the return, for the current year and previous years
You do not file, current year onlyHMRC's online claim service, applied through your tax code
You cannot claim online, or you are an agentBy letter to HMRC

What you need is listed: your National Insurance number, the type of pension, the provider's name, the net contributions for each tax year claimed, and your payroll reference. You must also send proof for each year, showing your name, the contributions, the year, and evidence the 20% was claimed automatically. HMRC says it will "review your claim and contact you within 28 working days".

One trap, quoted because it is easy to get wrong: amending a claim means providing "details for all the pensions you added previously, including any new ones", and that "will replace your previous claim for the selected tax year only". An amendment is a full restatement, not an addition.

On earlier years, this page will not print a backdating limit. The two GOV.UK pages quoted here do not state one, and a made up number on a tax page is worse than no number. If you are looking at years gone by, the Self Assessment route covers previous years where you file a return, and for anything else ask HMRC directly, because time limits do apply.

Worked example: Sandra in Reading

Sandra earns £62,000 and pays £400 a month, £4,800 a year, into a personal pension. It is a relief at source scheme.

What happens automatically. Her provider reclaims 20% and adds it to the pot. Her £4,800 becomes £6,000 gross.

What does not happen. Nothing else. The relief matching the 40% tax she paid on the top slice of her income sits unclaimed unless she asks.

What she can claim. Extra relief at 20%, on contributions up to the amount of income she paid 40% tax on. With a personal allowance of £12,570 and the higher rate starting above the basic rate band, the whole £6,000 gross contribution falls inside income taxed at 40%, so the extra relief is worth roughly £1,200.

The route. Sandra does not file Self Assessment, so for the current year she uses HMRC's online claim service and the relief comes through her tax code. She needs the net contribution figure for the year and a statement from her provider as proof.

What she nearly did. Assume the pension "already had the tax relief in it", which is true of the first 20% and not of the second. Every rate in this example is illustrative and depends on her exact income, which is the reason to run it on your own figures rather than borrowing hers.

5. Three cases that are not higher rate taxpayers

The claim service is not only for 40% taxpayers, and its eligibility list is broader than most summaries suggest. HMRC says you can claim if you are:

  • an intermediate rate or higher rate taxpayer paying into a personal or workplace pension;
  • a basic rate taxpayer in a workplace pension where the employer does not, or will no longer, claim the relief;
  • a basic rate taxpayer paying a lump sum into a personal or workplace pension that is not under a net pay scheme.

The middle one is worth reading twice. If your employer used to claim the relief and has stopped, the relief does not stop, but it does become yours to claim.

Two more details. If you pay no Income Tax, your provider can still claim at 20% on contributions up to 80% of your earnings, or £2,880 if you have no earnings. And on the 19% Scottish starter rate, the provider claims at 20% and GOV.UK says "you do not need to pay the difference".

Where this does not apply: in a net pay scheme your relief is already complete. Our guide to whether pension consolidation is worth it covers a question that often arises alongside.

The honest verdict

If you pay tax above 20% and your pension is a relief at source scheme, roughly half your tax relief is unclaimed until you claim it. HMRC publishes this on its own website, in a list of situations where you have to act, and it publishes the rates: 20% against income taxed at 40% and 25% against income taxed at 45% in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, with four separate figures in Scotland.

The first job is not the claim, it is the check. Find out whether your scheme is net pay or relief at source, and check every pot separately, because a workplace scheme and a personal pension alongside it can work differently.

Then take the right route. If you file Self Assessment you must claim on the return. If you do not, HMRC's online service handles the current year through your tax code, and you will need the net contribution figure and documentary proof for each year.

Where this does not help you: it is not tax advice, the annual allowance is a separate limit this page does not cover, and it does not print a backdating limit because the GOV.UK pages quoted here do not state one. If you are chasing earlier years, ask HMRC, because time limits apply.

Frequently asked

How do I know if my pension is relief at source? Check whether your contribution comes out of pay before or after Income Tax, and check whether money arrives in your pot from the provider that you did not pay in. GOV.UK says relief at source applies in all personal and stakeholder pensions and some workplace pensions, so a SIPP or personal pension is almost certainly relief at source.

Do I have to file a tax return to claim? No, but the route depends on it. HMRC says that if you complete a Self Assessment return you must claim through the return, for the current year and previous years. If you do not file, the online claim service handles the current tax year through your tax code.

How much is it worth? In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, an extra 20% on contributions up to the amount of income you paid 40% tax on, or 25% up to the amount you paid 45% tax on. GOV.UK's own example has someone earning 60,270 pounds claiming extra relief on 10,000 pounds of a 12,000 pound contribution, and nothing extra on the remaining 2,000.

What if my employer has stopped claiming the relief? That is one of HMRC's listed eligible cases. A basic rate taxpayer in a workplace pension whose employer does not, or will no longer, claim tax relief can claim it themselves through the same service.
