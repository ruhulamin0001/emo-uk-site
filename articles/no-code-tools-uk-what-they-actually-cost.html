Nobody Cancels a £25 Subscription, and That Is the Whole Problem

Automation tools are almost never judged against the only thing that matters, which is how many hours a year they must save before they have paid for themselves. Here is that number for every price point, and the pricing model that quietly decides your bill.

The short version

  • Price automation in hours saved, not in pounds a month. A £20 a month tool costs £240 a year, and the only question is whether it gives you those hours back.
  • At a £40 an hour rate, a £20 a month tool breaks even at six hours a year. That is half an hour a month, which almost any working automation clears.
  • At £100 a month the break-even is 30 hours a year at £40 an hour, which is two and a half hours a month, and far fewer tools clear that.
  • Per-run pricing punishes the wrong things. A workflow that fires on every website visit burns runs on nothing. One that fires on every sale burns runs on money.
  • The honest answer for most one-person businesses is one tool, not four. Four subscriptions at £25 is £1,200 a year and 30 hours of break-even before anything is gained.

1. The only framing that works

Automation tools are sold on capability and bought on hope. Neither tells you whether to pay for one.

The useful question is arithmetic. An annual subscription cost divided by what an hour of your time is worth gives you the number of hours the tool must save before it has earned anything. Below that number it is a cost. Above it, it is a margin.

So you need one figure before you start: what is an hour of your time actually worth? For a freelancer that is roughly your day rate divided by billable hours. For an owner whose time is not billable, use what you would pay someone else to do the task, which is usually a good deal less.

Read the right-hand column first, because months are how people actually think. At £40 an hour, a £20 tool needs to save you half an hour a month. That is a low bar and most genuinely working automations clear it on the first task.

A £100 tool needs two and a half hours a month, every month, forever. That is a real commitment, and it is the level at which people should stop and check rather than subscribe.

The last row is the one that catches one-person businesses. Four modest subscriptions cost exactly the same as one expensive one and carry exactly the same break-even, but the saving is spread across four tools each doing a little. It is much easier to save 30 hours with one well-chosen automation than with four half-used ones.

Nobody cancels a £25 subscription. That is why four of them is the most common automation mistake, and the most expensive.

3. Per-run pricing, and what it punishes

Many automation tools charge by the run, task or operation rather than by the seat. The tiers are usually generous at the bottom and steepen sharply, so the thing that decides your bill is not how useful a workflow is but how often it fires.

This creates a specific failure. A workflow triggered by something common and low value, such as a page view, a form field change or an inbox rule that matches everything, consumes your quota on nothing. A workflow triggered by something rare and high value, such as a completed sale or a signed contract, consumes almost nothing and pays for the whole subscription.

Two rules follow, and they are worth more than any tool comparison:

  1. Trigger as late as possible. Filter first, act second. A run that ends in a filter still counts on most platforms, so move the filtering into the trigger where the tool allows it.
  2. Count your runs before you commit. Estimate events per month and multiply. A workflow firing 40 times a day is 1,200 runs a month, which is a different pricing tier from one firing 40 times a month.

4. The three tasks worth automating first

Across small businesses the same three jobs produce most of the return, because they are frequent, rule-based and dull.

  • Moving data between two systems you already pay for. Sale to accounts, form to CRM, booking to calendar. High frequency, zero judgement, immediate saving.
  • Chasing. Invoice reminders, follow-ups to unanswered quotes, review requests after delivery. These are tasks people skip when busy, so the automation adds revenue rather than just saving time.
  • Assembling the same document every time. Quotes, onboarding packs, monthly reports. Slow by hand, instant once templated.

And the one that is almost never worth automating: anything requiring a judgement call you would want to review anyway. If you are going to read every output before it goes out, the automation has saved you the typing and left you the thinking, which is the smaller half of the job.

Worked example: Adaeze in Croydon

Adaeze runs a one-person bookkeeping practice. Her day rate works out at about £40 an hour once she counts non-billable time.

What she was paying. Four subscriptions at £22, £25, £29 and £19, which is £1,140 a year. Break-even at her rate is 28.5 hours a year.

What she measured. She logged what each tool actually saved over one month. The invoice chaser saved about 90 minutes a month. The client onboarding automation saved about 40 minutes. The other two saved under 15 minutes between them, because she had built them enthusiastically and used them rarely.

The total. Roughly 2 hours 25 minutes a month, or 29 hours a year. Almost exactly break-even, which means the whole stack was earning her nothing.

What she changed. She cancelled the two unused tools, saving £528 a year, and moved the two that worked onto one platform at £29 a month. New cost £348 a year, break-even 8.7 hours, actual saving 26 hours.

The result. Same work automated, £792 a year back, and the stack now returns about three times its cost instead of exactly its cost.

The part that mattered most. Not choosing the right tool. Measuring for one month before renewing anything.

5. Frequently asked

Are free tiers actually usable? Often yes for one or two low-volume workflows, which is exactly where most one-person businesses should start. The break-even on a free tier is zero hours, so it cannot lose.

Is it cheaper to write a script? In subscription terms, yes. In total terms, only if you can maintain it. A script nobody can fix is a liability with no support line.

Should I count the time I spend building the automation? Yes, once, in the first year. A workflow that takes six hours to build and saves one hour a month has not broken even until month six.

Do these costs count as a business expense? Software used wholly and exclusively for the trade is normally an allowable expense, which reduces the real cost by your marginal rate and lowers every break-even in the table above.

6. The honest verdict

Work out your hourly value, find your row in the table, and measure for one month before renewing anything. That single habit is worth more than any tool comparison, because the common failure is not choosing badly. It is paying for four tools and using one.

Start on a free tier with one workflow that moves data between two systems you already pay for. If it saves half an hour a month at £40 an hour, a £20 subscription is already justified. If it does not, no amount of feature comparison will fix that.

Be sceptical above £50 a month. At £40 an hour that is fifteen hours a year, every year, and a small business that can genuinely find fifteen hours of repetitive rule-based work is usually better off building one really good automation than subscribing to a platform that can build forty.
