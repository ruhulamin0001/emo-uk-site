A VPN Does Three Jobs Well. The Adverts Claim About Twelve.

VPN advertising is everywhere and most of it oversells. Hackers will steal your identity, they say, unless you pay £3 a month right now. The truth is duller and more useful: it does two or three specific jobs properly, and for a lot of people those jobs are worth a few pounds a month. For everyone else it is a subscription doing nothing.

The short version

  • Using a VPN is legal in the UK. There is no law against it and no sign of one coming. Businesses have used them for decades.
  • It does three things well: hides your browsing from the network you are on, hides your location from sites you visit, and stops your broadband provider seeing which sites you use.
  • It does not make you anonymous, stop viruses, or protect your banking. Those are the four claims that sell subscriptions and none of them are true.
  • The monthly price is a trap. A monthly plan is often £10 to £12 while the same service on a two year plan is £1.50 to £3 a month. Almost nobody should buy a single month.
  • Renewal costs far more than the introductory deal. Diarise it the day you buy, or you will pay the real price by accident.
  • Mostly avoid free VPNs. Running servers costs money, so a free service is usually being paid another way. Proton VPN's free tier is the honest exception.

1. The legal question, answered

Using a VPN is legal in the UK. There is no law against it and there is no sign of one coming. Businesses have used them for decades to let staff connect to work systems remotely.

What a VPN does not do is make anything illegal legal. Copyright infringement, fraud and accessing criminal marketplaces are still crimes whatever your IP address says. And getting round a streaming service's regional limits usually breaks their terms of service rather than the law, so the risk there is your account, not the police.

One piece of context worth knowing: after the Online Safety Act's age verification rules began being enforced, UK VPN signups spiked sharply. Those checks often ask for ID or a face scan on ordinary sites, which is why a lot of people started looking at privacy tools who had never cared before. That is a legitimate reason to want one and it has nothing to do with hackers.

2. What a VPN genuinely does

  1. It hides your browsing from the network you are on. On public wifi in a cafe, hotel or airport, whoever runs that network can otherwise see a good deal about your traffic. This is real protection and it is the strongest case for buying one.
  2. It hides your real location from the sites you visit. This is how people watch content that is not available in their country, and how travellers keep their usual services working as they do at home.
  3. It stops your broadband provider seeing which sites you visit. How much that matters is a personal judgement, and for some people it is the entire reason.

3. What it does not do, despite the adverts

The claimThe reality
"Makes you anonymous"No. Log into your Google or Facebook account through a VPN and they know exactly who you are. The account is the identifier, not the IP address
"Protects you from viruses and scams"No. If you download something bad or type your card details into a fake site, the VPN watches it happen and does nothing
"Secures your online banking"No. UK banking apps and websites are already encrypted. The padlock in your browser was doing that job before you paid anyone
"Stops you being hacked"No. UK Finance figures show the overwhelming majority of consumer losses come from people being persuaded to send money, not from technical interception
A VPN protects the pipe. Almost every way UK households actually lose money happens at one end of it or the other, where the VPN cannot see and cannot help.

If security is genuinely why you are here, our antivirus guide makes the same argument with the fraud figures, and the two highest-value things you can do are free: unique passwords via a password manager, and two-factor authentication on your email.

4. What it costs, and the pricing trick

The honest answer is that VPN pricing is strange, and understanding why saves you most of the money.

How you buyTypical monthly costWhat you actually hand over
Monthly rolling£10 to £12£10 to £12, cancel any time
One year plan£3 to £5£36 to £60 upfront
Two year plan£1.50 to £3£40 to £70 upfront
Renewal, any planFrequently double or moreCharged automatically to the card you gave them

The long deal is roughly 70% cheaper per month, which is why almost nobody should buy a single month. As of August 2026, the well-known providers sit roughly here on long plans: Surfshark around £1.50 a month, NordVPN around £2.30, Proton VPN around £2.40. All three are established and have been independently tested. Prices change constantly, so check before buying.

Two warnings on price. The cheap headline rate is for the whole term paid upfront, so you will hand over £40 to £70 on day one rather than £1.50. And renewal is frequently much more expensive than the introductory deal, so put a reminder in your calendar before it auto-renews. That single reminder is worth more than choosing between providers.

5. Free VPNs

Mostly avoid them. Running servers costs money, so a free service is being paid another way, usually by logging what you do and selling it. That is the exact opposite of what you came for, and it is not a hypothetical concern.

The exception is Proton VPN's free tier, which is genuinely free, run by a company whose paid product is the business model, with no data selling. It is slower and has fewer locations, but for occasional cafe wifi use it is enough. If your need is "I use hotel wifi four times a year", start there and spend nothing.

6. Do you actually need one?

You do not want your broadband provider logging every siteBuy one. Legitimate, and a personal call
You run a business needing a stable location for suppliers or clientsBuy one, and it is a deductible business expense
You only browse at home on your own connectionSkip it. That £30 a year is better spent elsewhere
You want protection from viruses or scamsSkip it. Wrong tool. It does not do that

If you skip it, there are cheaper habits worth changing first, such as the daily coffee that costs around £900 a year.

If you run a side hustle you may need one for a real reason: some suppliers, marketplaces and payment tools behave differently by country, and freelancers working with overseas clients often need a stable location. That is a business expense, and business expenses reduce your taxable profit, which is covered in our side hustle tax guide.

7. How to buy without overpaying

  1. Pick the two year plan, never the monthly one, once you are sure you want it.
  2. Use the money back window, usually 30 days, to test it properly on your own devices and your own connection. Speed varies enormously by provider and location.
  3. Set a calendar reminder a week before renewal. The second year rarely costs what the first did, and this is where providers make their margin.
  4. Check the device limit if you want it on a phone, a laptop and a TV. Most cover several, some do not.
  5. Ignore the countdown timers. The deal is always on. A permanent sale is just the price.

The honest verdict

Buy one if you use public wifi regularly, travel, or genuinely object to your broadband provider logging your browsing. On a two year plan that is roughly £1.50 to £3 a month, which is fair for what it does.

Skip it if you browse at home on your own connection and are not bothered who knows which sites you visit, and skip it entirely if you were buying it to stop viruses or protect your banking, because it does neither.

Whatever you decide, never buy a single month, and diarise the renewal on the day you sign up. Those two habits are worth more than the choice of provider.

Frequently asked

Will a VPN slow my internet down? Somewhat, because traffic takes a longer route and is encrypted. On a decent provider and a nearby server the difference is usually small. On a free tier it can be severe.

Can I use one to get cheaper flights or subscriptions? Sometimes prices differ by country, and paying in another region often breaches the service's terms and can require a payment method registered there. Treat it as unreliable rather than a strategy.

Does a VPN hide me from my employer on a work laptop? No. Assume anything on a work device is visible to your employer, regardless of what you install. Use your own device for anything personal.

What is a no-logs policy worth? Only as much as the audit behind it. Several providers publish independent audits of their logging claims, and that is the thing to look for rather than the claim itself.

Do I need one on my phone as well? If the reason you bought it is public wifi, the phone is where you use public wifi most. Yes.
