Flipping Retro Games in the UK: Where the Real Money Is

21 July 2026 · 6 min read · Checked July 2026

The short version
  • The money is not in the famous rare games. It is in boring complete boxed titles that sell steadily at £15 to £40 and that nobody writes YouTube videos about.
  • Complete in box roughly doubles the value of a cartridge or disc alone. The box and manual are usually why a listing sells at all.
  • Buying at UK car boots and charity shops still works. Buying on eBay to resell on eBay almost never does, because the price is already the market price.
  • This is trading, not decluttering. Past the £1,000 trading allowance you must register for Self Assessment.
  • Counterfeit cartridges are widespread, especially Game Boy and DS. Learning to spot them is the single most valuable skill in this hobby.

1. Where stock actually comes from

The one repeatable eBay angle is badly titled listings. Search misspellings, "job lot", "bundle", "untested", and listings with no photographs of the box. Those are underpriced because nobody finds them.

Job lots are where the real margin is. A box of forty mixed games at £60 usually contains three or four you can sell for £25 to £40 each, and the rest bundles out. That is the actual business model.

2. What sells, and what sits

Forget the headline rarities. You will not find a sealed copy of anything valuable at a car boot, and if you do, you will be competing with people who know far more than you.

The steady middle is where the money is:

  • Complete boxed PAL games for PS1, PS2, N64, GameCube, Mega Drive and SNES, in the £15 to £40 range
  • Original controllers, in working order. Genuinely scarce, always in demand, and people pay well
  • Official power supplies, AV leads and memory cards. Deeply unglamorous, consistently profitable
  • Handhelds that work, especially with the original box
  • Strategy guides and manuals, which people buy to complete a game they already own

What sits and does not sell: sports titles from any year, loose discs with heavy scratching, mass produced PS2 titles that sold millions, anything with water damage, and consoles missing their leads.

The pattern is simple. Print run and completeness drive value far more than nostalgia does. A game everyone loved that sold ten million copies is worth very little.

3. Spotting fakes and reproductions

This is the skill that separates people who make money from people who lose it. Counterfeit cartridges are widespread, and selling one unknowingly still lands the dispute on you.

Cartridges, Game Boy, NES, SNES, N64, DS:

  • Weight and feel. Genuine cartridges are heavier and the plastic has a specific density. Once you have handled a few real ones, fakes feel wrong immediately.
  • The screw. Nintendo used a proprietary tri-wing or 3.8mm security bit. A standard Phillips screw is a very strong signal.
  • Label quality. Look for pixelation, a slightly wrong colour, a shiny finish where the original was matte, or a label that sits proud of the recess.
  • The board inside. If you can open it, genuine boards have proper manufacturer markings and a battery holder that is soldered, not glued.
  • Save function. Many reproductions cannot save, or lose the save on power off.
  • Region text and rating logos. Fakes frequently mix a PAL label with NTSC board text.

Discs: check the inner ring text and the label print quality. A colour laser printed disc face is a reproduction.

If you are not sure, do not sell it as genuine. List it honestly as unverified, or do not list it. Selling a counterfeit, even unknowingly, is a Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations problem as well as an eBay account problem.

4. Condition, cleaning and what not to do

Cleaning adds value. Over cleaning destroys it.

Safe and worth doing:

  • Isopropyl alcohol on a cotton bud for cartridge contacts. This fixes most "does not work" cartridges and is the single highest return five minutes in the hobby
  • A barely damp microfibre on plastic shells
  • A soft brush for dust in vents and seams
  • Disc resurfacing for light scratches

Do not do:

  • Do not clean labels with anything wet. You will lift the print and halve the value in one wipe
  • Do not use abrasive cleaners or magic sponges on shells. They dull the texture permanently
  • Do not repaint or retrobright unless you know what you are doing. Buyers can tell, and it is treated as damage
  • Do not throw away a battered box. A tatty box is worth far more than no box

Test everything before listing. "Untested" costs you 30 to 50% of the price, and testing takes two minutes if you have the console set up.

Dan in Leeds does four car boots and two collections in a month.

Gross margin £423. Then the costs nobody includes:

  • Postage and packaging, about £76
  • Fuel to six locations, about £45
  • eBay private seller fees, £0
  • Time: roughly 26 hours including buying, testing, cleaning, listing and posting

Net about £302, before tax, for 26 hours. That is £11.60 an hour. Below the money you would make doing almost any paid weekend work.

The honest reading: this is a hobby that pays for itself and a bit more. People who genuinely enjoy the hunt do very well out of it because the time does not feel like work. People doing it purely for money usually stop within a year, having discovered the hourly rate.

6. Pricing properly

  • Use eBay sold listings, not active ones. Active listings are what people hope to get. Sold listings are the market.
  • Match the exact condition. Complete in box, cartridge only and sealed are three completely different markets for the same title.
  • Check region. PAL and NTSC prices diverge sharply, and a US price tells you nothing about a UK sale.
  • Auction for anything genuinely scarce, Buy It Now for the steady middle. Auctions on common titles finish at the low end.
  • Price at the median of recent solds, not the peak. The one that went for £90 had a bidding war. The other eleven went for £38.

7. Tax, because this is trading

Important distinction from a house clearout. Buying goods with the intention of reselling them is trading. It is taxable from the first pound above the allowance.

  • £1,000 trading allowance per tax year across all self employed income. Above it, register for Self Assessment by 5 October following the end of that tax year.
  • You can deduct genuine costs: stock, postage, packaging, fuel to buy stock, eBay fees, a proportion of storage.
  • Keep a simple record from day one. What you paid, what it sold for, what postage cost. A spreadsheet is enough and it turns tax time into ten minutes.
  • Platforms report seller data to HMRC above 30 sales or about £1,700 a year. If you are trading, that report will match a return you have already filed, which is the position you want to be in.

Myths that cost people money

"I might find something worth thousands." You almost certainly will not, and building a business plan on a lottery ticket is why people overpay for job lots hoping there is a gem inside. Price every lot on what you can definitely sell, not what might be in it.

"Graded games are the future." Grading costs money and time, and the graded market has been volatile. For the £15 to £40 middle it makes no sense at all.

"Buy now, prices only go up." Retro prices spiked sharply in 2020 and 2021 and a lot of that came back down. Treating stock as an investment rather than inventory is how people end up with a spare room full of capital they cannot release.

The honest verdict

This works if you genuinely enjoy the hunt, you can get to car boots early, and you have somewhere to store stock. At around £300 net a month for weekend work it pays for itself and funds the collection.

This does not work if you want a reliable hourly rate. At roughly £11 to £12 an hour once you count buying, testing, cleaning, listing and posting, almost any paid weekend work pays better. That is the number nobody puts in the video.

Start with £50, one car boot, and a rule that you buy nothing you cannot price on sold listings while standing there.

If it is the reselling you enjoy rather than the games specifically, selling what you already own is a better first step, because there is no stock cost and no tax question.

Frequently asked

Which console is best to start with? Whichever you know. Product knowledge is the whole edge. If you grew up with a Mega Drive, start there and expand once you can price from memory.

Do I need to test everything? Yes, and it pays for itself. Keep one working console per format you deal in.

Is it worth cleaning contacts on cheap games? Yes. Isopropyl and a cotton bud turns a £2 faulty cartridge into a £15 working one in five minutes.

How do I store stock? Dry, out of direct sunlight, boxes upright not stacked flat. Sun fades spines and damp destroys cardboard, and the box is often most of the value.
