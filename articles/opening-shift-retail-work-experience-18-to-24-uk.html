Retail Says It Will Create 100,000 Jobs for Young People. The Thing Being Launched Today Is 11,000 Unpaid Placements

Two numbers went out together on 8 September and they are not the same kind of thing. One is a trade association ambition stretching to the end of the Parliament. The other is a specific programme with 11,000 places, a named age range and a defined length. Confusing them is easy, and it will waste somebody's autumn.

The short version

  • Opening Shift is the actual programme. More than 11,000 work experience placements with major retailers, announced 8 September 2026.
  • It is 18 to 24 only. If you are 17, none of this is open to you.
  • The shape: two to four weeks, at 16 to 35 hours a week, with workplace mentoring, interview experience and travel cost assistance where needed.
  • It is voluntary and routed through Jobcentres. DWP manages the pipeline of eligible young Universal Credit customers who get referred.
  • The 100,000 is different. That is the retail sector's own target, via the British Retail Consortium, to deliver 100,000 jobs for young people not in education, employment or training by the end of this Parliament. It is an ambition, not an offer you can apply to.
  • The genuinely paid route is the Jobs Guarantee: six months of fully funded paid employment for 18 to 24 year olds, after 18 months of claiming and searching. Over 90,000 supported over three years.

1. Two numbers, doing two different jobs

On 8 September 2026 DWP announced a partnership with the British Retail Consortium. The coverage led on 100,000 jobs. Here is what each number is.

The 100,000 is a target set by the retail sector, through its trade association, to deliver 100,000 jobs for young people who are not in employment, education or training by the end of this Parliament. It is an ambition announced by an industry body. There is no form to fill in for it. It is not a commitment by any individual employer to hire you.

Opening Shift is the thing that actually launched. It is a work experience programme, run by DWP and the BRC together, offering more than 11,000 placements.

Both are real. Only one of them is a door you can walk through this autumn, and it is the smaller number.

The retailers involved in developing and rolling out Opening Shift are named, and the list is long: Aldi, Amazon, Asda, B&M, B&Q, Boots, Currys, Dunelm, Holland & Barrett, Iceland, JD Sports, John Lewis Partnership, Lidl, Marks & Spencer, Morrisons, New Look, Next, Pets at Home, Pret, Primark, Screwfix, Superdrug, Tesco, Wickes and others.

2. What Opening Shift actually is

Who: 18 to 24 year olds who are not in employment, education or training.

How long: two to four weeks.

How many hours: 16 to 35 hours a week.

How you get on it: DWP manages the pipeline of eligible young Universal Credit customers, who are referred to the programme through Jobcentres.

What comes with it: ongoing Universal Credit support, travel cost assistance where needed, workplace mentoring and interview experience.

What happens at the end: three possibilities are named. A permanent role if one is available, a place on the retailer's talent bank, or being directed towards apprenticeships and other opportunities.

Note the wording on that last point carefully. "Could be offered a permanent role if one is available." That is a possibility, conditional on a vacancy existing. It is not a guarantee and the announcement does not present it as one.

The programme is described as voluntary, and the referral route runs through Jobcentres rather than through an open application on a retailer's website. That means the practical first step is a conversation with a work coach, not a job board.

3. What it pays, and the honest answer about that

The announcement does not say Opening Shift placements are paid a wage. It says participants get ongoing Universal Credit support and travel cost assistance where needed. Those are the two financial elements named.

We are not going to dress that up. This is work experience on your existing benefit, with travel covered, not a paid job. If it were paid at a wage the announcement would say so, and elsewhere in the same release the government is perfectly happy to describe the Jobs Guarantee as "fully funded paid employment", which shows they use that language when it applies.

For the avoidance of doubt about what a wage would look like if you were employed instead, the National Minimum Wage and National Living Wage rates that apply from April 2026 are £12.71 an hour for 21 and over, £10.85 for 18 to 20, and £8.00 for under 18s and apprentices. Those are the rates against which to judge any offer that follows a placement.

Whether an unpaid placement is worth doing depends entirely on what it leads to, which is the calculation in the worked example below rather than a matter of principle.

4. Worked example: Callum, 19, in Bolton

Callum is 19, on Universal Credit, and has never had a job. His work coach offers him a four week Opening Shift placement at 30 hours a week with a supermarket.

What he gets in cash during it: his Universal Credit continues, and travel is assisted where needed. No wage is named in the announcement, so plan on none.

What 30 hours a week for four weeks is worth if it were paid at the 18 to 20 rate of £10.85: 30 x £10.85 = £325.50 a week, x 4 = £1,302 gross. That is the opportunity cost of the four weeks, and it is only a real cost if he had a paid alternative. With no work history and no experience, he mostly does not, which is the entire problem the programme exists to address.

What he is actually buying with those four weeks: a reference, 120 hours of demonstrable attendance, interview experience, and a named contact inside a large employer. For a first-time jobseeker those are the missing items, not the wage.

The break-even is short. If the placement leads to a permanent 30 hour role at £10.85, he recovers the notional £1,302 in four weeks of work. If it leads to nothing at all, he has spent four weeks and gained a reference.

The question worth asking his work coach is not about the placement, it is about the exit. Specifically: is there a live vacancy at this store, or am I going onto a talent bank. Those two answers lead to very different months.

5. The routes that pay actual wages

If you are 18 to 24 and the priority is money rather than experience, these are named in the same announcement and they are different things.

The Jobs Guarantee. This is the paid one. Six months of fully funded paid employment, matched to skills, for Universal Credit claimants aged 18 to 24 after 18 months of claiming and searching for work. Over 90,000 young people over three years, backed by more than 400 employers, with first cohorts already started at Boots and Lidl and a national rollout later this year. The 18 month qualifying period is the catch and it is a long one.

The £3,000 Youth Jobs Grant and a £2,000 apprenticeship hiring payment for employers, both part of the £2.5 billion the government is putting into the Youth Guarantee and Growth and Skills Levy by 2029. The apprenticeship payment goes to the employer, not to you, which is worth knowing when you are negotiating.

An apprenticeship. The apprentice rate is £8.00 an hour from April 2026, which is low, but it is a wage and it comes with a qualification. For a lot of 18 to 24 year olds this is a better use of a year than a sequence of placements.

Sainsbury's has separately committed to around 10,000 work experience and employability opportunities for young people from October, and McDonald's runs what the government describes as the largest paid in-person work experience programme in the UK. If paid experience is what you want, that word is the one to search for.

The honest verdict

Opening Shift is a reasonable thing that has been announced behind a much bigger number, and the gap between the two is where people will get confused. Eleven thousand placements is not one hundred thousand jobs, and a sector ambition running to the end of a Parliament is not something you can apply to in September.

Taken on its own terms the programme is fine. Two to four weeks, a mentor, travel covered, and a reference at the end, aimed at exactly the people who cannot get a first job because they have never had one. The BRC's own phrase for the trap, a no experience no job doom loop, is accurate. This is a credible attempt at it.

But be clear-eyed about the money. It is work experience on Universal Credit, not a wage, and the announcement does not claim otherwise. If you are 18 to 24 and need income rather than a CV line, the Jobs Guarantee is the paid route, and an apprenticeship at £8.00 an hour is a wage plus a qualification. Ask your work coach about all three in the same conversation rather than taking the first thing offered.

And if you are 17: none of this is for you yet. Come back at 18.

Frequently asked

Am I eligible at 16 or 17? No. Opening Shift is 18 to 24. The Jobs Guarantee is also 18 to 24. At 16 or 17 the realistic routes are an apprenticeship, a traineeship or staying in education, and the apprentice minimum wage from April 2026 is £8.00 an hour.

Is the placement paid? The announcement names ongoing Universal Credit support and travel cost assistance where needed. It does not describe a wage. Elsewhere in the same release the government describes the Jobs Guarantee as fully funded paid employment, so the different wording is deliberate.

Can I apply directly to Tesco or John Lewis for a placement? The route described is a referral through Jobcentres, with DWP managing the pipeline of eligible young Universal Credit customers. Speak to your work coach rather than applying to the retailer for Opening Shift specifically. Ordinary vacancies at those retailers are of course still open to apply for in the normal way.

What is the difference between the Jobs Guarantee and Opening Shift? The Jobs Guarantee is six months of fully funded paid employment after 18 months of claiming. Opening Shift is two to four weeks of work experience with no wage named. Different length, different money, different qualifying period.
