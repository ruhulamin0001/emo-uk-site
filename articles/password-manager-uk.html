Password Managers: The Free One Is Probably Enough

22 August 2026 · 8 min read · Checked August 2026

The short version
  • For most households the free tier is genuinely enough. The paid features are mostly convenience, not security.
  • The one that is already on your phone and laptop, built into the operating system or browser, is better than the excellent one you never set up.
  • The real win is not strong passwords. It is never reusing one, because reuse is what turns one company's breach into your problem.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication on your email first. Email is the master key to everything else.
  • Whatever you choose, write down the master password and the recovery code and put them somewhere physical.

Why this matters more than it sounds

The threat most people imagine is someone guessing their password. That is not what happens.

What actually happens is credential stuffing. A company you signed up to years ago gets breached. Your email address and password end up in a list. Software then tries that pair automatically against hundreds of other sites, at scale, cheaply. If you reused the password, the attacker is now in your email, and from your email they can reset everything else.

That is why the single most valuable change is not password strength. It is uniqueness. A different password everywhere means one breach stays one breach. A password manager is simply the only practical way to have a hundred different passwords and still be able to log in.

Check whether your address is already in known breaches. The service Have I Been Pwned does this free and it is run by a well-regarded security researcher rather than a company trying to sell you anything. Most people find at least one.

Open-source options are worth a look if you care about being able to verify what the software does, and some allow you to host the vault yourself. That is a real advantage for the technically minded and an unnecessary complication for everyone else.

When paying is worth it

Paying makes sense in four situations, and not really otherwise:

  1. The free tier will not sync between your phone and your computer. If it does not sync, you will not use it, and an unused password manager provides nothing.
  2. You share logins with a partner or family. Family plans are the best value in this category by a distance, and the alternative is passwords in a WhatsApp message.
  3. You want emergency access. If something happened to you, could anyone get into your accounts? This is the feature people dismiss and then wish they had.
  4. You run a business. Shared team vaults with per-person access and revocation are worth paying for, and a spreadsheet of passwords is not an acceptable substitute.

What is not a good reason to pay: encrypted storage you will never use, a dark web report that tells you what a free breach checker already would, or a VPN bundled in that you did not need.

The built-in option you already have

Every major operating system and browser now includes a password manager, free, with syncing across that ecosystem. If your household is all one platform, the built-in option is a completely reasonable answer and involves installing nothing.

Its real weakness is cross-platform use. An iPhone with a Windows laptop, or an Android with a Mac, is where the built-in options get awkward, and awkward means abandoned. Mixed households are the strongest case for a dedicated manager.

One caution that applies to browser-stored passwords specifically: if someone has unlocked access to your computer, browser-saved passwords are typically easy to view. That is a reason to have a device password and not to leave a laptop unlocked, rather than a reason to avoid the feature.

What happens when the manager itself is breached

This is the obvious objection and it deserves a straight answer rather than reassurance.

Reputable password managers use zero-knowledge encryption: your vault is encrypted and decrypted on your device with a key derived from your master password, and the provider never holds that key. If their servers are breached, attackers get encrypted blobs.

The honest caveat is that this protection is only as strong as your master password. If a vault is stolen and the master password is short or reused, it can be attacked offline at leisure. This is not hypothetical. It has happened to a major provider, and the users with weak master passwords were the ones who suffered.

Which gives the rule: the master password is the one you make genuinely long. Four or five random words is easier to remember and harder to attack than a short string of symbols. Never reuse it anywhere else.

A one-hour setup that actually holds

Do not try to migrate two hundred accounts on day one. You will stop halfway and abandon it.

  1. Pick one manager. Built-in if you are on one platform, a cross-platform one if you are not. The choice matters far less than finishing.
  2. Set a long master password. Several random words. Write it on paper.
  3. Save the recovery code somewhere physical, with the master password. A drawer at home is a perfectly good threat model for most people.
  4. Fix email first. New unique password, and two-factor turned on. Email is the recovery route for everything else, so it is the account that matters most.
  5. Then the money accounts. Bank, PayPal, anything with a card stored.
  6. Then let it happen naturally. Every time you log into something over the next month, change that password and save it. Within six weeks the important ones are all done, with no marathon session.
  7. Run the health check the manager offers. It will find reused and breached passwords. Fix the reused ones first.

Two-factor, passkeys and where this is going

Two-factor authentication matters more than password strength on your most important accounts. In rough order of security: a hardware key, then an authenticator app, then SMS. SMS is the weakest because of SIM-swap fraud, but SMS two-factor is still far better than none.

Passkeys are the direction of travel. They replace the password with a cryptographic key stored on your device and unlocked by your fingerprint, face or PIN. There is nothing to type, nothing to reuse, and nothing phishable, which removes the most common attack entirely.

Adoption is uneven. Many large services support passkeys now, plenty do not, and recovery when you lose the device is still the rough edge. Use passkeys where they are offered, keep the password manager for everywhere else, and expect the balance to shift over the next few years.

Questions people ask

Is a notebook in a drawer really that bad? For a home user with no visitors, a written book of passwords is a defensible choice, and it defeats remote attackers entirely. The problems are that it does not help you generate strong passwords, it is useless when you are out, and it is a single point of failure in a fire. A manager plus a written master password gets the best of both.

What if the company shuts down? Every reputable manager lets you export your vault. Do it once a year, keep the export somewhere encrypted, and delete old ones.

Should I change all my passwords regularly? No. Forced regular changes are outdated advice and lead to weaker, patterned passwords. Change a password when there is a reason: a breach, a suspicion, or a shared account someone has left.

Can I trust autofill? Yes, and it has a security benefit people miss. A manager will not autofill on the wrong domain, so if it refuses to fill, that is a strong hint the site is a phishing copy.

The verdict

Use whichever one you will actually use, and in most cases that is the free tier or the one already built into your devices. Make the master password long, turn on two-factor for your email today, and stop reusing passwords. That is the whole of it, and the paid upgrade is a convenience decision rather than a safety one.
