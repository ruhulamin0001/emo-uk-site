A Hot Tub Costs About £990 A Year In Electricity

This is the largest single running cost most British households will ever add to their home on purpose. It is also one of the few where a £350 decision can halve the bill, and almost nobody makes that decision because they are looking at the wrong number on the brochure.

The short version

  • The heater's kW rating tells you nothing. A 3 kW heater reaching temperature quickly and a 2 kW heater taking longer use almost the same energy, because both are replacing the same heat loss.
  • A four person tub held at 38 degrees loses about 13 watts per degree. Across a British year that is roughly 3,763 kWh, or £990.42 at the October 2026 cap rate.

1. Why the heater's kW rating is the wrong number

Walk into a hot tub showroom and you will be told about the heater. Hard shell tubs in the UK are typically hardwired with a 3 kW heater, most hard shell models sit somewhere between 2 and 4 kW, plug and play tubs use 1 to 2 kW to stay inside a 13 amp socket, and inflatables are usually 1.8 to 2.4 kW.

None of that predicts your bill, and the industry's own guidance says so: a heater with a bigger kW rating heats the water faster but does not need to stay on as long. The energy you pay for is not set by how powerful the heater is. It is set by how fast heat escapes from the tub, and how long you keep it at temperature.

This is exactly the same logic as a house. Nobody asks how many kilowatts your boiler is when estimating your gas bill; they ask about insulation and thermostat setting. A hot tub is a very small, very hot, very badly insulated house that you keep at 38 degrees all year, which is why it costs what it does.

A note on how firm these numbers are. Reputable dealers will quote consumption as kWh per day at 38 degrees in a 10 degree ambient, and that is the figure to ask for in writing. We could not obtain a published set of cabinet and cover U-values from a manufacturer, so section two builds the model from conventional construction values and then checks the result against the published industry range. Treat the model as a well grounded estimate, not a specification.

2. The heat loss model, and what a year actually costs

Take a common four person tub: roughly 2 metres square, holding about 1,000 litres, with an insulated cabinet and a foam cover.

  • Cover, 4 m² at a U-value of 1.0: 4.00 W/K
  • Cabinet sides, 7.2 m² at 0.8: 5.76 W/K
  • Base, 4 m² at 0.8: 3.20 W/K
  • Total: 12.96 watts per degree

The water sits at 38 degrees. The UK annual mean air temperature is around 10 degrees, so the average temperature difference across a year is 28 degrees.

12.96 × 28 = 363 watts, continuously, every hour of every day. That is 8.71 kWh a day just standing still. Add a circulation pump drawing about 200 watts for eight hours a day to keep the water filtered, which is another 1.6 kWh, and you get 10.31 kWh a day.

Across a year: 3,763 kWh. At the Ofgem price cap electricity rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 26.32p per kWh, that is £990.42 a year, or £82.54 a month.

Sanity check: the published industry range for UK hot tub running costs is roughly £40 to £85 a month. Our model lands at the top of that range, which is what you would expect for a tub held at temperature all year round rather than shut down over winter.

For scale, Ofgem's price cap for the same period sets the typical household dual fuel bill at £1,723 a year. The hot tub alone is 57 per cent of that. It is comfortably more than heating a garden office at £133.76, more than a second fridge freezer, and more than most households spend on every other appliance combined.

Two rows in that table are worth more than everything else on this page. The cover row, which we come to next, and the heat pump row.

A hot tub air source heat pump moves roughly four and a half units of heat per unit of electricity instead of one. On this model that takes the bill from £990 to £340, a saving of £650.79 a year. Installed cost is typically in the region of £2,000 to £2,500, so the payback is about 3.4 years on a tub that will last fifteen. If you already own a tub and use it year round, this is the single best thing you can do with two thousand pounds, and it is the same physics that makes a domestic heat pump worth considering for the house.

Note also what the seasonal rows say. Shutting down for the winter saves £413, but it also removes the months when a hot tub is most enjoyable, which is a strange trade to make. Turning the temperature down three degrees saves £89 and most people would not notice.

4. The cover: an eight month payback hiding in plain sight

The cover is the top of the tub, so it is the surface with the largest temperature difference across it and the one heat most wants to leave through. It is also the component that fails soonest and most invisibly.

A hot tub cover is foam in a vinyl skin. Over three or four years the vinyl develops small splits, the foam absorbs water, and a waterlogged foam core conducts heat far better than a dry one. The cover gets noticeably heavier, which everybody notices, and its insulation collapses, which nobody notices.

Model a tired cover at a U-value of 3.0 instead of 1.0. Total heat loss goes from 12.96 to 20.96 watts per degree, an increase of 8.0. At the same 28 degree average difference that is an extra 224 watts continuously, which is 5.38 kWh a day and 1,962 kWh a year.

At 26.32p per kWh, a knackered cover is costing you £516.40 a year.

Here is the payback, in the standard shape used throughout this series:

£350 for a replacement cover, divided by £516.40 saved a year, is 0.68 years, which is about eight months.

That is faster than the towel rail timer, faster than draught proofing, faster than anything else in this batch. And the test for whether you need one takes ten seconds: try to lift your cover with one hand. If you cannot, and you could when it was new, the foam is waterlogged and it is costing you about £43 a month.

Two cheap things that extend a cover's life considerably: a floating thermal blanket laid directly on the water under the cover, which stops steam reaching the foam, and keeping the cover's underside dry by propping it open for a few minutes after use rather than slamming it straight back on.

5. Worked example: the Okonkwos in Peterborough

The Okonkwos bought a five seat hard shell tub four years ago for £7,200. They use it, honestly counted, about twice a week, so a hundred soaks a year.

Their electricity bill went up by an amount they had never separated out, so they put a clamp meter on the tub's supply for a fortnight in March and found it averaging 14.8 kWh a day. At 26.32p that is £1,422 a year, a long way above the £990 base case.

The difference is the cover. Theirs is four years old and takes two people to lift. They replace it for £340, re-meter in April, and the figure drops to 10.6 kWh a day, or £1,018 a year.

Saving £404 a year for £340. Payback: 0.84 years, about ten months. Slightly slower than the model predicts, because April is milder than March, but the same order of magnitude and entirely real.

Then they do the total cost of ownership sum, which is the one that matters. Over ten years: £7,200 for the tub, about £9,900 of electricity, roughly £1,800 in chemicals and filters, and four water changes a year at around £17 on a metered supply, call it £680. Total £19,580 for a thousand soaks.

£19.58 per soak. Two of them going in together, twice a week, for a decade. They decide that is worth it, which is a reasonable conclusion and a much better informed one than the "it is only a few pounds a week" they were told in the showroom.

6. The honest verdict

If you already own a hot tub, go and lift your cover with one hand right now. If you cannot, a £350 replacement is saving you around £500 a year and paying for itself before next summer. That is the single most valuable sentence on this page.

If you own one and use it year round, price an air source heat pump. Around £2,000 to £2,500 fitted against a saving of roughly £650 a year is a payback of about three and a half years on a fifteen year appliance.

If you are thinking of buying one, budget £990 a year for electricity and about £180 for chemicals, and do not believe a lower figure unless it comes with a stated kWh per day at 38 degrees in a 10 degree ambient. Ask for that in writing. A dealer who will not give it to you is telling you something.

And do the per soak sum before you buy, not after. Around £19 a soak at twice a week is the honest price. If that sounds fine, buy it and enjoy it. If it makes you wince, the local spa does day passes.

If you are buying

The two specifications that decide your bill are the cover's insulation value and thickness, and whether the cabinet is fully foam filled or just a panelled box with a gap. Ask about both and ignore the heater's kW rating entirely. A thermal floating blanket is a £30 item that protects a £350 one.

Frequently asked

Is it cheaper to turn it off between uses? Almost never, for a hard shell tub. Reheating 1,000 litres from cold to 38 degrees takes around 30 kWh, or roughly £7.90, and takes most of a day. If you use the tub twice a week, keeping it warm costs about £19 a week while switching it off and reheating costs about £16 a week plus the inconvenience of planning every soak a day ahead. The margin is small enough that convenience wins. If you use it once a fortnight, switching off does win, and you should probably ask whether you want a hot tub.

Are inflatable tubs cheaper to run? Per hour of use, usually not. They have thinner insulation and a much poorer cover, so they lose heat faster, and their 1.8 to 2.4 kW heaters struggle to keep up in winter. They are dramatically cheaper to buy and you can put them away for six months of the year, which is where the real saving is. If you only want a tub in summer, an inflatable is the rational choice.

Does the number of jets affect the bill? Running the jets uses a lot of power for the short time they are on, typically 1.5 to 3 kW, but an hour of jets a week is only about £10 a year. What jets really cost you is heat: agitated water with air blown through it loses heat far faster than still water, so a long session with the blower running cools the tub significantly and the heater then works to put that back.
