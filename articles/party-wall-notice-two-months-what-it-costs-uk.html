The Part of Your Extension That Takes Two Months and Costs Nothing to Start

Most people worry about the party wall surveyor's fee. The expensive part is the statutory notice period, which runs to two months for a party structure notice and cannot be shortened by paying anyone. Serving it on the right day is free. Serving it late costs a season.

The short version

  • A party structure notice under section 3 of the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 needs at least two months before work starts. An adjacent excavation notice under section 6 needs at least one month.
  • Serving the notice costs nothing. You can write and serve it yourself. The fees only start if your neighbour dissents and surveyors are appointed.
  • Silence is dissent. If your neighbour does not reply in writing within 14 days, a dispute is deemed to have arisen and the surveyor process begins whether anyone wanted it or not.
  • Two months of a hired scaffold, a booked builder and a rented skip is the real number, and on a mid-sized extension that is easily four figures of dead cost.
  • The Act is not planning permission and not building regulations. Having both does not remove the party wall duty, and neither does a friendly neighbour.

1. What the Act actually catches

The Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies across England and Wales, and it catches three separate categories of work. Most people only know about the first.

Section 1, a new wall at the line of junction. Building a wall on or up to the boundary where there is not one now.

Section 2 and 3, works to an existing party structure. Cutting into it to take a beam, removing a chimney breast, raising it, underpinning it, demolishing and rebuilding it, or cutting away a projection. Anything structural to the wall you share.

Section 6, excavation near a neighbour's building. This is the one that surprises people. If you dig within three metres of a neighbouring building and go deeper than their foundations, or within six metres and go below a line struck at 45 degrees down from the bottom of their foundations, you must serve notice. That covers a very ordinary rear extension with ordinary trench foundations, on a street where the houses sit close together.

Planning permission and building regulations approval are separate systems. Having both, or needing neither because the work is permitted development, does not touch your party wall duties at all. Nor does a good relationship with your neighbour: the duty is statutory, and if you skip it your neighbour can seek an injunction that stops the job dead.

2. The three notice periods, and when the clock starts

These come straight from the Act and cannot be shortened by agreement with a surveyor, by paying more, or by the work being urgent.

  • Section 1, line of junction notice: at least one month before work begins.
  • Section 3, party structure notice: at least two months before work begins.
  • Section 6, adjacent excavation notice: at least one month before excavation begins.

The only way to start sooner is for the adjoining owner to consent in writing and then waive the remainder of the period, which they are entitled to do but are under no obligation to do. Plan on the full period and treat an early start as a bonus.

There is a second clock inside the first. Once served, your neighbour has 14 days to respond. If they consent in writing within those 14 days, that is the end of it: no surveyors, no fees, and you start when the notice period expires. If they do not respond at all, the Act deems a dispute to have arisen.

A notice also has a shelf life. A party structure notice ceases to have effect if the work does not begin within 12 months of the notice being served, so serving it two years ahead of a job that keeps slipping achieves nothing.

3. Why silence is the expensive answer

This is the single most useful thing to understand about the Act, and it is counterintuitive. A neighbour who ignores your letter is not being neutral. Under the Act, failure to consent within 14 days is treated as dissent, and once a dispute is deemed to exist, surveyors must be appointed and an award drawn up.

From there, two routes. An agreed surveyor acts for both parties: one appointment, one fee, and the building owner normally pays it. Or each side appoints their own surveyor, and the building owner normally pays both, plus a third surveyor's costs if the two cannot agree.

That is why the practical advice is always the same. Talk to your neighbour before the letter arrives. A neighbour who has been told what is happening, shown a drawing, and given a start date is far more likely to write "I consent" on a form. A neighbour who opens a formal legal notice out of nowhere is far more likely to put it in a drawer, which is legally identical to objecting.

4. What an award actually contains, and why you want one

Landlords and homeowners tend to treat the award as a bill. It is better understood as the only piece of evidence that will exist if something cracks next door in eighteen months.

A party wall award normally contains four things. The works, described precisely, so that nobody can later argue about what was agreed. The working hours and access arrangements. A schedule of condition, which is a photographic record of the adjoining property before anything starts. And the allocation of costs.

The schedule of condition is the valuable part. Old houses have old cracks. Without a dated photographic record, a hairline crack that has been in a neighbour's plaster since 1987 becomes, the moment your digger arrives, a crack your digger caused. With a schedule, the argument ends in thirty seconds.

That is why the following is worth considering even when your neighbour consents happily and no surveyor is required: commission a schedule of condition anyway. It is a fraction of a full award, typically a few hundred pounds, and it protects you against the one claim that can genuinely run into five figures. A consenting neighbour is not a neighbour who has waived their right to complain about damage; the Act still requires you to make good.

The award is also enforceable. Either party can appeal it to the county court within 14 days of it being served, and after that it binds both sides.

Worked example: the Okonkwo household, Walthamstow

Rear extension on a Victorian terrace, 4 metres out, trench-fill foundations 900mm deep. The neighbouring house sits directly against the boundary, so this is a section 6 excavation notice, minimum one month, and because a steel goes into the party wall it is also a section 3 party structure notice, minimum two months. The longer period governs.

They planned to start in early March. They served notice on 1 February, which made the earliest lawful start 1 April, four weeks later than the builder's slot. The builder moved them to his next gap, which was June.

What the delay cost. Nothing in fees, because the neighbour consented in writing on day nine. But the quote was re-priced after the spring materials increase, and the job ran into a wetter period, adding roughly £1,100 between the two.

What serving in December would have cost. Also nothing. The notice is a letter. Two months earlier, on the same free piece of paper, and the March slot holds.

The lesson is that the only genuinely expensive decision in this whole process was deciding when to post a letter.

6. The honest verdict

Serve the notice the day your drawings are firm enough to describe the work, not the day your builder confirms a start date. It is free, it is a letter, and it is the only part of the process with a clock that nobody can accelerate.

Speak to your neighbour first, in person, before anything formal lands. Written consent within 14 days removes the surveyor fee entirely, and the difference between a conversation and a cold legal notice is usually the difference between consent and silence.

Do not skip it because the work seems small or the neighbour seems relaxed. An injunction stops a site, and a stopped site costs more in a fortnight than every surveyor fee in the table above.

Frequently asked

Does the Party Wall Act apply in Scotland? No. The Party Wall etc. Act 1996 covers England and Wales. Scotland relies on common law and the Tenements (Scotland) Act 2004, and Northern Ireland has no direct equivalent.

Can I serve the notice myself? Yes. There is no requirement to use a surveyor to serve a notice, and doing it yourself costs nothing. The Act sets out what the notice must contain, including your name and address, the nature of the work and the proposed start date.

What if my neighbour refuses to appoint a surveyor? The Act provides for this. If an adjoining owner refuses or fails to appoint within ten days of being asked, the building owner may appoint one on their behalf, and the process continues.

Does a party wall award give me permission to build? No. It records the condition of the neighbouring property, sets out how the work will be done, and resolves the party wall dispute. Planning permission and building regulations are entirely separate.
