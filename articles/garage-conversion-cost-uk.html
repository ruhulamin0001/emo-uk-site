Two Ways To Get One More Room. One Of Them Costs Four Times As Much Per Square Metre

Households that need another room almost always price an extension first. The garage is usually sitting there already, dry, walled and roofed, at roughly a quarter of the cost per square metre. Here is the arithmetic, and the rules that decide whether it is allowed.

The short version

  • Planning permission is not usually required where the work is internal and does not enlarge the building, according to the Planning Portal. Converting into a separate dwelling is a different question and may need permission.
  • Building regulations approval is needed. Floor, walls below ground level, the in fill where the garage door was, ventilation, insulation and fire safety all have to be signed off.
  • Typical cost: 8,000 to 15,000 pounds for a single garage of roughly 15 square metres, which is around 800 pounds per square metre of habitable space created.
  • An extension of the same floor area runs roughly 2,800 to 3,500 pounds per square metre, so the same room costs three to four times as much.
  • The payback depends on what it replaces. A converted garage used instead of a 250 pound a month rented desk or storage unit repays 12,000 pounds in 4 years.
  • Permitted development rights are sometimes removed on new developments, in conservation areas and under Article 4 directions. Check with your local planning authority before spending anything.

1. What the rules actually say

The Planning Portal, which is run as a joint venture with the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, puts it plainly: planning permission is not usually required, providing the work is internal and does not involve enlarging the building.

That is the whole general rule, and it is why a garage conversion is so much less painful than an extension. You are not building anything. You are changing what an existing box is used for.

There are four situations where that general rule stops applying, and all four are worth checking before you spend a pound:

  • Converting into a separate house. If the intention is a self contained dwelling, regardless of who lives in it, permission may be required whatever the work involves. Discuss it with the local planning authority first.
  • Permitted development rights removed. The Planning Portal specifically warns that rights are sometimes withdrawn with regard to garage conversions, particularly on new housing developments and in conservation areas.
  • Article 4 directions and planning conditions. These do the same thing by another route, and they are common on estates where the parking provision was part of the original consent.
  • Listed buildings. Listed building consent may be required separately.

Flats and maisonettes are outside these householder allowances entirely, as are houses created through change of use permitted development rights.

2. Building regulations: the five things that get checked

Planning and building control are different systems and people conflate them constantly. You will almost certainly avoid planning. You will not avoid building regulations, because you are turning a cold store into a room people sit in.

The Planning Portal breaks the technical detail into the areas that matter, and in practice a building control officer will want to see all five:

  • The floor. Garage slabs are usually laid below the house floor level and to a fall, so they need levelling, damp proofing and insulating up to current standards.
  • Walls below ground level. Where the new floor sits above outside ground level, the wall behind it has to be tanked or treated so damp does not track in.
  • The in fill where the door was. The new wall has to carry the existing lintel loading, match thermally, and tie into the original brickwork properly.
  • Ventilation. Habitable rooms need background ventilation and purge ventilation. This is the item most often missed on unapproved conversions.
  • Fire safety and escape. Especially where the room opens into a hallway that is also the escape route.

An unsigned off conversion is not just a regulatory risk. It is a room that your buyer's solicitor will ask about, and an indemnity policy is a worse answer than a completion certificate.

4. Worked example: the Nasir household in Bedford

An integral single garage, 5.2 metres by 2.9 metres, so 15.1 square metres of floor. Used for bicycles, a chest freezer and boxes. Both adults work from home and one of them has been renting a desk in a shared office at 250 pounds a month.

The conversion. Floor levelled, insulated and screeded; door opening filled with a window under the existing lintel; walls insulated and plasterboarded; two double sockets and lighting; radiator extended from the existing system; building control application and inspections. Quoted at 11,800 pounds, plus a 340 pound building control fee. Total 12,140 pounds.

Cost per square metre: 12,140 divided by 15.1 = 804 pounds. The extension quote for the same floor area, on the same house, was 46,500 pounds, or 3,079 pounds per square metre.

The payback. The rented desk was 250 pounds a month, so 3,000 pounds a year. 12,140 divided by 3,000 gives a payback of 4.05 years, after which the room is free and the desk money stops leaving.

Two things that sum does not include, and they point in opposite directions. It ignores any increase in the value of the house, which on a conversion that is properly signed off is usually real but is not money you can spend. And it ignores the heating cost of a room that used to be outside the warm envelope, which on a 15 square metre insulated room in a Bedford semi is a modest but genuine annual number, not zero.

5. The argument against, stated fairly

Conversions have a reputation problem and some of it is deserved. Three honest objections.

You lose the parking, and on some streets that is worth more than the room. Where a driveway is short and on street parking is permit controlled or genuinely scarce, an estate agent will tell you the garage was carrying value you have just spent 12,000 pounds to remove. This is very local and worth one free valuation conversation before committing.

You lose the store, and the store does not disappear, it relocates. Bikes, the freezer and the boxes end up in the hall or in a garden shed you then have to buy. Price the shed into the conversion, not after it.

A cheap conversion reads as a cheap conversion. The tell is always the same one: a window that is obviously the width of a garage door, sitting in a wall that does not match. Paying a bit more for brick matching and a properly proportioned opening is the difference between an extra bedroom and a filled in garage.

If the room you actually need is upstairs rather than down, the loft is the other route and prices very differently, which is worth reading alongside what a bathroom refit really costs if the plan involves moving plumbing at the same time.

Frequently asked

Do I have to keep the garage door? No, and keeping it purely to avoid attention is a common and pointless half measure. A retained door on a converted space causes damp and cold problems and fools nobody, including a surveyor.

Will it add value? Usually yes where habitable floor area is what local buyers are short of, and usually not where off street parking is what they are short of. Ask a local agent the direct question rather than relying on a national average percentage.

How long does it take? A straightforward single garage is commonly three to five weeks on site. Building control inspections happen at stages during that, not at the end, so the work has to be opened up at the right moments.

Can I do it without building regulations approval and sort it later? A regularisation application exists for exactly this and it is more expensive and more intrusive than doing it properly, because the officer may require opening up to see what is behind the plasterboard. It is also the point at which the saving evaporates.

The honest verdict

If you need one more room, the garage is almost always the cheapest square metres you will ever buy, at roughly 800 pounds a metre against 3,000 for an extension. Do that arithmetic before you ring an architect.

Do not do it if the parking is worth more than the room on your street, and find that out with one phone call rather than one regret.

Get building regulations approval. It is a few hundred pounds against a conveyancing problem that arrives years later when you have no leverage and a buyer who does.
