Access to Work: what it actually pays for, and why £6,500 is not the cap you were told about

Access to Work is a grant that helps you get or stay in a job. It is not a benefit, it is not repayable, and it does not touch anything else you claim. The £6,500 that keeps appearing in write-ups is real, but it is not a limit on what you can get.

The short version

  • £6,500 is a minimum annual turnover for self-employed applicants, not a cap on the grant. GOV.UK puts it under the income and benefits heading on the eligibility page, read 6 September 2026.
  • If you are employed, you can get support however much you earn or have in savings. There is no means test on the employed route at all.
  • It pays for specialist equipment, assistive software, support workers, travel to work if you cannot use public transport, vehicle adaptations and physical changes to your workplace. Your workplace includes your home if you work there.
  • It will not pay for reasonable adjustments. Those are your employer's legal duty, and Access to Work will tell them so.
  • You do not need a diagnosis to apply, and a temporary condition such as a broken leg can qualify.

1. Three different things called Access to Work

Access to Work is not one scheme. It is three, with three different application routes, and people apply to the wrong one constantly.

A grant for practical support with your work. This is the one most people mean. You apply through GOV.UK and DWP assesses what you need.

Support with managing your mental health at work. You do not apply to DWP for this. You apply directly to either Able Futures or Maximus, and GOV.UK is explicit that you must only apply to one. It can include a tailored plan to help you get or stay in work and one-to-one sessions with a mental health professional. There is a hard limit: if you have had mental health support through Access to Work before, you cannot apply again.

Communication support at a job interview. A separate route again, with its own eligibility rules. It covers a British Sign Language interpreter or lipspeaker if you are deaf or hard of hearing, and communication support if you have a physical or mental health condition or a learning difficulty. This one is for the interview itself, before you have the job.

The three do not share a form and they do not share a decision. Applying for the grant will not get you mental health support, and getting mental health support once does not stop you applying for the grant.

2. What the grant pays for

GOV.UK's list for the practical support grant:

  • specialist equipment and assistive software
  • support workers, such as a British Sign Language interpreter, a job coach or a travel buddy
  • costs of travelling to work, if you cannot use public transport
  • adaptations to your vehicle so you can get to work
  • physical changes to your workplace

Two lines around that list do most of the work.

"Your workplace can include your home if you work from there some or all of the time." Home working is not a reason to be turned away, and equipment for a home desk is within scope.

"If you get an Access to Work grant, it will not affect any other benefits you get and you will not have to pay it back." That is the sentence that removes the fear people have about applying while on Universal Credit or ESA.

One practical warning from the same page: you or your employer may need to pay some costs up front and claim them back later. That cash flow gap is real and worth agreeing with an employer before anything is ordered, particularly for equipment that runs to four figures.

3. The £6,500 rule, and what it is actually for

Search for Access to Work and you will find £6,500 described as a limit, a cap, or a maximum for some kinds of support. It is none of those.

Here is where the figure actually sits on GOV.UK, under the heading "Your income and benefits" on the eligibility page:

  • "You can get Access to Work at the same time as most benefits, as long as you work more than 1 hour a week."
  • "If you're employed, you can get support however much you earn or have in savings."
  • "If you're self-employed, you must have an annual turnover of at least £6,500."

So it is a floor, and it applies to one group only. It is the minimum trading activity a self-employed applicant has to show. Turnover, not profit, which matters: a sole trader turning over £9,000 and making very little after costs still clears the test.

Get the direction of that wrong and you make two different mistakes. An employed applicant assumes their support is capped at £6,500 when there is no such cap on their route. A self-employed applicant with modest turnover assumes there is no income test at all, applies, and is refused for a reason nobody warned them about.

The line above it is worth as much. If you are employed, your earnings and savings are irrelevant. There is no means test, no capital limit, and no taper. A software developer on £70,000 who needs a screen reader and a job coach is assessed on need, not on income.

And the one about hours. You can get Access to Work alongside most benefits provided you work more than 1 hour a week. If you get Employment and Support Allowance there is a further rule: you can have both at the same time if you work less than 16 hours a week, within the wider rules about working while claiming ESA. So the ESA route lives in the window between 1 and 16 hours, and your work coach can advise on it.

4. What it will never pay for

GOV.UK gives this its own heading, which tells you how often it comes up.

"Access to Work will not pay for reasonable adjustments. These are the changes your employer must legally make to support you to do your job."

And then: "Access to Work will advise your employer if changes should be made as reasonable adjustments."

Read that as what it is. Access to Work is not a fund that lets an employer off a legal duty under the Equality Act 2010. If the thing you need is a reasonable adjustment, the answer is not a grant, it is your employer doing it. And if your employer has been telling you to apply to Access to Work for something that is plainly their responsibility, an Access to Work assessment is quite likely to say so in writing, which is useful to have.

Two other exclusions worth knowing:

  • Voluntary work is not covered. You need a paid job, or to be about to start or return to one.
  • Civil servants do not use Access to Work. Your employer provides the support instead, and you contact them to arrange it.

5. Who can apply, and how

To get the practical support grant or the mental health support you must:

  • have a physical or mental health condition or disability that means you need support to do your job or to get to and from work
  • be 16 or over
  • be in paid work, or be about to start or return to paid work in the next 12 weeks
  • live and work, or be about to start or return to work, in England, Scotland or Wales

Northern Ireland runs its own version through nidirect. You cannot get Access to Work if you live in the Channel Islands or the Isle of Man.

"You do not need to be diagnosed with a condition to apply." That single sentence removes the most common self-imposed barrier, given NHS waiting lists for autism and ADHD assessments running into years.

The list of conditions GOV.UK gives as examples is broad: a physical disability such as being hard of hearing or using a wheelchair, a learning disability such as Down's syndrome, a developmental condition such as autism spectrum disorder, ADHD or dyslexia, an illness such as diabetes or epilepsy, a temporary condition such as a broken leg, and a mental health condition such as anxiety or depression.

A temporary condition counts. Somebody who cannot drive for three months after surgery and cannot get to work any other way is inside the scheme, and almost nobody applies.

What counts as paid work is also wider than "a job": employment, self-employment, an apprenticeship, a work trial or work experience, an internship, and a work placement all qualify. Full time or part time.

The 12 week rule is the timing point. You can apply before you start, which means the equipment and the support can be in place on day one instead of three months in. If you have accepted an offer, do not wait for the start date.

If you are unsure whether you qualify, the Access to Work helpline is 0800 121 7479, Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm. Relay UK is 18001 then the same number, and there is a British Sign Language video relay service. If phone calls are difficult for you, GOV.UK says you can ask for all communication to be by email instead, which is a right worth using rather than a favour to request.

The verdict

Access to Work is one of the few things in this area with no means test for employed people, no repayment, and no effect on other benefits, and it is badly under-used because of two myths. The first is that £6,500 caps what you can get, when it is actually a minimum turnover the self-employed have to clear. The second is that you need a formal diagnosis, when GOV.UK says you do not. If you are starting a job in the next twelve weeks and you will need equipment or support to do it, apply now rather than on your first day. And if your employer is pointing you at Access to Work for something that is obviously their duty, that is a reasonable adjustments conversation, not a grant application.

Frequently asked

Is £6,500 the most I can get? No. It is the minimum annual turnover a self-employed applicant needs in order to be eligible at all. It is not a limit on the value of the support, and it does not apply to employed applicants, whose earnings and savings are not assessed.

Will applying affect my Universal Credit or ESA? GOV.UK says an Access to Work grant will not affect any other benefits you get and does not have to be repaid. You can claim alongside most benefits provided you work more than 1 hour a week, and alongside ESA if you work less than 16 hours a week.

Do I need a diagnosis? No. GOV.UK states that you do not need to be diagnosed with a condition to apply. That matters given the length of NHS waiting lists for autism and ADHD assessments.

Can I apply before I start the job? Yes, if you are about to start or return to paid work within the next 12 weeks. Applying early is the difference between having the support on your first day and waiting months for it.
