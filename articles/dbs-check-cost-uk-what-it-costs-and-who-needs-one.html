Five Clients, One Certificate: the DBS Rule That Stops You Paying Five Times

Tutors, cleaners, carers and dog walkers get asked for a DBS check constantly. A basic one costs £21.50, it has no expiry date, and if you are self employed the person hiring you is not allowed to order it for you in the first place.

1. What it costs, and how long it takes

A basic DBS check costs £21.50. That figure appears on the GOV.UK page for requesting a basic check and it is the same whichever route you use.

The timescale is where GOV.UK gives two answers, and it is worth knowing both. The page for applying direct for your own basic check says it usually takes up to 3 days for your application to be processed, after which a paper certificate is posted to you. The separate page covering basic checks arranged for an employee or volunteer, through a responsible organisation, gives up to 14 days.

Both can be true, because they describe different routes with different numbers of parties in the chain. The practical answer for a self employed person is: apply direct, expect the process itself to be quick, and still allow a couple of weeks before you promise a client anything, because the certificate arrives in the post.

On price, one qualifier. Where a check is processed through a responsible organisation from GOV.UK's registered list, that organisation may charge an administration fee on top of the £21.50. Applying direct through the GOV.UK service avoids that. Ask what the total is before you start.

To apply direct you prove your identity through GOV.UK One Login, and you will need addresses for the last 5 years with dates, plus, if you have them, your passport, driving licence and National Insurance number. You must be 16 or over. The service is open 8am to 11:30pm.

Standard and enhanced checks cost more and are published on a separate GOV.UK page. We are not quoting those figures here because we have not read them on the same day as the £21.50, and a wrong fee is worse than a missing one. Most side work sits at basic level anyway.

2. The four levels, and what each one shows

GOV.UK lists four things you can request, and the difference between them is exactly what appears on the certificate.

  • Basic: unspent convictions and conditional cautions.
  • Standard: spent and unspent convictions and cautions.
  • Enhanced: the same as standard, plus any information held by local police that is considered relevant to the role.
  • Enhanced with barred lists: the same as enhanced, plus whether the applicant is on the list of people barred from doing that role.

You do not get to pick freely. The higher levels are tied to eligible roles, and GOV.UK points to its own tool for finding out what type of check you can get. People applying for a role can sometimes request an enhanced check, or an enhanced check with barred list, for themselves.

A basic check only shows convictions that are not spent. GOV.UK gives an example on the same page: some types of caution disappear after 3 months. If you are worrying about something old, the question is whether it is spent, not whether it happened.

One point that is easy to miss and expensive to discover late. A DBS check will not cover the time someone lived outside the UK, and GOV.UK publishes separate guidance on criminal record checks for overseas applicants. If a client needs cover for a period you spent abroad, the DBS certificate does not provide it.

Scotland and Northern Ireland are separate systems, run through Disclosure Scotland on mygov.scot and AccessNI respectively. You can still get a basic DBS check if you live in Scotland or Northern Ireland but the job is in England or Wales.

3. The self employed rule, and why it saves you money

Here is the sentence that changes how side workers should handle this. GOV.UK says that if you are hiring a self employed person, or a personal employee, to deliver services to you or a member of your family, you cannot request a DBS check for them. They need to request one for themselves, and you check their certificate.

So a family hiring a private tutor cannot order the check. The tutor orders it, and the family looks at the certificate. That inverts the usual assumption and it is the reason a lot of self employed people end up paying for something they could have organised once.

Hassan in Bolton tutors GCSE maths for five families. Each family asks about a DBS check.

What he assumes: five checks, five times £21.50, which is £107.50.

What the rules actually say: none of those families is allowed to request a check for him, because he is self employed and delivering a service to their household. He requests one check, at £21.50, and each family checks the same certificate.

What he saves: £86, which at a typical GCSE tutoring rate is most of an hour's work recovered for the price of reading one GOV.UK page.

The catch he should still plan for: there is no expiry date on the certificate, so a family in two years may ask for something more recent. That is their judgement to make, not a rule, and the update service exists precisely so a certificate can be re-checked rather than re-bought.

GOV.UK is clear that an employer can accept a certificate requested for a previous role, provided they check that the applicant's identity matches the details on it, that the certificate is the right level and type for the role, and, if the applicant is signed up to the update service, whether anything has changed.

That update service is the mechanism that keeps one certificate useful over time, because a DBS check has no official expiry date and nothing on the paper tells anyone whether it is still accurate. The update service carries its own subscription fee, which we have deliberately not quoted here, and GOV.UK's update service page is the place to check it before signing up.

If you are a childminder, this is handled differently again: GOV.UK says childminders get a check through Ofsted.

The verdict

If your side work involves other people's homes, children or vulnerable adults, get a basic check before you need it rather than when a client asks. The direct route is quick on GOV.UK's own figure, but the certificate still comes by post and the employer route is quoted at up to 14 days, so treat two weeks as the planning number. £21.50 once is a small cost of doing business.

Then treat the certificate as an asset rather than a one off purchase. One certificate, checked by each new client, is the design GOV.UK describes. Anyone telling you that every client needs a fresh check is describing their own preference, not a legal requirement, because there is no expiry date to point at.

Where this does not help you: if a role genuinely requires a standard or enhanced check, the basic one will not do, and the fees for those are published separately. Check what type of check the role qualifies for before paying for the wrong one.

Frequently asked

Can a client order a DBS check for me if I am self employed? No. GOV.UK says that where you are hiring a self employed person or personal employee to deliver services to you or your family, you cannot request a check for them. They request it and you check the certificate.

How long does it actually take? GOV.UK gives two figures on two pages. Applying direct for your own basic check, it says usually up to 3 days to process. Through the employer and volunteer route it says up to 14 days. Plan for the longer figure and treat the shorter one as the good outcome.

How long is a DBS check valid? There is no official expiry date. GOV.UK says the information is accurate at the time the check was carried out and it is up to the employer to decide when a new check is needed.

Does it cover the years I lived abroad? No. GOV.UK says DBS checks will not cover the time someone lived outside the UK, and publishes separate guidance for overseas applicants.

Is £21.50 the total I will pay? It is the cost of the basic check. Where a responsible organisation processes it, GOV.UK notes it may also charge an administration fee, so ask what that is before you apply.
