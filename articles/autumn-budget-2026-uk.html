Autumn Budget, 28 October: Three Things Already Decided, and the Rest Is Noise

Budget season produces more speculation than policy. Three changes affecting UK households are already confirmed, and they are the only ones worth planning around today. Here is what is real, what it means for you, and the one thing worth doing before the Chancellor stands up.

Ruhul Amin Published 18 August 2026 · 7 min read · Checked August 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The Budget is Wednesday 28 October 2026, delivered by Chancellor John Healey.
  • Confirmed 1: income tax thresholds stay frozen to 2030/31, dragging more people into higher bands as wages rise.
  • Confirmed 2: the cash ISA allowance falls to £12,000 for under-65s from 6 April 2027. Over-65s keep £20,000.
  • Confirmed 3: salary sacrifice pension contributions above £2,000 a year attract National Insurance from 2029.
  • Everything else is speculation. Property taxes, pension reform and further ISA changes are rumoured every year, and most rumours do not happen.

1. The three confirmed changes

ChangeFrom whenWho it affects
Income tax thresholds frozenNow, to 2030/31Almost everyone who earns. Higher-rate taxpayers rise from 5.75m to 7.7m
Cash ISA limit £12,0006 April 2027Under-65s saving more than £12,000 a year in cash ISAs
NICs on salary sacrifice above £2,0002029Employees making large pension contributions via salary sacrifice

Of the three, the threshold freeze affects by far the most people, and it is the one that gets the least attention because it never appears as a headline tax rise. We cover it in detail in our guide to frozen tax thresholds.

The cash ISA change matters only if you save large amounts in cash, which most people do not. Full detail in our cash ISA guide.

The salary sacrifice change is three years away and only bites above £2,000 a year of sacrificed contributions. Worth knowing, not worth panicking about.

Every year the pre-Budget press invents a dozen tax rises. Most never arrive. Planning around a rumour is how people make expensive, irreversible decisions for nothing.

2. What is only speculation

Ahead of any Budget, the same subjects circulate: property and council tax reform, changes to pension tax relief, capital gains tax, inheritance tax, and further ISA restrictions.

These are discussed because they raise meaningful revenue, not because anything has been announced. Treat every one of them as unconfirmed until the Chancellor says it at the despatch box. Predictions from banks, accountancy firms and newspapers are informed guesses, and their track record is mixed at best.

3. The one thing worth doing before 28 October

Use the allowances that already exist, on their current terms. Not because a change is coming, but because unused allowances usually expire at the end of the tax year regardless.

  1. ISA allowance. £20,000 this tax year, on current rules. If you were going to use it, use it.
  2. Pension annual allowance. Particularly valuable if you are near £50,270 or £100,000, where contributions reduce assessed income.
  3. Marriage Allowance, if one partner earns under the personal allowance. Free, and widely unclaimed.
  4. Check your tax code. Wrong codes are common, and the money is yours either way. A five minute check on your Personal Tax Account can be worth hundreds.

None of these depend on what happens on 28 October. All of them are worth doing anyway, which is exactly what makes them sensible rather than reactive.

4. Why acting on rumours usually costs money

Every Budget season produces the same pattern: people withdraw pension money, sell assets, or restructure ownership because of a headline, then the change never arrives. The tax consequences of those moves are real. The rumour was not.

Two rules that hold up well. Never make an irreversible financial decision based on a prediction. And if a move only makes sense because of a rumoured tax change, it is not a good move. Anything worth doing is usually worth doing on its own merits.

Frequently asked

When exactly is the Budget? Wednesday 28 October 2026, in the House of Commons.

Do changes take effect immediately? Some do, from the day of the announcement or shortly after. Others start at the beginning of the next tax year in April, and some are years out, like the 2029 salary sacrifice change.

Should I move money out of my pension before the Budget? Withdrawing pension money early has real tax consequences and cannot usually be undone. This is exactly the situation for regulated advice rather than a news story.

5. The honest verdict

Three changes are confirmed, and only one of them, the threshold freeze, affects most households. The rest of what you will read between now and 28 October is speculation dressed as analysis.

Use your existing allowances because they expire, check your tax code because it is often wrong, and ignore anything that asks you to make a permanent decision based on a prediction.
