Induction Is Twice As Efficient As Gas And Still Costs More To Run. Only One Thing Changes That

Every induction advert leads with efficiency, and the efficiency claim is true. It is also the wrong number. Efficiency tells you how much of the energy reaches the pan. Price tells you what that energy costs, and at the October 2026 cap electricity costs 3.30 times what gas costs.

The short version

  • Induction puts about 87% of what it draws into the pan. Gas manages about 45%. That part of the sales pitch is correct.
  • Electricity costs 26.32p a kWh and gas costs 7.97p under the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026. That is a ratio of 3.30 to 1, against an efficiency ratio of only about 1.94 to 1.
  • Net result: induction costs roughly 70% more to run than gas, which for a normal household is about £18 a year extra, not the saving the adverts imply.
  • The one case where induction wins is when the hob is your last gas appliance. Removing it removes the gas standing charge of 29.68p a day, which is £108.33 a year.
  • In that case the net saving is about £90 a year, and a £700 hob plus £350 of electrical work pays back in about 11.7 years. If you keep gas heating, it never pays back at all.

1. The efficiency claim, and why it is not the answer

An induction hob does not heat the hob. It induces a current directly in the base of the pan, so the pan is the heating element. Almost nothing is lost to the room. Manufacturers and retailers put the figure at 85 to 90 per cent of the electricity drawn ending up as heat in the food. Take the middle of that, 87 per cent.

A gas burner heats a flame, the flame heats the pan, and a large share of the flame goes past the pan and up into the room. Published figures for domestic gas hobs sit between 38 and 50 per cent. Take 45 per cent.

So induction is about 1.94 times as efficient. That is a real advantage and nobody disputes it.

The problem is that efficiency is a measure of energy, not of money. To turn it into money you have to divide by the price of each fuel, and in Britain those two prices are nowhere near each other.

This is the same trap that catches people looking at infrared heating panels, which are marketed as 100 per cent efficient and are, and which still cost three times what gas costs to produce the same warmth.

2. The two prices, from the Ofgem cap

Ofgem publishes the capped unit rates for every cap period. For 1 October to 31 December 2026 the direct debit figures for a typical region are:

Electricity: 26.32p per kWh, plus a standing charge of 54.83p a day.
Gas: 7.97p per kWh, plus a standing charge of 29.68p a day.

If you are reading this before 1 October, the cap still in force is 26.11p and 7.33p, with standing charges of 57.19p and 29.04p. The arithmetic below uses the October figures because that is what these numbers will be for most of the life of this page.

26.32 divided by 7.97 is 3.30. A unit of electricity costs three and a third times what a unit of gas costs.

Now put the two ratios together. Induction is 1.94 times as efficient, and its fuel is 3.30 times the price. Divide 3.30 by 1.94 and you get 1.70. Induction costs about seventy per cent more to run than gas for the same cooking.

4. The standing charge is the real decision

A gas standing charge is what you pay for being connected to the gas network. It is 29.68p a day under the October cap, whether you burn a single unit or not. Over a year that is £108.33.

If your hob is the only gas appliance you own, because you have a heat pump or electric heating and a heat pump or immersion for hot water, then that £108.33 buys you nothing except the ability to cook on gas. Switching to induction lets you close the gas account entirely.

£108.33 saved on standing charge, minus about £18 lost on running cost, is a net saving of roughly £90 a year.

Set that against the cost of switching. An induction hob needs a dedicated circuit, usually 32 amp, because a four-zone hob can pull 7kW. Almost no kitchen with a gas hob already has one. A qualified electrician running a new circuit from the consumer unit is typically £250 to £450. A Gas Safe engineer capping off the gas supply is another £80 to £120. The hob itself is anywhere from £300 to over £1,000.

Call it £700 for the hob and £350 for the electrical and gas work. £1,050 divided by £90 a year is 11.7 years.

That is a long payback but it is a real one, and hobs do last that long. If you still have gas central heating, the standing charge does not go away, the saving is zero, and you are simply choosing to pay £18 a year more for a nicer hob. There is nothing wrong with that. It is just not a saving.

5. Worked example: Farida in Rochdale

Farida had an air source heat pump fitted two years ago. Her heating and hot water are electric. The only gas appliance left in the house is a four-burner hob, and she is paying £108.33 a year in standing charge to keep the gas meter alive for it.

She cooks most nights and reckons on about four litres of boiling a day across kettleless pasta, rice and vegetables. On the arithmetic above, gas costs her about £25.53 a year in units. Induction would cost about £43.62. So her cooking bill rises by £18.09.

But the £108.33 disappears. Net saving: £90.24 a year.

She is quoted £620 for a four-zone induction hob and £390 for the electrician plus the gas cap-off. £1,010 divided by £90.24 is 11.2 years.

Farida's decision is not really about the eleven years. It is that she was going to replace a twelve-year-old hob within about three years anyway. Once the hob is being replaced regardless, the only marginal cost is the £390 of trade work, and £390 divided by £90.24 is 4.3 years. That is a decision worth making.

Her neighbour with a gas combi boiler runs exactly the same numbers and gets nothing, because the gas meter stays either way.

6. What induction is genuinely better at, none of which is money

The running cost case is weak. The other cases are strong, and it is worth being clear which is which.

Speed. An induction zone will bring a litre to the boil in roughly half the time of a gas burner, because almost nothing is lost to the room. If you are cooking after a late shift this matters more than eighteen pounds.

The kitchen stays cooler. The energy that gas wastes has to go somewhere, and in summer it goes into a room you may then be paying to cool.

Cleaning. A flat glass surface against burner crowns, pan supports and a spill tray. Nobody argues about this one.

Indoor air. Burning gas indoors produces nitrogen dioxide. Whether that matters at domestic levels is a live scientific argument and not one this page will settle, but if you have a small kitchen with poor extraction it is a reasonable thing to weigh.

Pans. Against all that, induction only works with ferrous pans. Hold a fridge magnet to the base of yours. If it does not stick, that pan is scrap on an induction hob, and a decent replacement set is £80 to £200 that belongs in the payback sum.

7. The honest verdict

If you have gas central heating, do not buy an induction hob to save money. It will cost you roughly £18 a year more to run and the gas standing charge stays exactly where it is. Buy one because it is faster and easier to clean, and count the running cost as the price of that.

If your hob is the last gas appliance in the house, switch. Killing the £108.33 standing charge nets you about £90 a year, and if you were replacing the hob anyway the trade work alone pays back in about four years.

Do not let anyone sell you induction on efficiency. The efficiency is real and it is not enough.

Frequently asked

Does an induction hob need a special plug? No, it needs a hard-wired circuit. A four-zone hob can draw around 7kW, which is far beyond a 13 amp socket. It is normally a dedicated 32 amp circuit from the consumer unit, and that is why the electrician's bill is a real part of this decision.

Are single-zone plug-in induction hobs any different? They are limited to about 2kW so they will run from a normal socket, and they cost under £60. As a way of trying induction before committing they are excellent. As a permanent hob they are slow for anything involving more than one pan.

What about the gas standing charge if I only remove the hob but keep a gas fire? Then nothing changes. The standing charge is per meter, not per appliance. It only disappears when you close the account and have the meter removed, which requires every gas appliance to go.

Will electricity always be this much more expensive than gas? Not necessarily. The ratio has narrowed slightly, and government policy on moving levies off electricity bills would narrow it further. Ofgem sets the cap quarterly, so check the rates rather than trusting a ratio you read once.
