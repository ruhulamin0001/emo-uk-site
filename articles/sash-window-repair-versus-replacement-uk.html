Nobody Wants to Tell You Your Window Will Not Pay for Itself

Replacement window salespeople quote energy savings, and repair specialists quote heritage. Both are avoiding the same awkward number. Here is the heat loss of a single-glazed sash worked out at the October 2026 cap, and what each option genuinely buys you, which is not a payback.

Ruhul Amin Published 18 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • A single-glazed sash loses roughly 245 kWh a year more than a modern double-glazed unit of the same size, which is about £19.51 a year at 7.97p per kWh.
  • Replacement at around £1,200 a window therefore pays back in about 61 years on energy alone. The window will not last that long.
  • Draught-proofing and overhauling an original sash costs around £250 to £400 and saves most of the air leakage, which on typical figures is a 31 year payback.
  • So buy for the reasons that are actually true: comfort, draughts, noise, condensation, security and the fact that the thing does not open any more.
  • Approved Document L sets 1.4 W/m²K as the limit for replacement windows, and in a conservation area or on a listed building you may not be permitted to replace at all.

1. What a single-glazed sash actually loses

Let us do the number first, because everything else follows from it.

Figures use the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: gas at 7.97p per kWh including VAT at 5% and electricity at 26.32p per kWh with no VAT, because the zero rate on electricity runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027.

Single glazing in a timber frame has a whole-window U-value of roughly 4.8 W/m²K. A modern double-glazed replacement at the Approved Document L limiting standard is 1.4 W/m²K. The difference is 3.4 W/m²K.

Take a fairly typical sash of 1.5 m², and a UK heating season of roughly 2,000 degree days:

3.4 multiplied by 1.5, multiplied by 2,000, multiplied by 24, divided by 1,000, is about 245 kWh a year.

At 7.97p per kWh, that is £19.51 a year for that one window.

Now the second component, which is the one that actually makes an old sash unpleasant. Air leakage. A worn sash with shrunken timber, missing parting beads and gaps at the meeting rail leaks continuously, and the effect on comfort is far larger than the conduction figure suggests because moving cold air feels much colder than a cold surface. Draught-proofing an overhauled sash is generally reckoned to cut the infiltration loss substantially, and on a typical window that is worth in the order of 100 kWh a year, or £7.97.

Note that draught-proofing captures a saving that replacement also captures, so the two are not additive. A new window does both jobs.

2. The three options, and what each costs

Overhaul and draught-proof the original. The sashes come out, cords and weights are replaced, the timber is repaired where it has decayed, brush or compression seals are routed into the staff and parting beads, and the whole thing is rebalanced so it opens properly. Typically £250 to £400 a window, more where there is significant rot to splice out. The window remains single glazed, and the original glass stays, which for a period house is the whole point.

Slim double glazing into the original sashes. The existing frames and boxes are kept and new sashes, or reglazed originals, carry a narrow double-glazed unit. This gets the U-value down to somewhere around 1.8 to 2.2 W/m²K, keeps the appearance, and costs considerably more than an overhaul, commonly £700 to £1,200 a window. It is the technically elegant answer and the one that most often gets past a conservation officer.

Full replacement. New timber, or PVCu, sliding sash units to the modern limiting standard. Typically £900 to £1,800 a window depending on material, size and glazing bars. Best thermal result, worst outcome for the character of a period elevation, and in some locations not permitted.

There is a fourth route worth knowing: secondary glazing, a separate internal frame inside the existing window. It performs well thermally, is outstanding for noise, is fully reversible, and is usually acceptable on listed buildings precisely because it touches nothing original.

Read the payback column and then stop using it. Every row is longer than the life of the thing you are buying. That is not a failure of the options, it is a statement that windows are not an energy investment in a British climate at British gas prices, and anyone selling them as one is selling you a payback they have not calculated.

What the table does tell you is relative value. The overhaul is the cheapest route to most of the comfort improvement, because the draught is the part you feel. That is why it appears first.

4. The rules that may decide it for you

Three constraints can remove the choice entirely.

Listed building consent. Replacing windows in a listed building is almost always alteration requiring consent, and consent for uPVC sliding sashes in a Georgian elevation is not usually forthcoming. Doing it without consent is a criminal offence, not a planning technicality.

Conservation areas and Article 4 directions. In a conservation area, permitted development rights for window replacement on the principal elevation are frequently removed by an Article 4 direction. Check with the local planning authority before ordering anything, because the answer is address-specific.

Approved Document L. Where you are permitted to replace, the new window must meet the limiting standard of 1.4 W/m²K or a Window Energy Rating of band B, and must not be worse than what it replaces. Replacement windows are notifiable, and a FENSA or Certass registered installer self-certifies. That certificate is asked for at every future sale.

Worked example: the Trelawny household, Truro

Six original sashes on the front of an 1840s townhouse in a conservation area. Draughty, rattling, two painted shut, condensation every winter morning.

Replacement quote: £7,900 for six timber sliding sashes. Refused at pre-application stage, because an Article 4 direction removed permitted development rights on the principal elevation and the proposed glazing bar profile was not acceptable.

Overhaul and draught-proof: £1,950 for all six, including splicing out rot on two sills and rebalancing every sash so they open again.

The energy saving: about 600 kWh a year across six windows, or £47.82. Payback, 41 years, which is meaningless and they knew it.

What they actually bought. Six windows that open, a front room that stopped being the cold room, and no condensation on the glass in January. They then spent £2,400 on secondary glazing to the two worst rooms for the traffic noise, which was the real complaint all along.

Total £4,350 against £7,900, a better outcome for the building, and a decision made on comfort rather than on a payback nobody could have honoured.

5. How to tell whether the frames are worth saving

Everything above assumes the original joinery can be repaired. Usually it can, because Georgian and Victorian sashes were made in slow-grown softwood or oak with a density modern timber rarely matches. But not always, and the test is straightforward.

Take a bradawl or a small screwdriver and press it into the vulnerable places: the bottom rail of the lower sash, the outer face of the sill, the joint where the stile meets the bottom rail, and the base of the box frame where it meets the masonry. Sound timber resists. Rot goes soft and dark and takes the point easily.

Localised rot is not a reason to replace. A joiner splices in new timber, which is routine work and costs a fraction of a new window. The question is how much of the section is gone. A sill with 20mm of surface decay is a repair. A bottom rail that has lost its joints and a sill you can push a screwdriver through to the inside is at the point where replacement becomes the sensible economic answer.

Two other conditions push towards replacement. Hardwood glazing bars that have parted at the joints across most of a window are a rebuild rather than a repair. And a box frame that has dropped, because the masonry above it has moved, is a structural job first and a joinery job second.

Get the opinion from a joiner rather than from a replacement window company. The two are asked to solve different problems and will reliably reach different conclusions.

6. The honest verdict

If the frames are sound, overhaul and draught-proof. It is the cheapest option, it fixes the part you actually feel, and it keeps a window that has already lasted a hundred and fifty years.

If noise is the problem, secondary glazing beats everything else on the list and costs less than replacement.

Replace only when the timber has gone beyond economic repair, or when you are in a house where nobody will ever care about the joinery. And do not let anyone sell you a window on a payback calculation, because on any honest set of numbers there is not one.

Frequently asked

Is secondary glazing allowed on a listed building? Usually yes, and it is often the option a conservation officer prefers, because it is reversible and does not alter the original fabric. Consent is still generally required, so ask before ordering.

Does draught-proofing cause condensation or damp? It can, if you seal the house and do nothing about ventilation. An old house ventilated by its own gaps loses that ventilation when you close them. Fix the extract ventilation at the same time.

Are slim double-glazed units reliable? Modern warm-edge slim units are much better than early ones, but the cavity is narrow and the seal is working harder. Check the guarantee term on the unit specifically, not on the joinery.

What about uPVC sliding sash windows? Thermally they perform well and they need little maintenance. On a period elevation the sight lines and the frame depth rarely convince, and in a conservation area they are often refused outright.
