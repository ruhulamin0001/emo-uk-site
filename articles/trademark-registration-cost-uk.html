£20.50 a Year to Keep Your Own Name, or Several Thousand to Change It

Registering a UK trade mark costs £205 online and lasts a decade, which makes it one of the cheapest things a small business can buy. The option that looks cheaper is not, the class you are tempted to add is a liability, and the free search you skip is where the real money goes.

The short version

  • A UK trade mark costs £205 online for one class, plus £60 for each additional class. By post it is £250 plus £60 a class. Those are the Intellectual Property Office fees as published on GOV.UK.
  • Registration lasts 10 years. So one class online works out at £20.50 a year over the term, and three classes at £32.50 a year.
  • Right Start costs more, not less, if your application is fine. £125 now plus £125 later is £250 against £205 standard. You are paying about £45 for a check, not saving money.
  • The expensive part is the search you skip. The fee is not refundable if somebody opposes you, and a forced rebrand costs far more than £205 in signage, packaging, domains and lost search traffic.
  • Copyright is free and automatic. A trade mark is neither. They protect different things, and confusing them is the most common and costly mistake.

1. What it actually costs

The Intellectual Property Office publishes its fees plainly, and GOV.UK summarises the position as costing at least £205. Here is the full structure.

Two findings fall straight out of that table.

Right Start is not the budget option. It looks cheaper because the first invoice is £125. But if your application passes, you pay another £125 to continue, and the total is £250 against £205 for the standard route. At three classes it is £370 against £325. Either way you have paid roughly £45 more for the privilege of finding out first. That can be money well spent if your mark is borderline. It is money wasted if it is clearly distinctive.

Applying by post costs £45 extra for nothing. The protection is identical. Unless you cannot apply online, that is a straightforward £45 given away.

Right Start is a check, not a discount. If your mark was always going to sail through, you have paid about £45 to be told so.

2. One class or three, and how to decide

A trade mark is registered against classes of goods and services. Protection only exists in the classes you register. A clothing brand registered in class 25 has no trade mark protection if somebody launches a cafe under the same name in class 43.

Each extra class is £60, which is a modest sum against £205, and the temptation is to register widely. Resist it, for a specific reason: a UK trade mark can be revoked for non-use if it has not been genuinely used for the registered goods or services within five years of registration. Classes you registered speculatively and never traded in are vulnerable, and defending a wide registration you never used is not free.

The practical approach is to register the classes you trade in now, plus the ones you have a concrete plan to trade in within five years. Not the ones you can imagine.

3. The search you skip is the expensive part

Here is the risk the fee table does not show. Application fees are not refundable. If the IPO examines your mark and objects, or if an existing rights holder opposes it, you lose the fee and you still have no registration.

Worse, an application is published on GOV.UK as soon as you file. GOV.UK flags this explicitly: somebody else could use details from your application, for example by buying the domain for your brand name. A speculative application is not a private enquiry.

So before filing, do the free work:

  1. Search the IPO trade mark database for identical and similar marks in your classes. It is free and it takes an afternoon.
  2. Search Companies House for company names close to yours.
  3. Search plain Google and the domain registries. An unregistered business trading under your name for years may hold rights through passing off, even with no registration.
  4. Check the mark is distinctive. Descriptive names, plain geographical terms and common phrases are the ones examiners object to.

The IPO usually takes around three to four months from application to registration if there are no issues. If there are issues, you resolve them first, and that is where cost escalates beyond the headline fee.

Worked example: Rebecca in Chesterfield

Rebecca runs a small ceramics business. She sells pottery, and she is starting to run weekend workshops.

What she registers. Two classes: goods for the pottery, and education and training services for the workshops. Online, that is £205 plus £60, which is £265, or £26.50 a year across the ten-year term.

What she does not register. She considered adding a class for clothing, because she might sell branded aprons one day. She left it out. At £60 it was tempting, but with no concrete plan inside five years it would be exposed to a non-use challenge and would be £60 spent on nothing.

The alternative she priced. If somebody else registers her name first and she has to rebrand, the bill is not £265. A new sign for the studio, new packaging and labels, reprinted business cards, a new domain, and the loss of every search result and social handle built up over four years. She put that at well over £3,000 and several months of disruption, and that is before any legal correspondence.

The payback framing. £265 over ten years against a rebrand risk she estimates at £3,000 or more. Expressed as insurance, the registration costs under 9% of the downside, once, for a decade of protection. That is not a close call.

What she chose on Right Start. She skipped it. Her mark is an invented word, clearly distinctive, and the free database search found nothing similar in her classes. Paying £45 more to be told that would have been a purchase of reassurance, not protection.

4. Trade mark against copyright, which are not the same thing

This is the confusion that costs people most, so it is worth being blunt about it.

  • Copyright arises automatically the moment you create an original work. There is no registration, no form and no fee in the UK. It protects the work itself: the photograph, the text, the drawing, the code.
  • A trade mark protects the badge of origin: the name or logo that tells customers the goods are yours. It requires registration, a fee and a renewal every ten years.

You can own the copyright in your logo artwork and still have no trade mark in the name. Somebody can then register that name, and your copyright in the drawing will not help you, because they are not copying your drawing. They are using your name.

5. Frequently asked

Does a UK trade mark protect me abroad? No. It covers the UK and the Isle of Man. Guernsey and Jersey have separate processes, and protection elsewhere requires separate applications or an international route.

Do I have to renew it? Yes, every ten years, for the registration to stay in force. That renewal fee is separate from the application fee.

Can I use the ® symbol before registration? No. The symbol indicates a registered mark, and using it before registration is a false claim.

What if I never register anything? You may still have unregistered rights through the law of passing off, but enforcing those means proving goodwill, misrepresentation and damage, which is slow and expensive. A registration turns that into showing a certificate.

6. The honest verdict

If you have a distinctive trading name, customers who find you by that name, and any plan to still be trading in three years, register it. At £205 for one class online, or £20.50 a year across the term, it is one of the cheapest pieces of business protection available, and the downside it insures against runs to thousands.

Apply online rather than on paper, because the post route costs £45 more for the same protection. Skip Right Start unless your mark is genuinely borderline, because it adds about £45 rather than saving it. Register the classes you actually trade in, not the ones you can imagine.

And do the free searches first. The application fee is not refundable, and your application becomes public the day you file it.
