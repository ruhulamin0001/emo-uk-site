1. The two Energy Saving Trust figures and what they assume

The Energy Saving Trust's appliances blog, updated 3 August 2026 and read again on 5 September 2026, says that a 60-inch F-rated TV costs £40 a year to run in Great Britain and £50 in Northern Ireland, and that a smaller 40-inch F-rated TV costs £20 a year in GB and £25 in NI. It adds that consumer electronics, meaning TVs, laptops and games consoles together, account for around 6 per cent of an energy bill, and that switching devices off standby saves £45 a year in GB and £55 in NI.

The two TV figures share a rating and differ only in size, and the bigger one costs exactly double. That is the Trust's point and it is the point of this article: at the same letter, the inches set the bill. The energy label for televisions rates the set on energy per 1,000 hours in a standard picture mode, and the classes are so demanding that almost every current set sits at E, F or G. A better letter at the same size saves a little; a smaller screen saves a lot.

The figures imply an assumption about hours. Working backwards at the current unit rate, £40 a year is about 152 kWh, which is a 60-inch set drawing roughly 100 W for about four hours a day, and £20 is a 40-inch set at about 50 W for the same four hours. Your household may watch two hours or eight, so the useful thing is not the Trust's number but the sum behind it.

2. The three-line sum: watts, hours, 26.32p

Every TV sold in the UK carries an energy label with an on-mode power figure in watts and an energy consumption figure in kWh per 1,000 hours, and the second is just the first times 1,000 divided by 1,000, so either will do. The sum is:

Watts, divided by 1,000, times hours a day, times 365, times 26.32p, which is the Ofgem cap unit rate for electricity paid by Direct Debit from 1 October to 31 December 2026. A 100 W set on four hours a day: 0.1 times 4 times 365 is 146 kWh, times 26.32p is £38.43 a year. A 50 W set on the same hours: £19.21. An 180 W set on six hours a day: 394 kWh, £103.

Two things the label will not tell you. First, the on-mode figure is measured in the standard picture mode with automatic brightness on; the vivid or dynamic preset that sets are shipped in for showrooms can draw 50 to 100 per cent more, and OLED sets showing bright content draw more than their label suggests. Second, HDR content, gaming at high frame rates and a soundbar all add to the real draw. If you want the real number, a £15 plug-in energy monitor between the wall and the TV will tell you in an evening, and that is the one purchase in this article that is worth making before any other.

Read the table two ways. Down the running-cost column, the step from 40 to 65 inches is £26 a year, and from 50 to 75 is £36, which over a ten year life is £260 to £360: real money, and money you are choosing to spend for a bigger picture, which is a perfectly good reason. Across the payback column, a better letter at the same size is £1 to £7 a year, and a £100 premium for it takes 15 to 70 years. The label is a fair way to choose between two otherwise identical sets; it is not a reason to pay more.

The last row is where the free money is. Sets ship in a showroom mode; switching to standard, cinema or filmmaker mode with the ambient light sensor on typically brings the real draw back to the label figure and improves the picture for most rooms. It takes a minute and is worth more than any label class.

4. The £45 standby figure and where it actually sits

The Energy Saving Trust's £45 a year for switching devices off standby is a whole-house figure, not a TV figure, and it is larger than a 60-inch TV's running cost. Modern televisions themselves draw under half a watt in standby under EU and UK rules, about £1 a year, so the £45 is not mostly the set. It is what sits around it: a set-top box that stays warm to record, a games console in rest mode, a soundbar, a streaming stick, an old amplifier, plus the microwave clock, the printer and the chargers left in around the house.

The TV stack is the biggest single share because it is where the highest-standby devices cluster, and it is the one place a switched extension lead or a smart plug recovers most of the £45 with a single action. What not to switch: anything with a recording schedule you rely on, and anything that takes minutes to boot and update. We cover the plugs themselves separately; for this article the point is that if you are worried about what the TV costs, the box under it is a bigger and cheaper target.

5. Worked example: Yusuf in Bradford

Family of four, a 50-inch set in the living room on about five hours a day, a 32-inch in the kitchen on two, a console and a set-top box under the big one, shopping for a 65-inch replacement

Yusuf has two 65-inch sets in front of him, one F-rated with an on-mode figure of 125 W, one E-rated at 110 W and £120 dearer. He wants to know if the E pays for itself.

The sum on each. 125 W at five hours: 228 kWh, £60 a year. 110 W at five hours: 201 kWh, £53 a year. Saving £7 a year. £120 divided by £7 is 17 years. Buy on picture and price, not on the letter.

The sum on the size. His current 50-inch at about 75 W and five hours costs £36 a year. The 65-inch will cost £53 to £60. The upgrade costs £17 to £24 a year to run, £170 to £240 over ten years, which he decides is worth it for the picture and now knows the price of.

The free part. The new set arrives in vivid mode. He switches it to standard with the light sensor on, which brings the real draw back to the label. He puts the console and set-top box on a switched extension lead and turns it off at night, which on the Energy Saving Trust's figure is worth most of £45 a year across the house, more than the TV itself costs to run.

6. The honest verdict

Price your TV with the three-line sum, watts times hours times 26.32p, and you will find what the Energy Saving Trust's figures show: a 60-inch set costs about £40 a year at four hours a day, a 40-inch about £20, and the size sets the bill. Buy the size you will watch and know that each 10 inches is roughly £8 to £12 a year more. Do not pay a premium for a better energy letter at the same size; the saving is £1 to £7 a year and the payback is 15 to 70 years.

Then do the two free things: take the set out of vivid mode, and put the boxes under it on a switch. Between them they are worth more than any choice you can make in the shop.

Frequently asked

How much does a TV cost to run per year in the UK? The Energy Saving Trust, page updated 3 August 2026, puts a 60-inch F-rated TV at £40 a year in Great Britain and £50 in Northern Ireland, and a 40-inch F-rated TV at £20 and £25. For your own set, take the on-mode watts from the label, divide by 1,000, multiply by hours a day, by 365, and by 26.32p, the October 2026 cap rate.

Is an A-rated or E-rated TV worth paying more for? Rarely on running cost. At the same screen size one label class is roughly a 10 per cent difference in wattage, which is £1 to £7 a year at four hours a day depending on size. A £100 premium takes 15 to 70 years to pay back. Choose the letter only between otherwise identical sets.

Does a bigger TV cost much more to run? Yes, and more than the rating does. On Energy Saving Trust figures a 60-inch set costs double a 40-inch at the same rating. Each 10 inches is roughly £8 to £12 a year at four hours a day. A 75-inch set can cost £60 or more a year against £20 for a 40-inch.

How much does standby cost? The Energy Saving Trust puts switching devices off standby at £45 a year in Great Britain and £55 in Northern Ireland across the whole house. A modern TV itself draws under half a watt in standby; most of the £45 is set-top boxes, consoles, soundbars, streaming sticks and chargers, so the TV stack on a switched lead recovers a large share of it.
