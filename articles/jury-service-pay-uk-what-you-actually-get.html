Jury Service Does Not Pay You. It Reimburses You, Up to 64.95 Pounds a Day

A jury summons arrives with a legal obligation attached and no salary. Your employer can choose whether to keep paying you, and if they do not, the court will make up part of the gap against a published daily ceiling. Whether that ceiling is a problem depends entirely on what you earn and how long the trial runs.

The short version

  • You are not paid for jury service. You claim money back if your earnings are affected, which is not the same thing.
  • £64.95 a day is the ceiling on loss of earnings and care costs for the first 10 days, if you spend more than 4 hours at court. It is £32.47 for 4 hours or less.
  • After 10 working days the ceiling doubles to £129.91 a day over 4 hours, and £64.95 for 4 hours or less.
  • Food and drink is £5.71 a day, rising to £12.17 if you are there more than 10 hours.
  • Car mileage is 31.4p a mile, bicycle 9.6p, and there is 4.2p a mile extra for carrying one other juror.
  • Your employer must give you the time off but can choose whether to pay you, and can ask you to apply to delay if your absence would seriously affect the business.

1. Unpaid is the starting point

GOV.UK does not soften it: "You will not be paid for doing jury service, but you can claim some money back if you lose earnings."

That distinction runs through everything below. Jury service is not a job with a low rate of pay. It is an unpaid civic duty with a partial reimbursement scheme attached, and the scheme is capped per day rather than set as a proportion of what you earn. Whether the caps are generous or painful is entirely a function of your own pay.

The basic shape of the obligation: the summons comes from the electoral register, you must respond within 7 days, jury service usually lasts up to 10 working days, and you will usually be at court from 10am to 5:30pm Monday to Friday, though times vary and the first day starts earlier.

Scotland and Northern Ireland run their own jury systems with their own rules, and the figures in this guide are the England and Wales ones.

2. The daily ceilings, and the ten day cliff

The loss of earnings claim covers both lost pay and the cost of any care or childcare outside your usual arrangements. There is one pot, not two, and it is capped per day.

For the first 10 days of jury service:

  • up to £64.95 a day if you spend more than 4 hours at court
  • up to £32.47 a day if you spend 4 hours or less

If your jury service lasts longer than 10 working days:

  • up to £129.91 a day if you spend more than 4 hours at court
  • up to £64.95 a day if you spend 4 hours or less

Two things follow that are not obvious from the numbers alone.

The four hour split is about time at court, not about the trial. A day where the jury is sent home at lunchtime, which happens often, can halve that day's ceiling even though you could not work that morning either.

The doubling at day eleven is the single most important feature of the scheme. It exists because a long trial turns a manageable inconvenience into a genuine income problem, and it means the financial shape of a three week trial is very different from three separate weeks. If you are on a long trial, check that the higher rate is being applied from the eleventh working day.

The word up to is doing real work as well. You claim what you actually lost, up to the ceiling. Someone whose employer pays them in full has lost nothing and claims nothing under this heading, though travel and food are separate.

3. Travel and food, at the published rates

These are paid regardless of whether you lost earnings, and the rates are fixed.

Travel. Bus or underground is the cost of the ticket. Train is the cost of the ticket at the standard class return fare. Bicycle is 9.6p per mile. Motorcycle is 31.4p per mile. Car is 31.4p per mile, and you should check whether the court will pay for parking. Carrying one other juror as a passenger adds 4.2p per mile, and each additional passenger adds 3.2p per mile. A taxi is the fare, but you must ask the court for permission before using one.

Food and drink. £5.71 per day up to and including 10 hours, and £12.17 per day over 10 hours.

Two limits stated plainly on the page: you can only claim for travel inside England and Wales, and you cannot claim for accommodation.

The passenger rates are the one genuinely actionable item here. Two jurors from the same town travelling separately claim 31.4p a mile each. Travelling together, the driver claims 31.4p plus 4.2p and the passenger claims nothing, so the pair is worse off in total. That is worth knowing before anyone offers a lift out of politeness.

4. Worked example: Callum in Merthyr Tydfil

Callum earns £28,000 a year, which is roughly £107 a day before tax on a five day week. His employer's policy is not to pay during jury service. The court is 14 miles away and he drives.

Days 1 to 10, full days. His lost pay is about £107 a day, but the ceiling is £64.95. He claims the ceiling. The shortfall is about £42 a day, so roughly £420 across two weeks.

Travel. 28 miles round trip at 31.4p is £8.79 a day, so about £88 over ten days, plus parking if the court pays it.

Food. £5.71 a day, so £57.10 over ten days.

Net position after ten days. He is down roughly £420 on pay, and his travel and food are reimbursed. The honest summary is that jury service costs him about two fifths of a fortnight's wages.

If the trial runs to four weeks. From the eleventh working day the ceiling becomes £129.91, which is above his £107 daily pay, so from day eleven he can claim his actual loss in full and the shortfall stops growing. His total loss stays at about £420 rather than tripling. That is the whole point of the doubling, and it is why the length of the trial matters more than the fact of it.

If he were on benefits. GOV.UK says to show the summons to your benefit office or work coach as soon as you get it, and that financial support such as Universal Credit continues for the first 8 weeks, after which the court provides a loss of earnings form to hand in.

5. Your employer, and what they can and cannot do

They must give you the time off. Give them a copy of the summons.

They can ask you to apply to delay it if your absence would have a serious effect on the business. Note the shape of that: they ask you to apply, and the court decides. An employer cannot themselves postpone your jury service.

They choose whether to pay you. GOV.UK: "Your employer can choose whether or not to pay you during your service." There is no statutory jury service pay. Check your contract and staff handbook, because many employers do pay, some pay the difference between salary and the court allowance, and the ones that pay nothing are within their rights.

If they refuse the time off, that is an employment tribunal matter, and if you are sacked for doing jury service you may be able to claim unfair dismissal. Both routes are named on the GOV.UK page.

The practical sequence, then, is: respond to the summons within 7 days, hand the employer a copy the same week, ask in writing whether they will pay and at what rate, and only then work out what the shortfall will be. If the shortfall is unmanageable, an application to defer is a separate conversation with the court, not with your employer.

The honest verdict

Jury service is financially neutral for people whose employers pay them and for people earning under about £65 a day, and it is a real cost for everyone in between. The cap is £64.95 a day for the first ten days and £129.91 after that, and the doubling is the feature worth knowing about.

The single most useful thing to do on the day the summons arrives is to ask your employer in writing whether they will pay you, because everything else follows from that answer. If they will, there is nothing to claim beyond travel and food. If they will not, you can work out the shortfall in five minutes with the figures above.

Where this does not help you: it will not get you out of jury service, and deferral is decided by the court rather than argued on cost alone. And the figures here are England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate schemes.

Frequently asked

Can I be sacked for doing jury service? You should not be, and GOV.UK points to an unfair dismissal claim if you are. If an employer will not allow the time off at all, that is a matter for an employment tribunal. Keep the summons and any written refusal.

What if I am self-employed? There is a separate GOV.UK page for self-employed jurors, and the claim works differently because the loss is business income rather than wages. The daily ceilings are the same structure, but the evidence required is not, so read that page rather than this section.

Do I get the money during the trial or afterwards? GOV.UK says you will be told how to claim expenses after your jury service has ended, so plan on the basis that the reimbursement arrives afterwards rather than weekly. That cash flow gap matters more than the cap for some households.

Is there a calculator? Yes. GOV.UK links to an expense calculator on the jury summons service, which will estimate what you can claim from your own figures. It is the quickest way to turn the ceilings above into your own number.
