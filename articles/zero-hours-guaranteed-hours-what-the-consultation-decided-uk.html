The Right to Guaranteed Hours Exists. Whether You Have It Is Still a Blank in a Document

The consultation that fills in the details closed at 11:59pm on 25 August 2026 and the government is analysing the responses. Three new rights are already written into the Employment Rights Act 2025. None of them has taken effect, and the number that decides who is covered has not been set.

Ruhul Amin Published 10 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Three rights are in the Employment Rights Act 2025: guaranteed hours reflecting hours actually worked, reasonable notice of shifts and changes, and payment when a shift is cancelled, moved or curtailed at short notice.
  • None of them has taken effect. GOV.UK's own wording on the consultation page is that these measures have not yet taken effect.
  • The consultation ran from 9:30am on 2 June 2026 to 11:59pm on 25 August 2026 and the Department for Business and Trade is now analysing responses.
  • The detail lives in regulations, not the Act. The reference period, the hours threshold and the eligibility rules are all still to be written.
  • The government's stated preference is a 12 week initial reference period for working out the guaranteed hours offer.
  • It does not ban zero hours contracts. It changes who carries the risk when the hours do not come.
  • It applies to England, Scotland and Wales. Employment law is not devolved in the same way in Northern Ireland.

1. The three rights, in plain terms

The government's manifesto committed to ending what it calls one sided flexibility, meaning arrangements where the worker absorbs all the risk of uncertain demand. The Employment Rights Act 2025 delivers that through three measures.

A right to guaranteed hours. Where a qualifying worker has regularly worked a certain number of hours over a reference period, they get a right to be offered a contract guaranteeing hours that reflect what they actually worked. The point is to stop a contract saying zero when the reality has been twenty for a year.

A right to reasonable notice of shifts and changes to them. If an employer schedules shifts with unreasonable notice, the worker can bring a tribunal claim.

A right to payment when a shift is cancelled, curtailed or moved at short notice. At the moment, a shift cancelled at 6am costs the worker a day's pay and costs the employer nothing. That is the imbalance this is aimed at.

All three sit in the Act. Not one of them is currently in force.

2. The blank that decides everything

The Act creates the rights. Regulations decide who has them, and the regulations do not exist yet.

Three things in particular are still open. The reference period, over which your usual hours are measured, where the government has said it prefers 12 weeks. The hours threshold, which determines which low hours workers are covered at all. And the detailed eligibility rules for each of the three rights.

That hours threshold is not a technicality. It is the difference between a reform that reaches a few hundred thousand people and one that reaches millions, and it has already caused confusion inside government's own documents. The consultation page was updated on 14 July 2026 specifically to explain the hours threshold criteria accurately and consistently, in both the guaranteed hours part and the reasonable notice part. When the department has to correct its own explanation mid-consultation, that is a sign of how much is still unsettled.

On 12 August 2026 the department added a substantial analytical package to the consultation: separate options assessments running to 105, 87 and 112 pages on the three rights, plus research papers on zero hours contracts and on the experiences of workers in insecure work. Publishing that volume of options analysis two weeks before a consultation closes tells you the decision was genuinely open.

A right that exists in an Act but not in regulations is not something you can rely on at work tomorrow. It is something to watch, and to prepare records for.

3. What this does not do

It does not ban zero hours contracts. The government's framing is about ending one sided flexibility, not ending flexibility. A zero hours contract will still be lawful.

It does not give you a guaranteed hours contract automatically today. Nothing has commenced. If your employer is not offering one, that is not currently a breach of anything.

It does not fix pay rates. It fixes the number of hours you can count on, not what each hour is worth.

It does not currently cover agency workers under the same instrument. The application of these measures to agency workers has been consulted on separately.

It has no published commencement date in these documents. Anyone quoting you a firm start date is working from a forecast rather than from the consultation page, which as of September 2026 says only that the government is analysing feedback and will develop final policy positions.

Worked example: Nadia Okonjo in Bolton

Nadia works in a warehouse on a zero hours contract. Over the last twelve weeks her hours were: 24, 26, 22, 28, 6, 20, 25, 23, 18, 27, 22 and 23. That averages 22 hours a week.

What a 12 week reference period would mean for her: if the regulations land on the government's stated preference, an offer would have to reflect hours in that range rather than zero. Her contract would say something close to 22 where it currently says nothing.

Why that one bad week matters so much: in the week she got 6 hours she earned roughly a quarter of a normal week. Under a 22 hour guarantee, that week could not have happened the same way. The variance, not the average, is what makes a zero hours income impossible to budget against.

What she cannot do yet: ask for the contract. The right is not in force. Asking now gets a lawful no.

What she can usefully do now: keep her own record of hours worked each week, by date. Whatever reference period the regulations choose, the evidence of what she actually worked will be the thing that decides her offer, and rotas have a habit of disappearing from workplace systems.

4. The honest verdict

The direction of travel is real and it is significant. A right to be offered hours that match the hours you actually work would change the economics of insecure work more than any single measure in a decade.

But the honest position in September 2026 is that none of it has started. The Act is passed, the consultation is closed, the responses are being analysed, and the number that decides whether you personally are covered has not been chosen. Nobody can tell you today whether you will qualify, because the criterion does not exist yet.

So the only useful action is preparation, and it costs nothing: write your hours down every week, with dates, somewhere your employer does not control. If and when this commences, that record is your case.

Frequently asked

When will this start? No commencement date is given on the consultation page. The government has said it will develop final policy positions following consultation, and the rights will then need regulations.

Will I have to accept a guaranteed hours contract? The mechanism in the Act is an offer of guaranteed hours. The detail of how offers work is among the things regulations will set out, so this is one to check when they are published.

Does it apply to agency workers? The application of these measures to agency workers was consulted on separately. Check that consultation rather than assuming the same rules.

Does it apply in Northern Ireland? This consultation applies to England, Scotland and Wales. Northern Ireland has its own employment law framework.
