Stamp duty for first-time buyers: nil to £300,000, and the pound that costs you £5,000

First-time buyer relief is real money, but it is capped at £5,000 of tax saved and it disappears completely one pound above £500,000. Here is the whole table, four worked purchase prices, and the parts estate agents skate over.

Ruhul Amin Published 6 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • No stamp duty to £300,000 if you and everyone buying with you are first-time buyers, then 5% on the slice from £300,001 to £500,000.
  • The relief is worth £5,000 at most. That is the entire tax a non first-time buyer would pay on the first £300,000. Above that price you are paying the same 5% everyone else pays.
  • Above £500,000 you get nothing at all. A £500,000 purchase costs a first-time buyer £10,000. A £500,001 purchase costs £15,000.05. One extra pound of price, £5,000 of extra tax.
  • England and Northern Ireland only. Scotland has Land and Buildings Transaction Tax, Wales has Land Transaction Tax, and both have their own thresholds.
  • Standard rates from 2 September 2026: nil to £125,000, 2% to £250,000, 5% to £925,000, 10% to £1.5 million, 12% above.

1. What you pay if this is not your first home

Stamp Duty Land Tax is charged in slices, not on the whole price. Each slice of the purchase price is taxed at its own rate, and you add the slices together. These are the rates on GOV.UK as at 2 September 2026, for a property that will be the only residential property you own.

Slice of the priceRate
Up to £125,0000%
£125,001 to £250,0002%
£250,001 to £925,0005%
£925,001 to £1.5 million10%
Above £1.5 million12%

GOV.UK's own worked example on that page: a house at £295,000 is nil on the first £125,000, £2,500 on the second £125,000, and £2,250 on the final £45,000. Total £4,750.

Two things follow from the slice structure that people get wrong constantly. First, going one pound over a threshold does not re-tax the whole price. At £250,001 you pay 5% on one pound, not 5% on a quarter of a million. Second, there is no way to reduce the bill by splitting the price between the house and the carpets. HMRC prices fixtures separately and expects the figures to be honest.

2. The first-time buyer discount, and what it is really worth

If you are buying your first home, and so is everyone else named on the purchase, you get relief. GOV.UK sets it out in one line: no stamp duty up to £300,000, then 5% on the portion from £300,001 to £500,000. If the price is over £500,000 you cannot claim the relief at all and you follow the standard table above.

Now the part that rarely gets said out loud. Work out what a non first-time buyer would pay on the first £300,000 of any purchase: nil on the first £125,000, £2,500 on the next £125,000, and £2,500 on the slice from £250,001 to £300,000. That is £5,000. So £5,000 is the entire benefit of first-time buyer relief, at every price from £300,000 up to £500,000. Above £300,000 you are paying the identical 5% that everybody else pays on that slice. The relief does not scale with the price. It caps.

Here is the same point as a table. Every figure is the slice arithmetic applied to the GOV.UK rates.

Purchase priceFirst-time buyerEveryone elseYou save
£200,000£0£1,500£1,500
£250,000£0£2,500£2,500
£300,000£0£5,000£5,000

Read the last two rows together. A first-time buyer at £500,000 saves £5,000. A first-time buyer at £520,000 saves nothing, and the tax bill has gone from £10,000 to £16,000 on a £20,000 increase in price.

One more condition that catches people. The relief goes if anyone named on the purchase has owned a residential property anywhere in the world before, including an inherited share and including a property owned years ago and sold. It is not "have you had a mortgage before". It is "have you ever owned".

3. The £500,000 cliff, in pounds

Almost every threshold in the tax system is a slice. This one is not. At £500,000 exactly, a first-time buyer pays 5% on the £200,000 above £300,000, which is £10,000. At £500,001 the relief is gone entirely and the standard table applies from the first pound: nil on £125,000, £2,500 on the next £125,000, and 5% on £250,001, which is £12,500.05. Total £15,000.05.

So one extra pound of purchase price costs £5,000.05 in extra tax. There is no taper and no partial relief. The practical consequence: if you are negotiating anywhere near £500,000, the difference between agreeing £499,995 and agreeing £505,000 is not £5,005 of price. It is £5,005 of price plus £5,250 of tax, so a shade over £10,000 out of your pocket. That is worth saying to an estate agent in exactly those words.

The same logic runs in reverse for sellers. A property being marketed at £510,000 to first-time buyers is competing with everything under £500,000 at a £5,000 handicap it did not choose. If your buyer is a first-time buyer and the deal is stuck, the number that unsticks it is £500,000, not £505,000.

4. A worked example: Nadia and Tom in Colchester

Nadia and Tom have neither owned a property before. They have agreed £352,000 on a three-bedroom terrace in Colchester and they have a 10% deposit of £35,200.

Their stamp duty with first-time buyer relief: nil on the first £300,000, then 5% on £52,000, which is £2,600.

Without the relief they would have paid nil on £125,000, £2,500 on the next £125,000, and 5% on £102,000, which is £5,100. Total £7,600. The relief has saved them £5,000, exactly as it does at every price in the £300,000 to £500,000 band.

The £2,600 comes out of cash, not out of the mortgage. Lenders will not add stamp duty to the loan. So the real cash they need at completion is the £35,200 deposit, the £2,600 of stamp duty, and their legal and survey costs on top. When people say they have "saved the deposit", the stamp duty is the bit that is usually still missing.

If the same house had been priced at £501,000, their bill would have been £15,050 rather than £10,050, and it would have been the single largest cost of moving.

5. The surcharges and the bits that catch people out

The additional property surcharge is 5%, on top. If buying means you will own more than one residential property, GOV.UK adds 5% to every slice, including the nil slice. That turns a £250,000 purchase from £2,500 into £15,000. It applies even if the other property is abroad, and even if it is a share you inherited.

Replacing your main residence is the exception. You do not pay the extra 5% if the property you are buying replaces your main residence and your previous main residence was sold within 36 months of completing the new purchase. If you have not sold on the day you complete, you pay the surcharge and then claim a refund.

The non-resident surcharge is a further 2%. If you were not present in the UK for at least 183 days in the 12 months before the purchase, GOV.UK treats you as not UK resident for stamp duty and adds 2% on residential property in England or Northern Ireland. It stacks on top of everything else, including first-time buyer relief if that still applies.

This is not a UK-wide tax. Stamp Duty Land Tax covers England and Northern Ireland. Scotland charges Land and Buildings Transaction Tax and Wales charges Land Transaction Tax, and both have different thresholds and different first-time buyer rules. If you are buying in Glasgow or Cardiff, none of the numbers above apply to you.

You do not usually pay it yourself. Your conveyancer files the return and pays HMRC out of the completion funds. That is convenient, and it is also why so many buyers only find out the size of the bill when the completion statement lands. Ask for the figure in writing before you exchange.

The verdict

First-time buyer relief is worth having and worth claiming, but it is worth a flat £5,000 at any price between £300,000 and £500,000, not a percentage that grows with the house. Budget the cash separately from your deposit, because no lender will finance it. And if your search is running into the £490,000 to £520,000 range, treat £500,000 as a hard ceiling rather than a round number, because the pound above it costs £5,000. Every figure here is from the GOV.UK residential rates page as at 2 September 2026 and applies to England and Northern Ireland only.

Frequently asked

Does first-time buyer relief apply if only one of us is a first-time buyer? No. GOV.UK requires that you and anyone else you are buying with are all first-time buyers. If one buyer has owned a residential property before, the whole purchase falls under the standard rates. Buying in one name only to preserve the relief has consequences for ownership and for the mortgage, so take advice before going near it.

Can I add the stamp duty to my mortgage? Not directly. Lenders size the loan against the property value, not against your costs, so stamp duty has to come from cash. Some buyers borrow a slightly larger amount by putting down a smaller deposit, but that raises your loan to value band and usually your rate, so you pay for it over the whole term.

What counts as having owned a property before? Any freehold or leasehold interest in a residential property, anywhere in the world, at any point. An inherited share counts. A property you owned and sold twenty years ago counts. Renting does not count, and neither does living in a property owned by a parent.

Is the £500,000 limit going to change at the Budget? It could. The Budget is on Wednesday 28 October 2026 and stamp duty thresholds have been changed at short notice before. Nothing here is a forecast: the figures on this page are the rules as they stand on 6 September 2026, and we will update the page if the Budget moves them.
