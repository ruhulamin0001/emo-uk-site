You Cannot Replace Your Landlord's Windows. Film Pays Back In A Winter, Magnetic Panels Take A Decade, And Both Leave With You.

A tenant with single-glazed windows has three options that need no permission: a shrink-fit film that costs less than a takeaway, a magnetic secondary glazing panel that costs as much as a month's council tax per window, or a jumper. This article prices the first two against the only reliable number that exists, the Energy Saving Trust's figure for what replacing the windows entirely would save, and sets out what your landlord is obliged to do before you spend anything.

The short version

  • The ceiling: the Energy Saving Trust says replacing every single-glazed window in a semi with A-rated double glazing saves £85 a year in Great Britain. No film or panel fitted inside those windows can save more than that, and most get a third to a half of it.
  • Shrink-fit secondary glazing film costs £5 to £15 a window and pays back in the first winter on single glazing. It lasts one season, looks like cling film because it is, and the Energy Saving Trust calls it the least effective option, which is still enough at that price.
  • Magnetic or clip-on secondary glazing panels cost £80 to £250 a window and, on our bounded estimate of £5 to £8 a year each on single glazing, pay back in 10 to 50 years. They are a comfort and noise purchase you can take to the next flat.

  • The order that pays: draught strips on the window frame, then film on single glazing, then a lined curtain you were buying anyway. Magnetic panels only for a room you sit in every evening with a single-glazed window facing a road.

1. The one number that bounds everything

Nobody has independently measured what a magnetic secondary glazing panel or a roll of shrink film saves in a British home, and the retailers' percentages are their own. What has been measured, by the Energy Saving Trust on its windows page updated 20 May 2026 and read again on 4 September 2026, is the saving from the best possible window upgrade: replacing all the single glazing in a semi-detached house with A-rated double glazing saves £85 a year in Great Britain, at July 2026 fuel prices.

That is the ceiling. Anything you stick on the inside of a single-glazed window is trying to do what a sealed double-glazed unit does, with a worse seal, a thinner air gap and a material that is not low-emissivity glass. The Energy Saving Trust lists three types of secondary glazing, in order: film, which "offers the least thermal benefits"; magnetic or clip-fit panels, which "can help reduce heat loss and can be easily removed"; and permanent framed secondary glazing, which it says can be more efficient than replacing the windows but which a tenant cannot fit.

Our working ranges, bounded by that £85 for a whole semi of perhaps ten windows: film on a single-glazed window, £3 to £5 a year; a well-fitted magnetic panel, £5 to £8. On double glazing, halve both. These are estimates and we say so; the point is not the decimal, it is that the numbers are single figures per window, which decides the payback on its own.

If you heat with electricity rather than gas, as many flats do, the same heat costs about three times as much at the October 2026 cap, 26.32p against 7.97p per unit, and every saving above roughly triples. Tenants in all-electric flats are the one group for whom the panels come close to paying back.

Two things fall out. The cheap sealed options, draught strip and film, pay back inside a winter or two because they cost almost nothing and they seal. The expensive removable option, the magnetic panel, pays back over a decade or more on energy and is bought for something else: it is the only one that noticeably cuts noise from a road, and the only one that survives being taken down and put up again.

Film's weakness is not performance, it is the look and the single season. A tenant who does not mind a window that reads as slightly hazy for five months has the best energy buy on the list. A tenant who does should go to the magnetic panel and accept that it is a comfort purchase.

3. What the landlord must do, and what you may do

The minimum standard. In England and Wales a landlord cannot let a property with an Energy Performance Certificate below E without a registered exemption, and the certificate is public: look it up on the government's find-an-energy-certificate service by postcode. An F or G rating with no exemption is a breach, and the local authority enforces it. The rules on raising that floor have been consulted on repeatedly; check the current position before assuming anything above E is required.

Repairs are theirs. A window that does not close, a broken sash cord, a missing pane, a rotten frame that lets rain in: these are disrepair, and the landlord must fix them under the repairing obligations in the tenancy and the law behind it. Draughts through a window that closes properly are not disrepair, and that is where you come in.

What you may fit without asking. Anything that leaves the window as you found it: self-adhesive draught strip, shrink film taped to the frame, a magnetic panel whose steel strip peels off, curtains on an existing rail. The safe rule is that if removing it needs nothing more than your fingernails and a cloth, it is yours to do. Anything that needs a screw or leaves a mark, ask in writing and keep the reply.

What to ask for first. Before spending your own money, ask the landlord for draught proofing of the windows and doors as a repair-adjacent improvement. Many will say yes to £30 of strip rather than argue, and some will fit it. Ask for the heating to be serviced and the thermostat to work. Those cost you nothing and they are the bigger numbers.

4. Fitting it so it actually works, and taking it with you

Film. Clean the frame, run double-sided tape around the frame not the glass, stretch the film over it, then shrink it with a hairdryer until the wrinkles go. The seal is the whole point; a film with a gap at one corner is decoration. Do it on a dry day in October and take it off in April, and buy the kit with enough film for every single-glazed window in the flat, because the second window is cheaper than the first.

Magnetic panels. Measure the reveal, order the acrylic cut to size with the magnetic tape and the steel strip, and fit the strip to a clean frame. The panel should sit inside the reveal with an air gap of at least 20 mm to the glass; closer than that and it does little. Mark the panel with which window it came from, because the next flat will not have the same sizes, and keep the box.

Condensation. Both options can leave condensation trapped between the panel and the cold glass in a bathroom or a bedroom where someone breathes all night. That is not a fault of the product; it is water that would otherwise be on the glass. Leave a trickle vent open, and if there is none, take the panel down every few weeks to wipe the glass. Do not seal the window shut in a room with a gas appliance.

Moving out. Film comes off with the tape and a bit of patience; the tape residue is the only thing that could cost you at check-out, so use the tape the kit supplies and remove it slowly. Magnetic strips peel off uPVC cleanly and off painted timber less cleanly; test a corner first. Photograph every window before and after so the inventory cannot argue.

5. Worked example: Owen in Cardiff

One-bed flat in a converted Victorian house, single-glazed sash windows, electric panel heaters, twelve-month tenancy

Owen's two front windows face the road, rattle in the wind and run with condensation. The flat is all-electric, so his heat costs 26.32p a unit, and the EPC is a D.

Draught strip first. Two sashes, brush strip along the meeting rail and the bottom rail, about £12. Our saving estimate, tripled for electric heating: £6 to £12 a year per window. Payback inside a winter.

Film on both. One kit, about £12 for two windows. Saving, tripled: £9 to £15 a year per window. Payback inside a winter, and the rattling stops because the film damps it.

Magnetic panels instead of film. Two panels, about £300. Saving, tripled: £15 to £24 a year per window, so £30 to £48 a year for the pair. Payback 6 to 10 years, which is five to nine tenancies. The road noise drops noticeably, which is the reason he would buy them.

Decision. Strip and film this winter, about £24, saving perhaps £30 to £50 over the season and paying back before Christmas. He asks the landlord in writing to fit draught strips to the back windows and to look at the sash cords, which are disrepair. If he is still there in a year and the road noise still bothers him, the panels are a comfort purchase he can price then.

6. The honest verdict

If you rent a home with single glazing, fit draught strip and shrink film this autumn: together they cost under £20 a window, need no permission, and pay back before the winter is out on the Energy Saving Trust's bounded figures. That is the whole energy case, and it is a good one.

Do not buy magnetic secondary glazing panels to save money; at £80 to £250 a window against £5 to £8 a year, they take a decade or more to pay back on gas heating and several years even on electric. Buy them, if at all, for road noise and for a room you sit in every evening, and buy them knowing they may not fit the next flat. And before any of it, look up the EPC and ask the landlord in writing for the draught proofing and the repairs that are theirs.

Frequently asked

Does window insulation film actually work? On single glazing, yes, modestly: it adds a sealed air gap, which is what double glazing does with glass. Our bounded estimate is £3 to £5 a year per window on gas heating, roughly triple on electric, against a kit cost of £5 to £15. It lasts one winter.

Is magnetic secondary glazing worth it for a rented flat? Not on energy alone: £80 to £250 a window against £5 to £8 a year is a 10 to 50 year payback. It is worth it for road noise in a room you use every evening, and it can be taken to the next home if the windows are a similar size.

Can my landlord stop me fitting window film or secondary glazing? Not in practice for anything that leaves no mark: self-adhesive strip, taped film and peel-off magnetic strips are all removable. Anything needing screws or leaving marks needs written permission. Check the tenancy agreement for a clause on alterations.

What energy rating must a rented home have? In England and Wales a landlord cannot let a property rated F or G on its EPC without a registered exemption. The certificate is public on the government's find-an-energy-certificate service. Proposals to raise the minimum have been consulted on; check the current rule rather than assuming.
