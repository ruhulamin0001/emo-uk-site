HMRC Has Two Years Of Vinted And eBay Data And Is Writing To Sellers. What The Letter Is, What It Is Not, And The Free Route Back.

Since 1 January 2024 every UK online marketplace has had to hand HMRC the details of sellers who cross a low threshold, and HMRC now holds two full years of it. It has been using that data to send One to Many letters, the polite kind that say "we have information that suggests". If one lands on your mat this autumn, the worst thing to do is ignore it and the second worst is to panic. Here is what the letter is, who actually owes something, and the free way to sort it out.

The short version

  • Platforms report you to HMRC if you make 30 or more sales, or receive about £1,700 (2,000 euros) in a calendar year. The rule has applied since 1 January 2024; data for 2025 reached HMRC by 31 January 2026.
  • HMRC's One to Many letters on online marketplace sales began in February 2025, aimed at people it suspects have undeclared income for years up to 5 April 2023. Source: Chartered Institute of Taxation, 25 February 2025. Tax advisers expect a further wave once HMRC has matched the 2025 data.
  • A letter is not an enquiry, not a penalty and not a bill. It asks you to check. Being reported by a platform does not mean you owe tax, and HMRC's own guidance says so.
  • Selling your own possessions is not trading. Clearing a wardrobe or a loft is outside income tax however many items it takes, unless a single item sells for more than £6,000, when capital gains rules can apply.
  • Trading, buying to resell or making to sell, is taxable above the £1,000 trading allowance, and needs a Self Assessment registration by 5 October after the tax year.
  • The route back is free: the Digital Disclosure Service on GOV.UK, with HMRC's own calculators for tax, interest and penalties. Coming forward before HMRC opens an enquiry keeps penalties at the lowest end.

1. What HMRC actually holds, and since when

The Platform Operators (Due Diligence and Reporting Requirements) Regulations 2023 brought the OECD's model rules into UK law from 1 January 2024. Vinted, eBay, Etsy, Depop, Amazon, Airbnb, Uber and every other digital platform with UK sellers must collect and report to HMRC the name, address, date of birth, National Insurance number where held, bank account and quarterly gross takings of any seller who, in a calendar year, makes 30 or more sales or receives at least 2,000 euros, around £1,700. The platform must also give the seller a copy of what it reported, by 31 January of the following year.

So HMRC received 2024 data by 31 January 2025 and 2025 data by 31 January 2026. Tax firms working from HMRC's figures put the 2025 batch at close to four million seller reports; HMRC itself has not published a headline number, so treat that as an adviser estimate rather than an official one. What is certain is that HMRC's system now has two years of platform takings to set against Self Assessment returns.

2. What a One to Many letter is, and is not

One to Many is HMRC's name for a standard letter sent to a group of taxpayers whose data suggests a common issue. The Chartered Institute of Taxation reported on 25 February 2025 that HMRC had begun a One to Many campaign "directed at individuals whom they suspect are operating in the hidden economy and not reporting their income", based on online marketplace data, covering the period up to the tax year ending 5 April 2023. The letter tells the recipient that HMRC holds information about their online income, and asks them either to make a disclosure through the GOV.UK guidance "Tell HMRC about underpaid tax from previous years" or to confirm there is nothing to declare.

Three things it is not. It is not a formal enquiry under the Taxes Management Act, so no enquiry window has opened and no information notice has been served. It is not a penalty; nothing has been assessed. And it is not a statement that you owe tax; it is a statement that HMRC has data and wants you to check it against your position.

What it does do is change the penalty position if you do owe something. A disclosure made after HMRC has written to you is "prompted" rather than "unprompted", and prompted disclosures carry higher minimum penalties. That is the real reason to respond rather than file it in a drawer.

3. Who owes something and who does not

HMRC's guidance "Check if you need to tell HMRC about your income from online platforms" draws the line clearly.

Selling personal possessions: clothes you wore, furniture you used, a phone you replaced. HMRC says you "probably do not have to pay Income Tax on these". The number of sales does not turn it into trading; a hundred items from a house clearance is still personal possessions. The only tax that can arise is capital gains tax on a single item, or a set, sold for more than £6,000, which for most sellers means jewellery, art or a collectable, not jumpers.

Trading: buying things to sell on, making things to sell, or selling regularly with a view to profit. That is income. The first £1,000 of gross trading income in a tax year is covered by the trading allowance and needs no return. Above it, you register for Self Assessment by 5 October following the tax year and report the profit. Our side hustle tax guide sets out the badges of trade and the allowance in full.

Services and renting: tutoring, driving, letting a room or a driveway through a platform. Income, with its own allowances, and reportable in the same way.

So the groups who owe nothing and should simply say so: anyone selling their own things, and anyone trading under £1,000 a year. The groups who may owe something: anyone who has been buying stock and selling it, or making and selling, above £1,000 gross, without a return.

4. A worked example in Swindon

Chloe in Swindon receives a letter in October 2026. Vinted reported her for 2024 and 2025: 180 sales in 2024 totalling £2,900, and 240 sales in 2025 totalling £4,100.

If the sales were her own wardrobe and her children's outgrown clothes, that is personal possessions. No income tax, no capital gains tax, nothing to disclose. She replies to the letter saying so, keeps a note of what she sold, and that is the end of it. The 420 sales do not change the answer.

If she had been buying bundles at car boot sales and relisting them, that is trading. For 2024 to 2025, gross takings of about £3,500 across the two calendar years straddling the tax year; say £3,200 fell in the 2024 to 2025 tax year. The trading allowance covers £1,000, leaving £2,200 taxable. As a basic-rate taxpayer with a job, that is £440 of tax. Because the letter arrived before she disclosed, the disclosure is prompted; on a "careless" failure the penalty range starts at 15% of the tax, so around £66, plus late payment interest at 7.75%. Roughly £530 in total on our arithmetic, against £440 had she registered on time, and against potentially far more if HMRC had opened an enquiry and found it first.

She also registers for Self Assessment for 2025 to 2026, because the trade continued, and files by 31 January 2027.

5. The free route: the Digital Disclosure Service

The GOV.UK page "Tell HMRC about underpaid tax from previous years" is the service the letter points to. You sign in with a Government Gateway account, tell HMRC you intend to disclose, and are given 90 days to submit the figures and pay. The page links to HMRC's own tax, interest and penalty calculators for the tax years ending 5 April 2010 to 5 April 2024, so you can see the number before you commit. There is no fee.

If nothing is owed, respond to the letter saying so, briefly and factually: the sales were personal possessions, or the trading income was under £1,000. Keep the platform's copy of what it reported and any records of what you sold.

If you are unsure whether you were trading, HMRC's "check if you need to tell HMRC" tool on GOV.UK walks through the questions, and TaxAid gives free advice to people on low incomes. None of it requires a paid "HMRC letter specialist".

6. The honest verdict

Most people who get one of these letters owe nothing, because selling your own possessions is not income. Reply saying so, keep your records, and stop worrying about the number of sales.

If you were buying or making to sell above £1,000 a year, you owe tax on the profit, and the letter has already made your disclosure a prompted one. Use the free GOV.UK service now, before it becomes an enquiry.

Do not ignore it and do not pay anyone to answer it. The letter is a check, the disclosure service is free, and the calculators tell you the bill before you commit.

Frequently asked

I got a letter but I only sold my own old clothes. Do I owe tax? Almost certainly not. HMRC's guidance says selling personal possessions does not usually attract income tax. Reply to the letter saying that is what the sales were, and keep the platform's report and any records.

Does being reported by Vinted or eBay mean I have to file a tax return? No. The platform reports everyone above 30 sales or about £1,700. Whether you need a return depends on whether you were trading and whether your gross trading income was over £1,000.

What happens if I ignore the letter? HMRC may open a formal enquiry using the platform data. If tax is owed, penalties for a failure found by HMRC are higher than for a disclosure you make yourself, and interest at 7.75% runs from the original due date.

Is the disclosure service free? Yes. The Digital Disclosure Service on GOV.UK is free, and HMRC publishes calculators for the tax, interest and penalties. TaxAid offers free help to people on low incomes.
