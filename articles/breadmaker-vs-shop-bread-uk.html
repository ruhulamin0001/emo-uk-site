A Home Loaf Costs 63p. The Question Is Which Loaf You Stopped Buying

Every breadmaker review compares home baking to the most expensive bread in the shop, which makes the machine look like a bargain. Compare it to what you actually buy and the answer moves by three years. Here is the arithmetic both ways.

1. What a home loaf actually costs, line by line

Electricity. Panasonic's own UK specification page for the SD-B2510 gives power consumption as 505 W to 550 W at 230 volts. A breadmaker does not draw that continuously across a four hour cycle, though: the motor kneads for a few minutes, the heater keeps the dough warm through proving, and the element runs properly only during the bake. Panasonic's own guidance puts a full cycle at roughly 0.4 to 0.6 kWh. We use 0.5 kWh.

At the Ofgem price cap electricity rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 26.32p per kWh, that is 13.16p of electricity a loaf.

Ingredients, for a standard 800g white loaf:

  • 500g strong white bread flour, at about £1.20 for 1.5kg: 40p
  • 7g fast action yeast, from a £1.20 tub of 125g: 5p
  • Salt, sugar and a spoon of oil or butter: 5p

Ingredients total: 50p. Grand total: 63.16p per loaf.

That figure is the whole article, and it is worth noticing how the two halves compare. The electricity is a fifth of the cost and the flour is most of the rest. Which means that if flour prices move, this calculation moves with them, and if electricity prices move it barely notices. Anyone worrying about the running cost of a breadmaker is worrying about the smaller number.

One honest addition. A 500g flour loaf comes out at roughly 750 to 800g baked, so it is genuinely comparable with a standard 800g supermarket loaf. If you are comparing against a 400g small loaf, halve the shop price before you use the table below.

Home baking it costs more in ingredients than wheat bread, at around £1.10 a loaf, but the saving against £2.80 is still £1.70 and the quality difference is considerable. If somebody in the house is coeliac, this is the row to read.

3. The failure mode nobody costs

Every table above assumes you keep using it. The single biggest determinant of whether a breadmaker pays back is not the price of flour, the price of electricity or the machine you choose. It is whether it is still on the worktop in March.

The pattern is extremely consistent. Enthusiastic fortnight, three or four loaves a week. Slowing month, one a week. Then the machine moves off the worktop to make room, into a cupboard, and the number of loaves it makes from a cupboard is zero.

Run the arithmetic on the realistic failure case. A £130 machine used 20 times and then stored has cost £6.50 a loaf, which is twice the price of the bakery sourdough it was bought to replace.

Three things that predict whether it stays in use, none of which appear in any review:

  • Is there worktop space for it to live on permanently? A machine that has to be lifted out and put away gets used a third as often. If your kitchen has no spare 30cm, this is the finding that should decide it.
  • Will you use the timer? The delay timer is the feature that turns baking into a habit, because you load it after dinner and wake to bread. People who use the timer keep baking. People who start the cycle in the morning and wait four hours mostly do not.
  • Do you actually eat two loaves a week? Home bread has no preservatives and stales in two days. A household that eats four slices a day will waste half of every loaf, which destroys the arithmetic more thoroughly than any of the numbers above.

That last point is worth quantifying. If you throw away a third of every loaf, your effective cost per loaf eaten goes from 63.16p to 94.74p, and the payback against standard own-brand bread stretches from 3.4 years to over nine. Bake smaller loaves more often rather than large ones twice a week, and freeze half on the day it comes out.

4. The version with no machine at all

It would be dishonest to write this page without mentioning that the machine is optional.

A loaf baked in the oven uses the same 50p of ingredients. What changes is the energy: a domestic oven at 220 degrees for 35 minutes uses roughly 1.4 kWh, which is 36.85p, against the breadmaker's 13.16p. So an oven loaf costs 86.85p, which is more than a breadmaker loaf and still less than most shop bread.

The breadmaker is therefore saving you 23.69p a loaf against your own oven, plus the kneading. On that basis alone, £130 divided by 23.69p is 549 loaves, or over five years. The machine is buying convenience, not efficiency, and it is the same conclusion we reached comparing a microwave, oven and hob: the small appliance wins because it heats a small space.

The genuinely cheap route, if you are baking anyway: bake two loaves at once in the oven, or bake bread when the oven is already on for something else. That halves the energy per loaf and costs nothing.

5. Worked example: Idris in Merthyr Tydfil

Idris and his partner buy two loaves a week, usually a £1.65 branded wholemeal, and occasionally a £2.90 sourdough at the weekend. So roughly 80 branded loaves and 24 sourdough a year, £201.60 of bread annually.

He buys a breadmaker for £119 in a sale. Home loaves cost him 63.16p each, and wholemeal flour is slightly dearer so he budgets 68p.

If he replaces everything: 104 loaves at 68p is £70.72 a year, against £201.60. Saving £130.88 a year.

£119 divided by £130.88 is 0.91 years, about eleven months.

What actually happens is more realistic and worth recording. He bakes three times a week for six weeks, then settles at about one loaf a week because they cannot eat two before they stale. So 52 loaves at 68p is £35.36, and he still buys about 52 shop loaves at an average of £1.94, which is £100.88. Total £136.24 against £201.60.

Real saving £65.36 a year. Real payback: 1.8 years. Half as good as the theory, still comfortably worth it, and the difference is entirely the staling.

The fix he finds in year two: bake a small 400g loaf every three days rather than a large one twice a week. Less waste, fresher bread, and the annual figure improves again. The machine is still on the worktop, which is the only statistic that really mattered.

6. The honest verdict

If you currently buy 75p own-brand bread, do not buy a breadmaker. It will not pay back in the life of the machine, and we would rather tell you that than sell you one.

If you buy branded, bakery or gluten free bread, buy one. The payback runs from six months to about fifteen, and the bread is better.

Before you order, find the 30cm of worktop it is going to live on. If there is not any, the machine goes in a cupboard by March and the payback becomes infinite. That check is worth more than any review.

And bake smaller loaves more often. Home bread stales in two days, and a third of a loaf in the bin wipes out half your saving.

If you are buying

The two specifications worth comparing are the delay timer, which is what turns this into a habit rather than a project, and the loaf sizes it offers, because a small setting is what stops you wasting bread. Frequently asked

Is the bread actually as good? For a plain white or wholemeal loaf, yes, and it is warm, which shop bread never is. For anything needing a proper long ferment, such as real sourdough, a breadmaker's fixed cycle cannot replicate it, and a machine-made sourdough setting produces a loaf that is pleasant and not sourdough. If sourdough is what you are replacing, know that going in.

Do the bread mixes work out cheaper? No. A packet mix typically costs £1.00 to £1.50 and makes one loaf, against 50p of separate ingredients, so it roughly doubles the running cost and can put the total above a shop loaf. They are convenient for a first try and a poor habit thereafter.

What breaks on them? The paddle and the pan's non-stick coating, in that order, and both are consumables rather than faults. A replacement pan and paddle for a mid-range machine runs to £30 or £40, so check they are still sold for the model you are considering. If they are not, the machine's life is capped by its pan, which is the same platform test we apply to cordless appliances.
