Boarding Other People's Dogs in Your Home Needs a Council Licence Once You Earn Over £1,000. What the Licence Demands, What Rover Keeps, and What a Night Actually Pays

The apps make home dog boarding look like the easiest side hustle there is: a spare room, a garden, a love of dogs, and £30 to £40 a night. In England the law sees a licensable business the moment the money passes a threshold, and the licence comes with an inspector, a set of conditions about rooms, barriers and gardens, and a fee. Here is what the rules actually require, what the platform keeps, and the sums on a realistic year.

1. When the law says you are a business

Since 1 October 2018, providing or arranging boarding for other people's dogs in England has been one of the activities that needs a licence from the local council under the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018. Home boarding, meaning the dog stays in your own domestic home for day or overnight stays rather than in outdoor kennels, is its own category with its own statutory guidance from Defra.

The test is whether you are running a business, and the guidance gives councils two markers. If someone has a trading income below the HMRC trading income allowance, currently £1,000 a year, they do not require a licence. Above it they do not automatically qualify as a business either; the council considers whether they make sales or carry out the activity to make a profit, or earn commission or fees, and applies HMRC's badges of trade. In practice, anyone boarding dogs regularly for money through an app and clearing more than £1,000 a year should assume the answer is yes.

That £1,000 line is the same trading allowance that decides whether you tell HMRC, which we set out in our side hustle tax guide. Cross it and two things happen at once: you register for Self Assessment, and you apply to the council. The boarding platforms do not do either for you, and their terms typically require you to hold whatever licence your local law demands.

2. What the licence actually requires of your home

The conditions are in Schedule 4 of the Regulations and spelled out in Defra's guidance, and they are the part most people have not read before listing a spare room. The home must have direct access to a private, non-communal, secure and hazard-free outdoor area, used only by the licence holder and not shared with other properties, and the guidance says in terms that this cannot be a balcony. There must be at least two secure physical barriers between any dog and any entrance or exit, so a hallway door plus a front door, or a gate plus a door.

Indoors, each dog, or each group of dogs from the same household, must be provided with its own designated room where it can be kept separate from other dogs, with a clean, warm, draught-free sleeping area. Dogs from different households may only be boarded at the same time with the written consent of every owner, and there must be a documented trial session so the dogs can familiarise with each other and with any resident dog before boarding. Each dog is fed separately in its room unless its owner consents otherwise. The higher standard, which earns a longer licence, includes an overnight trial for any stay longer than three nights and, at the optional level, boarding only one household's dogs at a time.

Read that against a typical flat and the answer is usually no: a shared stairwell fails the private outdoor space test, and a single front door fails the two barrier test. Read it against a house with a hallway and an enclosed garden and it is achievable, but the designated room rule limits how many dogs you can take, and with it how much you can earn.

3. The inspection, the fee and the star rating

You apply to your council with a fee, the council appoints an inspector, the inspector visits and reports, and the council grants or refuses. Licences are then rated on the animal activity star rating system. Meet the minimum standards and you get a lower rating and a one year licence; meet all the required higher standards and at least half the optional ones and you get four or five stars and a licence valid for two or three years, which Defra notes lowers the cost of the licence over time.

The fee is set by each council and reported to Defra annually, but there is no national figure and we will not invent one. It varies from council to council, is usually split into an application fee and a grant fee, and can include a vet's inspection charge for some activities. Ask your council's licensing team for the home boarding fee before you do anything else; it is the first hard cost of the business and it recurs at every renewal.

Operating a licensable activity without a licence, or in breach of a condition, is an offence under the Regulations, and the council can also suspend, vary or revoke a licence. A copy of the licence must be displayed at the premises and the licence number shown in advertising, which includes an app profile.

4. What the platform keeps, and insurance

Rover is the largest of the apps in the UK, and its Help Centre states the split. Pet sitters and dog walkers take home 85% of their earnings from each booking, and sitters offering recurring weekly services take home 95%. The owner pays a separate 15% service fee on top, capped at £49 a booking. So a £35 night pays the sitter £29.75 and costs the owner about £40.25.

The platform's guarantee is not insurance for you. Rover's Guarantee is a reimbursement programme aimed primarily at vet care for the pet in your charge, with conditions. Your own liability, if a boarded dog bites a visitor or damages a neighbour's property, and your home contents, are matters for a pet business insurance policy, which specialist insurers sell and councils often expect to see at inspection. Budget for it. Our dog walking guide covers the same insurance point for walkers, who do not need the boarding licence but face the same liability.

Then the ordinary costs: extra washing, cleaning products, a damaged rug now and then, and the hours. Changeovers, walks and the trial sessions the licence requires are real time, and at £12.71 an hour, the National Living Wage from April 2026, they are a real cost even if you enjoy them.

5. A worked example in Stockport

Meera in Stockport has a semi with a hallway door, a front door and an enclosed garden, one spare bedroom, and no dog of her own. She boards one dog at a time through Rover at £35 a night, about 100 nights a year, mostly school holidays and weekends.

Gross bookings are £3,500 on our arithmetic. Rover keeps 15%, £525, so her payouts total £2,975. She is well over £1,000, so she needs the licence. Her council quotes a fee for a home boarding licence; we will use £300 as an illustrative figure for the application and grant together, because the real figure is her council's and not a national one. Pet business insurance we put at £120 a year, again illustrative. Washing, cleaning and wear we estimate at £150. Total costs £570, leaving £2,405. With a three year licence at the higher standard the licence cost per year falls, and the net improves.

Her time: about 100 changeovers and daily walks, which she puts at 1.5 hours a boarded night on average, 150 hours a year. At £12.71 that is £1,906. Counting it, the business pays her about £500 a year on top of a fair wage for the hours; not counting it, £2,405. Either way she is over the trading allowance and files a Self Assessment return, deducting the licence, insurance and costs, or the £1,000 allowance, whichever is better.

If she boarded two dogs from different households at once she would need two designated rooms, every owner's written consent and trial sessions, and her earnings would roughly double for the same licence. That is where home boarding stops being pocket money and becomes a small business, and it is exactly what the room-per-dog rule is designed to control.

6. The honest verdict

Above £1,000 a year, home boarding in England is a licensed business: a room per dog, two barriers, a private garden, trial sessions, an inspection and a council fee. A lot of flats cannot meet it.

Rover keeps 15% and the owner pays 15% on top; a £35 night is £29.75 to you. Insurance is yours to arrange, and the guarantee is not it.

One dog at a time for 100 nights nets roughly £2,400 before your time, in a home that already qualifies. Good money for the right house; the wrong house should not list, because the inspector will say the same.

Frequently asked

Do I need a licence to look after a friend's dog? Not if you are not trading. Defra's guidance says someone with a trading income below the HMRC trading income allowance of £1,000 does not require a licence. Regular paid boarding through an app above that figure is a business and needs one.

Can I board dogs in a flat? Usually not. The conditions require direct access to a private, non-communal secure outdoor area, which cannot be a balcony, and two secure barriers between any dog and any exit. Check with your council, but most flats fail one or both.

How much does Rover take in the UK? Sitters keep 85% of each booking, or 95% for recurring weekly services. Owners pay a separate 15% service fee capped at £49 a booking.

How much is the licence? It is set by each council and not published nationally. Ask your council's licensing team for the home boarding fee, which usually has an application and a grant element and recurs at renewal after one to three years.
