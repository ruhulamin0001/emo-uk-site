A Robot Vacuum Costs Twice As Much A Year To Own As A Cordless

This is not an argument against robot vacuums. It is an argument against costing one as though it replaces something, because in almost every house that buys one, it does not. Price it as an addition and the decision gets much clearer.

Ruhul Amin Published 11 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The electricity is trivial for both. A robot costs about £5.76 a year to run and a cordless stick about £2.79. Nobody should choose on that.
  • The dock is the hidden one. A robot base station draws power for all 8,760 hours of the year, which at 3 watts is £6.92 a year of doing nothing, more than the robot spends cleaning.
  • Total ownership: £110.68 a year for a robot with a self emptying dock, £80.68 without one, £51.36 for a cordless stick. Brushes, filters, bags and batteries do all the work in those numbers.
  • The self emptying dock is a subscription in all but name. About £30 a year of disposable bags to avoid emptying a bin you could empty yourself.
  • A robot saves roughly 17 hours a year. It breaks even if an hour of your time is worth £6.40. For most people it is, which is a perfectly good reason to buy one. It is just not a saving.

1. The electricity, which almost does not matter

Let us dispose of this first, because it is the thing people ask about and the thing that matters least.

A robot vacuum draws roughly 40 watts while cleaning. Run it for 90 minutes a day, every day, and that is 21.9 kWh a year. At the Ofgem price cap electricity rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 26.32p per kWh, that is £5.76 a year.

A cordless stick vacuum has a battery of around 90 watt hours. Charge it twice a week, allowing about 15 per cent for charger losses, and you draw 10.6 kWh a year: £2.79 a year.

Three pounds of difference. If electricity were the question, this article would be one paragraph long. It is not, and the reason is in the next two sections.

But note one thing about the robot before we move on. It cleans for 90 minutes a day and sits on its dock for the other 22 and a half hours, and the dock is drawing power the whole time. A base station idles at somewhere around 2 to 4 watts maintaining the battery and keeping its radio awake. At 3 watts across all 8,760 hours of the year that is 26.3 kWh, or £6.92. The dock costs you more than the cleaning does. It is exactly the sort of thing our page on smart plugs and standby is about.

The last row is the honest one and it is the reason this article exists. Almost nobody who buys a robot vacuum throws away their stick vacuum, because a robot cannot do stairs, cannot do sofas, cannot do the car, and cannot deal with a dropped bag of flour. So the real comparison is not robot against stick. It is stick against stick plus robot, and that is £51.36 against £162.04 a year.

And look at the corded upright column, which is the same finding we reached on lawnmowers: the version with a cable is dramatically the cheapest to buy and to own, because it has no battery, and nobody will ever recommend it to you because there is no margin in selling it.

3. The dock, the bags and the battery

Three line items in that table do almost all of the damage, and each is worth understanding before you choose a model.

The self emptying dock. This is the feature that has driven robot vacuum prices up most in recent years, and what it does is empty the robot's small bin into a larger bag inside the base, so you deal with it monthly rather than daily. The bags are proprietary, they are not cheap, and you cannot stop buying them without losing the feature. At roughly £30 a year that is a subscription in everything but name, and it exists to save you emptying a bin that takes four seconds to empty. Whether that is worth £30 a year plus roughly £300 on the purchase price is a real question and the answer for a lot of households is no.

The consumables. Robots eat brushes. The main roller, the side brush and the filter are all wear items, and hair wraps round the roller in a way it does not on a stick vacuum's head. Budget £40 a year and check the parts are still sold for your model before you buy, which is the same platform question that decides whether a cordless vacuum lasts eight years or four.

The battery. A robot's battery cycles every single day, so it wears faster than a stick vacuum's does in cycle terms even though the stick's ages in the cupboard. Two to three years is common before runtime drops noticeably. The critical question is whether it is user replaceable. On many robots it is a screwdriver job and a £60 part. On some it is not accessible at all, and on those the battery's life is the machine's life. That is the single biggest do-not-buy signal in this category and it is rarely on the spec sheet, so search for a replacement battery for the exact model before you order.

4. What it actually buys, priced per hour

A robot vacuum does not save money. It saves you from doing something, and that is a legitimate thing to buy, so let us price it properly rather than pretend.

A household that vacuums the downstairs twice a week, twenty minutes a time, spends about 35 hours a year on it. A robot running daily does not eliminate that, because you still do the stairs, the edges and the sofas, but it credibly halves it. Call it 17 hours a year saved.

The robot with a self emptying dock costs £110.68 a year to own, plus the purchase price spread over its life.

£110.68 divided by 17 hours is £6.40 an hour.

That is the break-even. If an hour of your time on a weekday evening is worth more than £6.40 to you, the robot is a rational purchase. For most working households it comfortably is, which is why these sell. For a retired couple who quite like a tidy-up and have the time, it comfortably is not.

Add the purchase price and the picture sharpens further. A £650 robot over a five year life is another £130 a year, taking the total to £240.68, and the break-even to £14.16 an hour. That is above the National Living Wage, and it is the number to hold in your head when you are looking at the premium models.

The cheaper the robot, the better this sum gets, which is an unusual conclusion in appliances and an important one here. A £300 robot with a basic dock and a replaceable battery is a far better time-per-pound purchase than a £900 one with a self emptying base, because the expensive features buy convenience rather than cleaning.

5. Worked example: Tomasz in Swindon

Tomasz has a two bedroom flat, no stairs, a dog, and a cordless stick vacuum he bought three years ago. He is looking at a robot at £599 with a self emptying dock, or £319 for the same manufacturer's model with a basic dock.

He works out the ownership cost properly. The self emptying model is £110.68 a year in consumables, bags and power, plus £119.80 a year of purchase price over five years: £230.48. The basic model is £80.68 plus £63.80: £144.48.

The dock is costing him £86 a year to avoid emptying a small bin.

He also checks, before ordering, whether a replacement battery exists for each model. It does for both, at £54, which is the answer he wanted and is not always the answer.

He buys the basic model. The dog hair is the reason it earns its keep: with a robot running every morning the flat never reaches the state where vacuuming feels like a job, and he keeps the stick vacuum for the sofa and the car. His combined cost is £144.48 plus the stick's £51.36, so £195.84 a year for a permanently clean floor.

The thing he did not anticipate: he now spends about ten minutes a week picking cables and socks off the floor so the robot can work, which is not a saving but is arguably a benefit.

6. The honest verdict

Buy a robot vacuum for the time, not the money. There is no money in it. It costs about £110 a year to own against a stick vacuum's £51, and you will keep the stick vacuum anyway.

Skip the self emptying dock. Roughly £300 on the price and £30 a year in proprietary bags, to avoid a four second job. That is the worst value feature in the category.

Check a replacement battery exists for the exact model before you order. A robot cycles its battery daily and a sealed one caps the machine's life at two or three years.

And if you have stairs and no pets, think twice. A robot that can only do one floor of a house saves half of the time you hoped, at the same cost.

If you are buying

Three checks decide this and none of them is suction: is the battery replaceable and available, are the brushes and filters still sold for the model, and does it need a proprietary bag. A cheap plug-in energy monitor on the dock will also tell you exactly what your base station is drawing while it sits there, which is usually a surprise.

Frequently asked

Can I switch the dock off at the wall between runs? Physically yes, and it would save the £6.92, but most robots need the dock live to charge and to receive their schedule, so switching it off means the robot does not run in the morning and the whole point is lost. If the robot only runs twice a week, a timer socket that powers the dock for a few hours before and after each run is a reasonable compromise.

Do they actually clean properly? On hard floors and low pile carpet, modern ones clean well enough that the floor stays consistently acceptable, which is arguably better than a proper clean once a week. On thick pile, in corners, along skirting and on stairs they do not, and no amount of mapping fixes it. Think of a robot as maintaining a floor rather than cleaning one.

What about the mopping versions? They add a water tank, pads and usually a second set of consumables, and on the self emptying models they add clean and dirty water tanks in the base too. They damp wipe rather than mop, which is fine for dust and hopeless for anything sticky. Cost them as another £20 to £40 a year of pads on top of everything in the table above.
