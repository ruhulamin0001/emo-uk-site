The personal allowance has not moved since 2022, and now it is frozen to 2031

Frozen thresholds are the tax rise nobody has to announce. With the Budget on 28 October, here is exactly what the freeze is, how it takes money without a rate ever changing, and what it does to one household's payslip.

The short version

  • The personal allowance is £12,570 and has been since April 2022.
  • The higher rate threshold is £50,270, made up of the personal allowance plus the £37,700 basic rate limit.
  • The freeze was extended at the 2025 Budget and now runs to April 2031, rather than April 2028 as previously legislated.
  • This is a tax rise without a rate change. If your pay rises and the threshold does not, a larger share of your income is taxed.
  • The additional rate threshold is £125,140, and the personal allowance tapers away above £100,000.

1. The numbers, and how long they are frozen

For the 2026 to 2027 tax year, the figures are these.

  • Personal allowance: £12,570. Unchanged since April 2022.
  • Basic rate limit: £37,700, taxed at 20%.
  • Higher rate threshold: £50,270, which is the personal allowance plus the basic rate limit. Income above it is taxed at 40%.
  • Additional rate threshold: £125,140, above which the rate is 45%.

The freeze was originally legislated to run to April 2028. At the 2025 Budget the Chancellor extended it by a further three years, so the personal allowance and basic rate limit, and the equivalent National Insurance thresholds, are now maintained until 5 April 2031.

That is a nine year freeze on a figure that, in a normal year, would rise with inflation.

2. How a freeze takes money without a rate changing

The mechanism is simple and it is worth spelling out, because "fiscal drag" is a phrase that explains nothing to anybody.

Income tax is charged on what you earn above the personal allowance. If your pay rises 4% and the allowance rises 4% too, the proportion of your income that is taxed stays roughly the same. If your pay rises 4% and the allowance does not move at all, then the entire 4% sits in taxable territory, and a larger share of everything you earn is taxed.

Two effects follow. People on low incomes who were previously below the allowance are pulled into paying income tax at all. People approaching £50,270 are pulled into the 40% band on part of their income for the first time.

No rate changed. No announcement was needed. The bill went up anyway.

3. A worked example: Aisha's payslip, with the arithmetic

Aisha is a senior nurse in Nottingham. Her salary this tax year is £48,000.

Her taxable income is £48,000 minus the personal allowance of £12,570, which is £35,430. That sits entirely within the basic rate limit of £37,700, so all of it is taxed at 20%.

£35,430 at 20% is £7,086 of income tax for the year.

Now suppose she gets a 5% pay rise, taking her to £50,400.

Her taxable income becomes £50,400 minus £12,570, which is £37,830. The first £37,700 is taxed at 20%, giving £7,540. The remaining £130 sits above the higher rate threshold and is taxed at 40%, giving £52.

Total income tax: £7,540 plus £52, which is £7,592.

So a pay rise of £2,400 increased her income tax by £506, and it made her a higher rate taxpayer for the first time. The 40% is only charged on £130 of her income, not on all of it, which is the single most misunderstood point in British personal tax. She is not "taxed 40% on her salary". She is taxed 40% on the last £130 of it.

Note what we have not calculated here. National Insurance and any student loan repayment come on top, and her pension contributions would reduce the taxable figure. We have not put numbers on those because we would need her exact NI category, plan type and contribution rate, and inventing them would make the example look more precise while making it less true.

4. Who it hits hardest, and who it barely touches

The freeze bites hardest on two groups. People just below the personal allowance, for whom any pay rise now creates an income tax bill that did not exist before. And people in the low £40,000s, who are on a path to crossing £50,270 within a few normal pay rises.

It barely touches people whose income is well above £125,140, because the additional rate threshold is where they already sit, and the marginal change is small relative to their bill. And it does not touch anyone whose income is entirely from sources outside income tax.

Being blunt: if you are on a fixed income below £12,570 and it is not rising, this freeze does nothing to you at all. That is not good news, it just means this particular article is not about your problem.

Two important qualifications. First, Scotland sets its own income tax rates and bands on non savings, non dividend income, so a Scottish taxpayer's bands differ from the figures above, although the personal allowance itself is set UK wide. Second, the freeze applies to the equivalent National Insurance thresholds as well as to income tax, which is why the effect on a payslip is larger than an income tax calculation alone suggests.

There is also a sharper edge above £100,000, where the personal allowance is withdrawn by £1 for every £2 of income. That withdrawal is what produces the well known band between £100,000 and £125,140 where the effective marginal rate is far higher than the headline 40%, and a frozen £100,000 starting point drags more people into it each year.

5. What the Budget on 28 October could and could not change

The Budget is on Wednesday 28 October 2026. A Chancellor can legislate to unfreeze thresholds, to raise them, or to extend the freeze again.

What is worth knowing is the timing. A change to the personal allowance announced on 28 October would almost certainly take effect from the start of the next tax year, 6 April 2027, not immediately. Your payslip in November would be unchanged either way.

So there is nothing to do before Budget day on the strength of a threshold rumour. The freeze is legislated to 2031 as things stand, and the useful move is to understand where you sit relative to £50,270, because that determines whether things like pension contributions or salary sacrifice are worth looking at in your case.

Frequently asked

How long are the thresholds frozen? The personal allowance and basic rate limit, and the equivalent National Insurance thresholds, are maintained until 5 April 2031, following the extension announced at the 2025 Budget.

If I cross £50,270, is all my income taxed at 40%? No. Only the income above £50,270 is taxed at 40%. Everything below it is taxed at the rates that apply to those bands. This is the most common misunderstanding in UK income tax.

Why is the higher rate threshold £50,270? It is the personal allowance of £12,570 plus the basic rate limit of £37,700.

Could the Budget change this on 28 October? It could announce a change, but income tax threshold changes normally take effect from the start of the next tax year on 6 April 2027, not on Budget day.
