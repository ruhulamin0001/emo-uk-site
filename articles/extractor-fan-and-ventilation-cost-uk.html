The Damp in Your Bathroom Is a Ventilation Bill You Have Not Paid Yet

Most damp in British homes is not rising damp and not a leak. It is water that people put into the air and never took out again, and the fix is a fan that costs pennies a year to run. Here is what Part F requires, what the hardware costs, and why sealing the house up made it worse.

1. Why the damp appeared after you improved the house

A household of four puts a substantial amount of water vapour into the air every day. Showering, cooking, washing, drying clothes indoors, and simply breathing. In an old draughty house that moisture leaked away through gaps you never noticed, at the cost of an enormous heating bill.

Then you did the sensible things. New windows with proper seals. Draught excluders. A letterbox brush. Filled the gaps around the skirting. The heating bill fell, which was the point, and the moisture stopped leaving, which was not.

Warm moist air finds the coldest surface in the room and condenses on it: a window reveal, the corner where two external walls meet, the wall behind a wardrobe. Mould follows, because mould needs surface moisture and nothing else. This is condensation damp, and it is by a wide margin the most common form of damp in British homes.

The distinction matters because of what gets sold to fix it. A chemical damp-proof course injected into a wall does nothing whatever for condensation, and it is an expensive nothing. Before anyone quotes for damp-proofing, establish which kind you have. Condensation shows on the surface, worst in winter, worst in corners and behind furniture, and comes with streaming windows. Rising damp shows a tide mark low on the wall with salts in the plaster. They are not the same problem and they do not share a fix.

2. What Part F actually requires, in litres per second

Approved Document F, the 2021 edition for England, sets minimum extract ventilation rates for wet rooms. These are the numbers a building control officer would check, and the numbers to specify when you buy.

RoomMinimum intermittent extract rateWhat that means in practice
Kitchen, fan adjacent to the hob30 l/sA cooker hood directly over the hob
Kitchen, fan elsewhere in the room60 l/sTwice the rate, because the steam has further to travel
Utility room30 l/sThe room where the tumble dryer and the airer live
Bathroom, with or without a WC15 l/sThe standard bathroom fan
Separate WC6 l/sOr an openable window

The kitchen figures are the ones people get wrong. Putting the extract on the far wall instead of over the hob does not halve the requirement, it doubles it, because the moisture and grease have to cross the room before the fan can catch them. If you are choosing where to put a fan in a kitchen, above the hob is both cheaper to buy and more effective.

One more rule that is routinely ignored: a fan needs air to replace what it removes. A well-sealed room with the door closed and no undercut gives the fan nothing to pull, and it stalls against its own back pressure. A 10mm gap under the door is the standard provision, and it is free.

3. The running cost, calculated at the October 2026 cap

This is where the argument is won, because people resist fans on the assumption that running one is expensive. It is not, and the arithmetic takes one line.

All figures use the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: gas at 7.97p per kWh including VAT at 5% and electricity at 26.32p per kWh with no VAT, because the zero rate on electricity runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027.

A standard 100mm bathroom fan rated at 15 l/s draws roughly 15W. Say it runs for 90 minutes a day, which covers two showers plus overrun.

15W multiplied by 1.5 hours is 22.5 Wh a day, which is 8.2 kWh a year. At 26.32p per kWh, that is £2.16 a year.

A larger 60 l/s kitchen fan at 30W running an hour a day is 11 kWh, or £2.90 a year.

Two pounds. That is the number people are avoiding when they leave the fan off and open a window in February instead, which loses far more heat than the fan ever costs. There is no version of this where running the fan is the expensive option.

The last two rows are the expensive answers to the wrong question. A dehumidifier at £115 a year is fifty times the running cost of the fan and does not remove the moisture from the building, it just condenses it into a tank you empty. A chemical DPC at several thousand pounds does nothing at all to condensation.

Worked example: the Naismith household, Falkirk

1970s semi, new windows fitted in 2023, black mould appearing each winter on the bathroom ceiling and in the corner of the back bedroom above the bathroom.

What they had been doing. Repainting the bathroom ceiling with anti-mould paint roughly every eighteen months, at about £90 a time in materials and a weekend, and running a dehumidifier in the bedroom for six hours a day through the winter months. The dehumidifier drew 200W, so over 150 winter days that was 180 kWh, or £47.38 a year at 26.32p.

Recurring cost: roughly £107 a year between the repainting amortised and the dehumidifier electricity, plus a bathroom that looked poor for half of every year.

What they did instead. A humidity-sensing 15 l/s fan with a 15-minute overrun, ducted through the gable wall, fitted by an electrician for £260. They also cut 10mm off the bottom of the bathroom door, which cost nothing and was the reason the old fan had never worked.

Running cost of the new fan: £2.50 a year.

Payback: £260 divided by the £104.50 a year saved, which is 2.5 years. The mould has not returned and the dehumidifier now lives in the loft.

5. The honest verdict

If you have black mould in a bathroom or a cold corner, buy the fan before you buy anything else. A humidity-sensing unit at the Part F rate for the room, properly ducted to outside rather than into a loft void, with a gap under the door so it has air to move.

Do not let anyone sell you an injected damp-proof course for a condensation problem. The symptoms are different, the causes are different, and the price difference is two thousand pounds.

And if you are about to draught-proof, budget the ventilation at the same time. Every gap you close raises the value of the fan, and doing the sealing first and the ventilation later is how a well-meaning improvement turns into a mould problem.

Frequently asked

Is a bathroom fan a legal requirement in an existing home? Approved Document F applies when you build, extend, or make a material change such as replacing a fan or refitting a bathroom. There is no duty to retrofit one into an untouched bathroom that has an openable window, though for a let property a persistent mould problem raises separate housing health and safety obligations.

Can the fan duct into the loft? No. Ducting into a loft void moves the moisture from a room you can see into a roof space you cannot, and the result is damp timber. It must terminate outside, through a wall or a tile vent.

Does an anti-mould paint fix the problem? It slows the reappearance of the stain. It does nothing to the moisture that caused it, which is why the same ceiling needs doing again in a year or two.

Why does my fan seem to do nothing? Usually one of three things: the duct is long, flexible and kinked, so the actual rate is far below the rating; there is no air path into the room because the door seals tightly; or the external grille is a closed backdraught shutter that has stuck.
