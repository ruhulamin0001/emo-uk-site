Fuel Duty Goes Up on Tuesday. The 1p Everyone Is Quoting Is Actually 1.2p

On 1 September 2026 fuel duty rises for the first time since 2011. Every report calls it a penny, and the duty line does rise by exactly a penny. But VAT is charged on top of duty, so the number that leaves your account is 1.2p a litre, and by March it is 6p, not the 5p being quoted.

1. What actually changes on Tuesday

The 5p a litre fuel duty cut introduced at the Spring Statement in March 2022 has been extended five times. It ends on 31 August 2026. From HMRC's published rates table for 2026 to 2027, the main rate for unleaded petrol and diesel moves like this:

To 31 August 2026: 52.95p a litre.

From 1 September 2026: 53.95p a litre.

From 1 December 2026: 55.95p a litre.

From 1 March 2027: 57.95p a litre, the rate that applied before 23 March 2022.

This is the first increase in fuel duty since 2011. Rebated and lower taxed fuels rise by the equivalent proportion, so LPG goes from 28.88p to 29.43p per kg on Tuesday and reaches 31.61p by March. From 1 April 2027 duty is due to be uprated annually in line with RPI, which is stated policy rather than a certainty, since that uprating has been cancelled every year since 2011.

HMRC's impact note says the measure affects up to an estimated 36 million individuals and that it will "save the average car driver £49 in 2026 to 2027 compared to previous plans". That framing is accurate and it is worth understanding: it compares the phased path against the alternative, which was the full 5p landing in one go in March 2026. Compared with what you paid last month, you are paying more. Both statements are true at the same time.

2. Why 1p is really 1.2p, and 5p is really 6p

Fuel duty is a fixed amount per litre. VAT at 20% is then charged on the whole pump price including the duty. So every penny of duty becomes 1.2p of pump price once VAT is applied.

Duty rises 5p by March. Your pump price rises 6p, because VAT is charged on the duty.

This matters for the tank figure being quoted everywhere this week. A 55 litre fill multiplied by 5p gives £2.75, which is the number in most coverage. Multiply by 6p and you get £3.30. The stages, per 55 litre fill, are 66p from Tuesday, £1.98 once December's step is in, and £3.30 from March.

Here is where the money actually goes today. In the week commencing 24 August 2026, the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero recorded average UK pump prices of 161.63p for petrol and 182.82p for diesel. Break the petrol price down:

Petrol at 161.63p a litre: duty 52.95p, VAT 26.94p, everything else (crude, refining, delivery, retailer margin) 81.74p.

Tax is 79.89p, or 49.4% of what you hand over.

There is a simple test for when tax passes half the pump price: it happens whenever the pump price is below three times the duty rate. At 52.95p duty that threshold is 158.85p, and petrol is currently above it. From 1 December the threshold becomes 167.85p, and unless wholesale prices rise meaningfully between now and then, petrol drivers will be paying more in tax than in fuel again. Diesel, at 182.82p, stays clear of that line throughout, with tax at 45.6% today.

3. Worked example: the Ahmed household in Stevenage

Farhan Ahmed drives 9,000 miles a year in a petrol car averaging 42mpg. That is 974 litres a year, or about £1,575 at today's 161.63p.

The temptation is to multiply 974 litres by 6p and call it £58 a year. That is the eventual full year cost once all three steps are in, but it is not what he pays over the next twelve months, because the rise arrives in stages. Splitting his usage evenly across the year:

September, October, November at plus 1.2p: 244 litres, £2.92.

December, January, February at plus 3.6p: 244 litres, £8.77.

March to August 2027 at plus 6p: 487 litres, £29.22.

Twelve month total: £40.91. The steady state cost, from September 2027 onwards, is £58.45 a year.

So the honest answer for a typical commuter is roughly £41 over the coming year, rising to about £58 a year after that. It is a real cost and it is not a crisis. Anyone telling you this is either nothing or a catastrophe is selling you something.

4. The rise is smaller than the gap you already pay

This is the part almost nobody puts next to the duty story. In its April 2026 monitoring, the Competition and Markets Authority found the average fuel retailer margin was 11.3p a litre, up from 10.7p a year earlier. The entire three stage duty restoration is 6p at the pump. The margin is nearly double it.

More usefully, the CMA has consistently found supermarkets cheaper than branded forecourts by around 3.9p a litre on petrol and 5.2p on diesel. For Farhan, 3.9p across 974 litres is £37.99 a year. That single choice of where to fill up is worth 93% of the entire tax rise he is about to absorb.

And since 2 February 2026 there has been a legal mechanism to see that gap. The CMA's Fuel Finder scheme requires every filling station in the UK to report any price change for any grade within 30 minutes. The CMA began actively enforcing it on 1 May 2026, and the data is published openly, which is why the free price comparison apps and the AA and RAC price checkers now show genuinely live forecourt prices rather than crowd sourced guesses. It costs nothing to check before you drive.

5. Why the pump price will not change at midnight

Fuel duty is charged at the duty point, which is when fuel leaves the refinery or bonded storage terminal, not when it leaves the pump. Every litre already sitting in a forecourt's underground tanks at midnight on Monday was duty paid at 52.95p.

A busy site turns its stock over in a few days, a quiet rural one can take a fortnight or more. So the 1.2p filters through over the following one to two weeks rather than appearing everywhere on Tuesday morning. That is not a conspiracy, it is just how the tax works. It does mean that a forecourt raising its price on 1 September is raising it on fuel it bought at the old rate, and that a site which has not moved by the weekend is not doing you a favour, it simply has not taken a delivery yet.

The practical version: filling up on Monday saves the average driver roughly 60p on a full tank. That is worth doing if you were going to fill up this week anyway. It is not worth a special journey, and queueing for it costs more in time and fuel than it saves.

The honest verdict

There is no action to take here that will save you serious money, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Roughly £41 over the coming year for a typical petrol commuter, rising to about £58 a year once all three steps are in. Diesel drivers and anyone doing high mileage should scale that up in proportion to their litres.

The two things worth carrying away are these. First, quote the pump number, not the duty number: 1.2p on Tuesday and 6p by March, because VAT compounds it. Second, the gap between a supermarket forecourt and a branded one is worth almost exactly as much as the entire tax rise, it is now legally required to be published in near real time, and checking it takes about fifteen seconds on a phone.

Watch 1 December. That is the 2p step, it lands in the most expensive week of the year for most households, and at current prices it is the point where tax quietly goes back over half of a litre of petrol.

Frequently asked

Could the government still cancel it? It could, and several opposition parties have asked it to. But the increase is legislated through secondary legislation under the Hydrocarbon Oil Duties Act 1979 and was costed at Budget 2025, where the phased extension is scored at £2,370m in 2026 to 2027. Reversing it now would need a new fiscal decision. Plan on it happening.

Is diesel rising by more than petrol? No. The duty rate and the increases are identical for both, at 52.95p rising to 57.95p. Diesel is more expensive at the pump for reasons of refining and demand, not tax. In fact diesel carries a lower proportion of tax than petrol, 45.6% against 49.4%, precisely because the untaxed part of its price is larger.

Does this affect red diesel or heating oil? Rebated fuels rise by the equivalent proportion of the cut. Marked gas oil goes from 10.18p to 10.37p a litre on 1 September and reaches 11.14p by March 2027. Domestic heating kerosene remains outside the main road fuel rates.

Will fuel prices actually go up by 1.2p, or will other things move too? Duty is only one input. Between the weeks of 6 July and 24 August 2026, average petrol rose 11.83p and diesel 18.05p on wholesale movement alone, which is roughly ten times the size of Tuesday's tax change. The duty rise is a certainty; the pump price is not, and it can easily be swamped in either direction.
