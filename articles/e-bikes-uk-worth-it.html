Before You Spend £1,500 on an E-Bike: The Law, the Battery Bill and the Fire Risk

E-bike sales keep climbing and so do e-bike fires, up 204% in two years. This is the guide we wanted before buying: what the UK law actually permits, what an e-bike really costs over five years once the battery dies, and the three checks that separate a safe bike from a dangerous one.

1. What is actually legal in the UK

This matters before price, because an illegal e-bike is not a bike in the eyes of the law. It is an unregistered motor vehicle, which means you need tax, insurance, a licence and a helmet, and riding it on a cycle path is an offence.

To count as a normal pedal cycle, an electrically assisted pedal cycle (EAPC) must meet three conditions: the motor must be rated at 250 watts continuous or less, assistance must cut out at 15.5mph, and the rider must be 14 or over. Meet those and you need no licence, no tax, no insurance and no registration.

Two details catch people out. First, you may legally pedal faster than 15.5mph, but the motor must stop pushing at that point. A bike that keeps driving you to 20mph is not a legal pedal cycle, whatever the seller calls it. Second, a twist-and-go throttle that moves the bike without pedalling is only permitted up to 3.7mph as a walk-assist. Anything more powerful requires type approval, and most imported bikes do not have it.

In 2024 the government consulted on raising the limit to 500W and permitting throttles. That proposal was dropped in February 2025, so the 250W rules still stand in 2026. Ignore any listing that implies otherwise.

If the advert boasts about 750W or a "no pedalling needed" throttle, it is not describing a legal UK bicycle. It is describing a fine waiting to happen.

2. What e-bikes really cost in 2026

Prices sit in three broad bands, and the difference between them is mostly the parts you cannot see: the battery cells, the brakes, and whether spares will exist in three years.

BandTypical priceWhat you are actually getting
Budget£500 to £900Unbranded motor and battery, basic brakes. Fine for light, dry, occasional use. Spares and support are the weak point.
Mid-range£1,000 to £1,800Recognised motor system, branded cells, hydraulic brakes, a dealer who can service it. Where most sensible commuter money goes.
Premium£2,000 and upBetter integration, lighter frames, longer warranties, replaceable everything. Worth it for daily miles, hard to justify for weekend use.

The mistake is comparing bikes on headline price and range. Compare them on five-year cost, and one number dominates.

3. The battery: the hidden second purchase

An e-bike battery is a consumable, not a permanent part. Most quality lithium-ion packs last 3 to 5 years, or roughly 500 to 1,000 full charge cycles. Capacity fades gradually rather than failing suddenly, so what you notice first is your range quietly shrinking.

Replacement typically costs £200 to £800, depending on capacity and brand. That single figure is usually the largest ongoing cost of e-bike ownership, and it is the one almost no review mentions.

It also changes which bike is cheapest. A £700 bike with an unbranded pack that no one sells a replacement for can become landfill in year four. A £1,300 bike using a standard, purchasable battery can be re-batteried and keep going. On a five-year view, the more expensive bike is often the cheaper one.

The five-year sum most buyers skip

Budget bike: £750 purchase, plus a replacement battery that either does not exist or costs £350 from a generic seller. Add basic servicing at around £60 a year. Five-year total: roughly £1,400, assuming you can even source the pack.

Mid-range bike: £1,400 purchase, a genuine £450 battery in year four, servicing around £80 a year. Five-year total: roughly £2,250, but you still own a working, serviceable bike at the end.

The gap is real, but it is far smaller than the £650 difference on the price tag suggests, and only one of the two bikes still exists in year six.

4. The fire risk nobody mentions in reviews

This is the part that changed how we would buy. UK fire services have reported a 204% increase in e-bike fires over two years, and London Fire Brigade attributes around 40% of its e-bike fire incidents to conversion kits and unsafe battery packs. Its Deputy Commissioner has described DIY conversion kits as the fastest growing fire trend in London.

The pattern is consistent: a cheap kit bought from an online marketplace, a battery and charger that were never designed for each other, charging overnight in a hallway. Lithium fires are fast, extremely hot and give off toxic smoke, and a hallway is exactly where they block the way out.

None of this means e-bikes are unsafe. A complete bike from an established brand, with a matched charger and proper certification, is a normal consumer product. The risk sits almost entirely in mismatched, uncertified, marketplace-sourced batteries and kits.

Charging rules we would treat as non-negotiable

Charge while you are awake and in the building, never overnight. Never charge in a hallway, doorway or the only escape route. Use only the charger supplied with the battery. Unplug when it reaches full. Stop using any pack that is swollen, damaged, or was in a crash, and never store or charge one near a fire exit.

5. Seven checks before you pay

  1. 250W and 15.5mph in the specification. If those numbers are missing or higher, walk away.
  2. UKCA or CE marking on the bike, the battery and the charger, not just the box.
  3. A named battery cell brand, and a battery you can actually buy again. Search for a replacement before you buy the bike.
  4. Matched charger, supplied with the bike, rated for that exact pack.
  5. A real UK address and warranty, ideally two years or more on the battery, with someone who answers the phone.
  6. Hydraulic disc brakes on anything you will ride in traffic. An e-bike is heavier and faster than it looks.
  7. Servicing. Ask a local bike shop whether they will work on that brand before you order. Some will not touch certain imports.

6. Worked example: does it beat the car?

James, 7 miles each way, Birmingham

James drives 14 miles a day to work, five days a week, roughly 230 days a year. Fuel and parking come to around £9 a day, so about £2,070 a year, before wear and tear.

He buys a £1,400 mid-range e-bike, spends £180 on a proper lock, lights and waterproofs, and rides four days a week from March to November, driving in deep winter. That is roughly 150 e-bike days.

Charging costs pennies a day, call it £12 a year. Servicing is £80. He avoids about £1,350 of driving cost in year one, against £1,672 of spending, so year one is roughly break-even. From year two onwards he is saving over £1,200 a year, minus a £450 battery in year four.

The honest reading: an e-bike does not pay for itself in month three. It pays for itself somewhere in year two, and only if you actually ride it in the rain.

If your commute is under three miles, a normal bike almost certainly wins on cost. If it is over ten miles with hills, the e-bike is the reason you will keep doing it. Between those, it comes down to whether hills and arriving sweaty are what currently stop you cycling.

8. Who should not buy an e-bike

If you have nowhere secure to store and charge it. An e-bike left in a shared hallway is both a theft risk and, if charged there, a fire risk. Solve storage before you buy.

If your journey is mostly under two miles and flat. A £200 second-hand normal bike will do the same job and never need a battery.

If the plan depends on a bargain conversion kit. That is precisely the category the fire statistics point at. Buy a complete bike, or keep saving.

If you would be borrowing at high interest to buy it. Check whether your employer runs a Cycle to Work scheme first, which spreads the cost from gross pay, before reaching for a credit card. Our cashback card guide explains why interest dwarfs any reward.

9. The honest verdict

An e-bike is worth it if you have a commute between roughly three and fifteen miles, somewhere safe to store and charge it, and the discipline to ride it when the weather is poor. In that case it typically pays for itself in year two and keeps paying afterwards.

It is not worth it as an impulse purchase, as a gadget, or as a cheap conversion project. The money is not really in the bike, it is in the battery, and the safety is not really in the brand name, it is in the certification and the charger.

Buy the legal specification, buy a battery you can replace, charge it while you are awake, and it is one of the better purchases available to a UK commuter.

Frequently asked

Do I need insurance for an e-bike? Not if it meets EAPC rules. Many home insurance policies cover a bike up to a value limit, and specialist cover is cheap. Given the theft rate on e-bikes, it is worth checking.

Can I ride an e-bike on the pavement? No. A legal EAPC is treated exactly like a bicycle, so the same rules apply.

Is a second-hand e-bike a good idea? It can be, if you can verify the battery age and the pack is a model still sold new. Ask for the charge cycle count and the original charger. A cheap used e-bike with an unknown, aged battery is not a bargain.

What about e-scooters? Different rules entirely. Privately owned e-scooters remain illegal on UK public roads and pavements, and are only legal on private land with permission.
