Your Landlord Had Thirty Days. If They Missed It, the Court Can Order Three Times the Deposit

Deposit protection is the cheapest stage of a tenancy dispute to get right and the most expensive to ignore. The rules are short, the deadlines are fixed, and the penalty for a landlord who never protected the money is a multiple of it rather than a fine.

Ruhul Amin Published 14 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Thirty days. Your landlord or letting agent must put your deposit in an approved scheme within 30 days of getting it.
  • Three schemes in England and Wales: the Deposit Protection Service, MyDeposits, and the Tenancy Deposit Scheme. If your money is protected, one of those three holds the record.
  • Ten days to pay you back once you and the landlord have agreed how much you get. Not ten days from moving out.
  • Up to three times the deposit. If a county court finds the deposit was not protected when it should have been, it may order the landlord to pay you up to 3 times the deposit within 14 days.
  • The court fee is £387 on Form N208, and you can claim it back from the landlord if you win.
  • Assured periodic tenancies replaced assured shorthold tenancies on 1 May 2026, which is the tenancy type the protection duty now attaches to.

1. The duty, and the thirty days

GOV.UK: your landlord must put your deposit in a government-approved tenancy deposit scheme if you rent your home on an assured periodic tenancy. That tenancy type is newer than most of the advice on the internet, because assured periodic tenancies replaced assured shorthold tenancies on 1 May 2026. Any guide still framing the duty purely around an AST is describing the pre-May position.

The deadline is flat: "Your landlord or letting agent must put your deposit in the scheme within 30 days of getting it." Thirty days from receipt, not from the tenancy start, not from the day you moved in.

Three further points sit alongside it and each one closes a gap landlords sometimes argue about:

Holding deposits. A landlord does not have to protect money you pay simply to hold a property before an agreement is signed. But GOV.UK adds the important half: once you become a tenant, the holding deposit becomes a deposit, and they must protect it. It does not stay exempt because of what it was called at the start.

Deposits paid by someone else. The duty applies even if your deposit is paid by a third party, such as a rent deposit scheme run by a council or charity, or your parents. Whose bank account it came from is irrelevant.

Non-money deposits. If you do not rent on an assured periodic tenancy, a landlord can take a valuable item instead of cash, a car or a watch for example. GOV.UK says plainly that those items will not be protected by a scheme. If you are asked for something other than money, you are being offered something with no statutory protection attached.

2. How to check whether yours was protected

You do not have to ask your landlord, and in a dispute you should not have to. In England and Wales the three schemes are:

  • Deposit Protection Service, telephone 0330 303 0030
  • MyDeposits, including deposits that were held by Capita, telephone 0333 321 9401
  • Tenancy Deposit Scheme, telephone 0300 037 1000

Each runs a public check. Contact all three, not one, because you have no way of knowing which the landlord chose and a nil result from one proves nothing. Do it by a method that leaves a record, and keep the answers. If the money is protected you will get a scheme reference, which is what you will need at the end of the tenancy anyway.

Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate schemes with their own rules, so a tenancy there is checked through mygov.scot or nidirect rather than through the three above.

3. Getting it back at the end

The rule people misremember is this one. "Your landlord must return your deposit within 10 days of you both agreeing how much you'll get back."

The ten days runs from agreement, not from the end of the tenancy and not from the day you hand over the keys. Which means a landlord who simply does not respond is not in breach of the ten day rule, because agreement has not happened. That is why the useful move at the end of a tenancy is to put a specific figure in writing and ask for acceptance or a costed counter-proposal, rather than asking an open question like "when do I get my deposit back". A concrete number forces the clock to start or forces a dispute you can escalate.

If you are in a dispute, GOV.UK confirms the deposit stays protected in the scheme until the issue is sorted out. The schemes run free adjudication services, which is almost always the right first route. Nothing below about county court is a substitute for using the scheme's own dispute process when the deposit was protected.

4. What happens if it was never protected

This is the section landlords who cut corners do not expect tenants to have read.

If you believe your landlord has not used a scheme when they should have, you can apply to your local county court. GOV.UK says to get legal advice first, and also says outright that you do not need a solicitor to do this.

The route is Form N208, the Part 8 claim form, and the court fee is £387. You can claim that fee back from your landlord if you win, and you can apply for money off the court fee if you are on certain benefits or have a low income.

Before going anywhere near a court, GOV.UK's own advice is that writing to the landlord is quicker and cheaper. That is not a formality. A letter that quotes the 30 day duty, names the three schemes you have already checked with, and says what you will do next, resolves a large share of these situations without a claim.

If the court does find against the landlord, it can order them to either repay the deposit to you or pay it into a scheme's bank account within 14 days. And then the part that changes the arithmetic: "The court may also order the landlord to pay you up to 3 times the deposit within 14 days of making the order."

Note the honest limits of that sentence. It says may, and it says up to. It is a judicial discretion with a ceiling, not an automatic three times payout, and a landlord who protected the deposit late rather than never is in a different position from one who never protected it at all. Anyone telling you three times the deposit is guaranteed is selling something.

One further consequence is easy to miss and can matter more than the money: the court may decide that you will not have to leave the property when the tenancy ends if the landlord did not use a scheme when they should have.

5. Worked example: Leah in Southend-on-Sea

Leah paid a £1,150 deposit in a tenancy that started in June. In September she looks for the scheme reference and cannot find one.

Step one, evidence. She contacts all three schemes and gets three nil results in writing. Cost: nothing. Time: an afternoon. This is the step that turns a suspicion into a case.

Step two, the letter. She writes to the landlord setting out the date the deposit was paid, the 30 day duty, the three nil results, and a request that the deposit be protected and the prescribed information provided. She keeps a copy and sends it in a way she can prove.

Step three, the arithmetic if he ignores her. The claim route costs £387 on Form N208, recoverable if she wins. The deposit at stake is £1,150. The ceiling on the penalty the court may add is three times £1,150, so £3,450, and that is a ceiling rather than an expectation. Against the fee, the claim is plainly worth considering on the numbers alone, and if she qualifies for help with fees the fee itself may fall away.

The honest caveat. This is a court process with a real chance of going the other way, and the amount awarded is at the judge's discretion. It is also not the right route at all if the deposit was protected and the argument is about deductions. That situation goes to the scheme's free adjudication, not to a county court, and using the wrong route costs money and time.

The honest verdict

Deposit protection is a rare case where the law is short, the deadlines are numbers you can remember, and the remedy is proportionate to the money. Thirty days to protect, ten days from agreement to return, three schemes, and a court that may order up to three times the deposit if it was never protected at all.

The cheap stage is the one almost everybody skips: check all three schemes at the start of the tenancy, not at the end. A scheme reference in your email on day thirty-one removes the entire problem this article is about.

Where this does not help you: if your deposit was protected and you are arguing about cleaning, damage or rent arrears, court is the wrong door. Use the scheme's own free adjudication. And if you are in Scotland or Northern Ireland, the schemes and the rules are different, so start from the national guidance rather than this one.

Frequently asked

Does the 30 days run from the tenancy start? No. GOV.UK says within 30 days of the landlord or agent getting the deposit. If you paid before the tenancy began, the clock started when they received the money.

What if I paid a holding deposit? A holding deposit does not have to be protected while it is only holding the property. Once you become a tenant it becomes a deposit and must be protected like any other. The label does not carry over.

Can my landlord take a car or a watch instead of money? Only if you do not rent on an assured periodic tenancy. GOV.UK is clear that such items are not protected by any scheme, so you would be relying entirely on the landlord's goodwill to get the item back.

Is there free help with this? Yes. Citizens Advice, Shelter and your council's private renting or housing standards team all deal with deposit protection and none of them charge. Use them before paying anyone for advice on a claim of this size.
