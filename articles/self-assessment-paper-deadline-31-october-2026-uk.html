Paper Tax Returns Must Reach HMRC By 31 October. Online Gets You Three More Months And HMRC Does The Sum. Who Should Still Post One.

If you earned from a side hustle, rent or freelance work in the year to 5 April 2026, your return is due. There are two deadlines, and picking the wrong one costs at least £100. Paper returns must be in HMRC's hands by 31 October 2026. Online returns have until 31 January 2027, and the system works out the bill for you. Here is what each route involves, what the penalties actually are, and the small number of people for whom paper still makes sense.

1. The two deadlines and what each actually buys you

The return now due covers the tax year from 6 April 2025 to 5 April 2026. GOV.UK gives two filing deadlines. A paper return must be received by HMRC by 11:59pm on 31 October 2026; the date is receipt, not posting, so second class post in the last week is a gamble. An online return must be submitted by 11:59pm on 31 January 2027. Both can be sent any time from 6 April 2026.

What people get wrong is the payment date. Whichever way you file, the tax for 2025 to 2026 is due by 11:59pm on 31 January 2027, along with any first payment on account for 2026 to 2027. Filing on paper in October does not make you pay in October. It just gives HMRC three months to process the form and send you a calculation, which is the trade you are making: three months less to file, in exchange for HMRC doing the arithmetic.

Online does the arithmetic instantly, saves as you go, pre-fills PAYE and State Pension figures HMRC already holds, and lets you amend within 12 months. It needs a Government Gateway account, which takes a few days to set up if you have never had one, and that is the one genuine reason not to leave online filing until the last week of January.

There is a third date on the same page. If you owe less than £3,000 and want HMRC to collect it through your tax code across 2027 to 2028 instead of paying a lump sum, an online return must be in by 30 December 2026. For a paper return the same option is available if it arrives by 31 October.

2. The penalties, in order, with the interest rate

The penalties are the same whichever route you chose; only the date they start differs. From GOV.UK:

How lateLate filing penalty
1 day£100, even if no tax is due
3 months£10 a day, up to £900
6 months5% of the tax due or £300, whichever is greater
12 monthsA further 5% or £300, whichever is greater

So a paper return that arrives on 1 November 2026 has cost £100 for arriving a day late, while an identical return submitted online on 1 November would be three months early. That asymmetry is the whole reason this article exists.

Late payment is separate: 5% of the unpaid tax at 30 days, another 5% at six months and another at twelve, plus interest from 1 February 2027. HMRC's late payment interest rate has been 7.75% since 9 January 2026, set at Bank Rate plus 4 percentage points. On £2,000 of tax paid three months late that is about £38 of interest on top of the £100 penalty at 30 days.

Penalties can be appealed with a reasonable excuse, which HMRC interprets narrowly: serious illness, bereavement, a failure of HMRC's own system. "I did not realise the paper date was different" is not on the list.

3. Who should still file on paper

An honest list, because it is short.

People who cannot get online. No reliable internet, no device, a disability that makes the online service unusable. HMRC's digital exclusion route exists for exactly this group, and the helpline will send a paper form. Nobody in this group should feel pushed online; they should feel pushed to post early.

People whose return needs a form the online service does not carry. A few supplementary pages, such as some trust and estate or non-resident situations, still cannot be filed through HMRC's free online return. Commercial software usually can, but where it cannot, paper is the route and 31 October is the date.

People HMRC has specifically told to file on paper. Rare, but if the letter says so, do so.

Everyone else, which is the large majority of side-hustle, landlord and freelance filers, gains three months, a calculated bill, pre-filled figures and a confirmation receipt by filing online, at no cost. HMRC no longer posts paper returns automatically; you have to ask for one, which is a sign of where it wants people to be.

4. A worked example in Plymouth

Liam in Plymouth is a full-time care worker who earned £4,800 from weekend furniture restoration in 2025 to 2026, his second year of trading. His PAYE tax is already collected by his employer; the return is only for the side income. After the £1,000 trading allowance his taxable profit is £3,800, and as a basic-rate taxpayer he owes 20%, £760, plus Class 4 National Insurance at 6% on profits above £12,570, which for him is nil.

Paper. He requests a form, fills in the self-employment pages, and posts it in the third week of October. HMRC works out the £760 and writes to him in December. He pays by 31 January 2027. If he posts it on 28 October and it arrives on 2 November, he owes £100 before the tax.

Online. He logs into the Government Gateway account he set up last year, the PAYE figures from his care job are pre-filled, he enters £4,800 income and claims the trading allowance, and the screen shows £760. He files on 15 November. Because it is under £3,000 and filed before 30 December, he ticks the box to have it collected through his 2027 to 2028 tax code: about £63 a month from his wages from April 2027 instead of a £760 payment in January.

Same tax, £760. The online route gives him three extra months, a free instalment plan and no risk of the £100 for a slow envelope. There is no version of Liam's situation where paper wins.

5. The three October dates that matter for a side hustle

5 October 2026. If you have never filed before and had untaxed income over £1,000 in 2025 to 2026, you must register by this date. Our side hustle tax guide covers the £1,000 threshold. Register later and HMRC sets a filing deadline three months from its letter, but the tax is still due 31 January.

31 October 2026. Paper returns received. Not posted, received.

31 December 2026, in practice 30 December. The last date to file online and have a bill under £3,000 collected through your tax code.

Anyone whose 2026 to 2027 self-employment or property income will be over £50,000 is in Making Tax Digital for Income Tax from April 2026 and reports quarterly through software for that year; the return covered here, for 2025 to 2026, is still an ordinary Self Assessment return. We cover MTD separately.

6. The honest verdict

If you can file online, file online. Three extra months, HMRC does the sum, and under £3,000 can go through your tax code if you file by 30 December.

If you must file on paper, post it in early October, not late. The deadline is receipt by 31 October, the penalty is £100 for a day, and second class post in the last week is how people pay it.

Neither route changes when the tax is due: 31 January 2027. Paper does not make you pay early, and online does not let you pay late.

Frequently asked

Does filing a paper return mean I have to pay my tax by 31 October? No. The tax for 2025 to 2026 is due by 31 January 2027 whichever way you file. Paper only brings the filing deadline forward.

Can I file on paper by 31 October and then online as well? Do not file twice. If you have filed on paper and want to amend, you can do that within 12 months. If you have not yet filed, choose one route.

Will HMRC send me a paper form? Not automatically. You have to request one from HMRC, and it must still be received by 31 October 2026.

I owe £1,500. Can it come out of my wages instead? Yes, if you file online by 30 December 2026 (or on paper by 31 October), owe under £3,000, and already pay tax through PAYE. HMRC collects it through your 2027 to 2028 tax code.
