Up to £30,000 from your council to adapt your home, and the mistake that voids it

A Disabled Facilities Grant pays for the ramp, the stairlift, the level access shower or the downstairs bedroom that lets someone stay in their own home. It does not affect any benefit you get. And for a disabled child under 18, the parents' income is not looked at at all.

The short version

  • Up to £30,000 in England, £36,000 in Wales and £25,000 in Northern Ireland. GOV.UK adds that some councils may give more (Disabled Facilities Grants, page updated 2 September 2026).
  • Scotland has no Disabled Facilities Grant. Support for equipment and adaptations runs through a separate Scottish system.
  • A disabled child under 18 can get a grant without their parents' income being taken into account. That waiver is the single most valuable line on the page.
  • Landlords can get a grant with no income or savings test, but the council may require the property to be let to another disabled person if the tenant moves within 5 years.
  • Start the work before the council approves your application and you might get nothing. Approval first, contractor second, in that order.

1. What it pays for, and who counts as disabled

A Disabled Facilities Grant comes from your local council, not from DWP, and it pays towards changes to your home so that a disabled person can carry on living there.

GOV.UK's list of who it is for is wider than the word "disabled" suggests to most people. It includes anyone who:

  • is physically disabled
  • has a learning disability
  • has age-related needs
  • is autistic
  • has a cognitive impairment, like dementia
  • has a progressive condition, like motor neurone disease
  • has a terminal illness
  • has a mental health condition

"Age-related needs" and "a mental health condition" are both on that list, and both are routinely assumed to be outside it. They are not.

The works GOV.UK gives as examples are equally broad:

  • widening doors and installing ramps or grab rails
  • improving access to rooms and facilities, for example with a stairlift or a level access shower
  • improving access to your garden
  • building an extension, for example a downstairs bedroom
  • providing a heating system suitable for your needs
  • adapting heating or lighting controls to make them easier to use

An extension is on that list. A downstairs bedroom is explicitly given as an example. People rule themselves out because they assume a grant of this kind means grab rails and nothing more.

And one line that removes the usual objection: "A Disabled Facilities Grant will not affect any benefits you get." It is not income, it is not capital in the way that matters here, and it does not reduce anything else.

2. The four caps, and what your income does to them

The maximum depends on where you live.

CountryMaximum grant
EnglandUp to £30,000
WalesUp to £36,000
Northern IrelandUp to £25,000
ScotlandNo Disabled Facilities Grant; a separate system covers equipment and adaptations

GOV.UK adds four words that are worth taking seriously: "Some councils may give more." The figures above are statutory maximums for the mandatory grant, not a ceiling on what a council is allowed to help with. If a quote comes in above the cap, that is a conversation to have with the council rather than an automatic dead end.

How much of it you actually get usually depends on two things:

  • your household income
  • your household savings over £6,000

Note the shape of that. Savings below £6,000 are ignored. There is no stated upper savings limit that shuts the door entirely, unlike Housing Benefit's £16,000 line. What savings above £6,000 do is increase the contribution you are expected to make. GOV.UK puts it plainly: "Depending on your income, you may need to pay towards the cost of the work to the property."

So the realistic outcome for many households is a grant plus a contribution, not all or nothing. Ask the council for the assessment in writing, with the contribution figure and how it was reached, before you commit to anything.

One more useful line: you can apply for another grant for the same property if you need to, for example if your condition changes. A grant used for a stairlift five years ago does not exhaust your entitlement if you now need a level access shower.

3. The waiver: children under 18, and landlords

Two groups skip the means test completely, and they are the most valuable sentences on the whole GOV.UK page.

Disabled children under 18. GOV.UK: "Disabled children under 18 can get a grant without their parents' income being taken into account." Not reduced, not tapered. Not taken into account.

The practical effect is large. A working household with a decent income and some savings, who would face a substantial contribution on an adult application, faces none on an application for their disabled child. GOV.UK tells you to contact your local council for more information, which is the right next step, but go into that conversation knowing the waiver exists, because it is not always volunteered.

Landlords. "Landlords can get a grant without their income and savings being taken into account." There is a condition attached: the council may ask for the property to be let to another disabled person if the current tenant moves within 5 years.

That matters to private renters more than it does to landlords. If you rent privately and need an adaptation, the application can be made by your landlord without the landlord's finances being assessed, and without your own being the barrier. A landlord who says "I cannot afford it" may not realise they are not being asked to.

4. The Ellis family in Kettering: £27,400 of work, nothing to pay

Sam Ellis is 9 and lives with his parents in Kettering, in a three bedroom house with the only bathroom upstairs. The occupational therapist's assessment recommends a level access shower on the ground floor and a small rear extension for a downstairs bedroom.

The council's approved contractor quotes £27,400.

The cap: England, so up to £30,000. The quote is inside it.

The means test: Sam is a disabled child under 18, so his parents' income is not taken into account. His father earns £41,000 and the household has £19,000 in savings. Neither figure is looked at.

The contribution: nil. The grant covers the £27,400.

Now change one fact. Suppose the applicant were Sam's grandmother, living in the same house, with the same works and the same quote. She is an adult, so the household income and the savings above £6,000 are assessed, and a contribution is likely. Same house, same builder, same invoice, very different answer, decided entirely by who the adaptation is for.

What the family should not do is book the builder while the paperwork is with the council. GOV.UK: "You might not get any grant if you start work on your property before the council approves your application." That is the whole £27,400 at risk to save a few weeks.

On payment, the council will either pay in instalments as the work progresses, or in full when it is finished. It may pay the contractor directly or give the family the money to pass on, and it agrees which when it approves the application. Payment is triggered either when the family and the council are both happy with the finished work, or when the invoice or receipt from the contractor is handed over.

One caveat on doing it yourself. GOV.UK says that normally, if you or a relative do the work, the council will only accept invoices for materials or services you have bought. Your own labour is not claimable, so a family member who is a builder cannot invoice the grant for their time.

5. Applying without wrecking the claim

The order of the steps is the part that goes wrong.

  1. Ask the council for an assessment first. In most areas this means an occupational therapist visit, arranged through adult or children's social care. The assessment is what establishes that the works are necessary and appropriate, which is the legal test.
  2. Get the application approved in writing before any work starts. This is the one that voids grants. No approval, no grant, regardless of how obviously necessary the work was.
  3. Ask for the means test calculation on paper, including any contribution and how savings above £6,000 were treated. If you are applying for a child under 18, check the waiver has actually been applied.
  4. Keep every invoice and receipt, because payment is released against them.
  5. If your needs change later, apply again. A previous grant on the same property does not use up the entitlement.

Waiting times are the honest weak point of this scheme. The assessment, the approval and the works are three separate queues in most councils, and the whole process commonly runs into many months. That is not a reason to skip the approval step, but it is a reason to start early and to chase in writing.

Free help exists and is worth using. Your council's home improvement agency, where it has one, will often manage the whole process including the contractor. Citizens Advice can help if the council refuses or the contribution looks wrong. Scope and the Disability Law Service both give free advice on adaptations and on challenging a decision. None of them charge, and none of them are the companies that advertise stairlifts.

The verdict

If someone in your household is struggling with the stairs, the bath or the front step, this is the first thing to check and most people never do. Up to £30,000 in England, more in Wales, and an extension for a downstairs bedroom is explicitly within scope. If the adaptation is for a disabled child under 18, the parents' income is not assessed at all, which turns a large contribution into none. If you rent privately, your landlord can apply without their finances being tested. The one thing that will cost you the whole grant is starting the work before the council has approved it, so get the approval in writing first and let the queue be slow.

Frequently asked

Does a Disabled Facilities Grant affect my benefits? No. GOV.UK states plainly that it will not affect any benefits you get. It is a grant for works to the property, not income to you.

What if the quote is more than the cap? Talk to the council rather than abandoning it. GOV.UK says some councils may give more than the statutory maximum. Councils also have discretionary powers to help beyond the mandatory grant, and a home improvement agency will know what your council actually does.

Do I have to own my home? No. Tenants can apply, and landlords can apply on behalf of a disabled tenant without their own income and savings being assessed. If your landlord applies, the council may ask that the property is let to another disabled person if you move within 5 years.

Can I use my own builder? Councils usually have an approved list and may require you to use it, so ask before you get quotes. If you or a relative do the work yourselves, the council will normally only accept invoices for materials or services bought, not for your own labour.
