The Immersion Heater Is The Most Expensive Switch In The House. It Costs Three Times What The Boiler Does, And Most People Leave It On By Accident.

Every home with a hot water cylinder has two ways to heat it: the boiler, through a coil, or the immersion, an electric element in the tank. They do the same job, and one costs three times the other, because electricity is 26.32p a unit under the October 2026 cap and gas is 7.97p. This article prices a tank of hot water both ways, prices a year of each, and sets out the four situations where the immersion is nonetheless the right choice and the one setting that stops it costing a fortune when it is not.

The short version

  • Heating a 120 litre cylinder from 15 to 60 degrees takes about 6.3 kWh of heat, our arithmetic from the physics of water. A standard immersion element is 3 kW, so that is about two hours of it running.
  • By immersion at 26.32p: £1.65 a tank. By gas boiler at 7.97p and 90 per cent efficiency: 56p. The immersion costs 2.9 times as much for identical hot water.
  • A household that heats the tank once a day pays £603 a year by immersion or £204 by gas, a gap of about £400. Twice a day, double it.
  • An immersion left on permanently does not heat the water endlessly; it tops up the standing loss. An uninsulated or badly insulated cylinder loses heat all day, and the Energy Saving Trust puts insulating one to 80 mm at £45 a year saved. By immersion, that loss costs three times as much again.
  • The immersion is the right choice when: the boiler is broken, the home has no gas, you have a cheap off-peak electricity window and a timer set to it, or you have solar panels and a diverter sending spare generation into the tank.
  • The fix for everyone else is a timer and a thermostat: an immersion on a timer for an hour before the morning shower, with the boiler doing the rest, costs pennies a day; an immersion on a wall switch left on costs pounds.

1. What it takes to heat a tank, and what each fuel charges for it

Water needs 4.186 kilojoules to raise each litre by one degree; that is a physical constant, not an estimate. A typical family hot water cylinder holds 120 litres, though 90 and 150 litre tanks are common, and a cylinder thermostat is normally set to 60 degrees, the temperature that keeps legionella at bay. Cold mains water in a British winter arrives at around 10 to 15 degrees.

So a full tank from cold, 120 litres raised by 45 degrees, takes 120 times 4.186 times 45 kilojoules, which is 22,600 kJ, or 6.28 kWh of heat. In practice you rarely heat a tank from stone cold, because the cylinder is still warm from yesterday and the top of the tank is drawn off before the bottom; but a household that uses its hot water fully each day is putting roughly that much heat in each day, so it is the right figure for a daily sum.

By immersion. An immersion heater is a resistive element, and the standard domestic size is 3 kW. It converts electricity to heat at essentially 100 per cent, so 6.28 kWh of heat is 6.28 kWh of electricity, about two hours and five minutes of the element running. At the Ofgem cap unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p, that is £1.65.

By gas boiler. The boiler heats the same water through a coil in the cylinder, and a modern condensing boiler turns about 90 per cent of its gas into heat delivered, so 6.28 kWh of heat needs about 6.98 kWh of gas. At the October cap rate of 7.97p, that is 56p. An older non-condensing boiler at 75 per cent efficiency makes it 67p; still well under half the immersion.

The ratio, 2.9 to 1, is simply the fuel price ratio of 3.3 to 1 with the boiler's inefficiency taken off. It is the same sum as an induction hob against a gas hob or an electric heater against a radiator, and it comes out the same way every time: for the same heat, mains gas is a third of the price of grid electricity in the winter of 2026.

3. Standing losses, the cylinder jacket and the left-on switch

A hot cylinder loses heat to the airing cupboard all day and night. A modern factory-insulated cylinder loses perhaps 1 to 1.5 kWh a day; an old copper cylinder with a thin or missing jacket can lose two or three times that. An immersion left switched on does not boil the tank dry; its thermostat cycles it to replace exactly that loss, all day, at 26.32p a unit. That is why the "backup" immersion that someone switched on in January and forgot about costs £100 to £300 a year while apparently doing nothing.

The Energy Saving Trust's boiler page says that upgrading a cylinder's insulation to 80 mm saves around £45 a year in Great Britain. That figure is for a gas-heated cylinder. If the same cylinder is kept hot by immersion, the same lost heat costs three times as much, so the jacket saves roughly £130 a year, and a jacket costs £15 to £25. There is no faster payback in the house; it is measured in weeks either way.

The three settings that decide it. First, the cylinder thermostat: 60 degrees, not higher, because every degree above is heat you pay for and then mix back down with cold at the tap. Second, the immersion's own thermostat, which should also be 60. Third, and most important, a timer on the immersion circuit. A timer set to bring the immersion on for an hour before the morning shower, with the boiler on its own programme doing the rest, means the immersion only ever runs when you meant it to. A wall switch with a neon on it is a licence to forget.

How to tell which is heating your water now. Look at the boiler programmer: if hot water is timed on and the boiler fires when it should, the coil is doing the work. Then look at the immersion switch, usually a fused spur near the cylinder with a neon; if the neon is lit, both are on, and the immersion will take over whenever the boiler's water is slightly cooler than its own thermostat wants. Turn it off and see whether anyone notices. Usually nobody does.

4. The four times the immersion is the right button

The boiler is broken. This is what the immersion is for. A week of immersion-heated water while you wait for the engineer costs about £7 more than the boiler would have; that is a bargain against cold showers, and it is the reason to keep the immersion working and to test it once a year.

The home has no gas. Then the cylinder is electric or nothing, and the question is not immersion against boiler but immersion at the day rate against immersion at an off-peak rate. If your tariff has a cheap overnight window, put the immersion on a timer inside it and size the cylinder to last the day; that is the whole art of an all-electric hot water system, and it is worth doing properly.

You have solar panels. A solar diverter sends the electricity you would otherwise export, paid at around 12p a unit under a typical Smart Export Guarantee tariff, into the immersion instead. Heat you would have sold for 12p replaces gas you would have bought for 7.97p a unit plus boiler losses, so it is roughly a wash on a good export tariff and a clear win on a poor one; the diverter costs a few hundred pounds and the sum depends entirely on your export rate.

You have a heat pump or an off-peak tariff and a modern cylinder. Some systems use the immersion for a weekly legionella cycle to 60 degrees while running the rest of the time at a lower, cheaper temperature. That is by design and costs pennies.

In every other home, the immersion should be off, on a timer, and checked.

5. Worked example: Jack and Lena in Plymouth

Three-bed semi, gas system boiler with a 120 litre copper cylinder in the airing cupboard, immersion on a wall switch with a neon, two adults and one child

Lena noticed the electricity bill had been high since the winter. The neon by the cylinder had been on since the boiler was serviced in February, when the engineer used the immersion to test the cylinder and nobody switched it back.

What it has been doing. With the boiler also timed on, the immersion has been topping up the standing loss and any shortfall, call it 2 kWh a day on a thinly jacketed old cylinder. 53p a day, £192 a year, for hot water the boiler was already providing at a third of the price.

The fix. Switch it off: saving about £190 a year, cost nil, payback immediate. Fit an 80 mm jacket, about £20: Energy Saving Trust saving £45 a year on gas, payback about five months. Ask the electrician who is coming for another job to put a timer on the immersion spur, about £40 fitted, set for one hour at 6am as a backup and otherwise off.

What they keep. The immersion itself, tested once a year. The week the boiler broke last November, it cost them about £7 more than gas would have, and they were glad of it.

Total: about £60 spent, about £235 a year saved. No purchase of anything larger than a jacket.

6. The honest verdict

If you have a gas boiler and a cylinder, heat the water with the boiler, every day, and keep the immersion for the week the boiler fails. At the October 2026 cap the immersion costs £1.65 a tank against the boiler's 56p, and a household heating its water by immersion out of habit is paying about £400 a year for nothing. Go and look at the neon by the cylinder tonight.

Buy nothing except a cylinder jacket if yours is thin, at £15 to £25 for the Energy Saving Trust's £45 a year, and a timer for the immersion spur if the switch has a history of being left on. The immersion is only the right button in a home with no gas, on an off-peak tariff with a timer, or with solar panels and a diverter. Everyone else should find it switched off.

Frequently asked

How much does an immersion heater cost to run per hour? A standard 3 kW immersion costs 79p an hour at full power at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p. Heating a 120 litre tank from cold takes about two hours, £1.65; once the tank is hot the thermostat cycles it and the cost per hour falls to whatever the standing loss is.

Is it cheaper to heat water with the boiler or the immersion? The boiler, by about three to one, if it runs on mains gas. A tank that costs £1.65 by immersion costs about 56p by a condensing boiler at 7.97p per kWh and 90 per cent efficiency. Only a home with no gas, a cheap off-peak tariff, or solar panels and a diverter should heat water by immersion routinely.

Does leaving the immersion on all the time cost a lot? Yes, even when the boiler is also heating the water, because the immersion replaces the cylinder's standing heat loss at the electricity price. On an old, thinly insulated cylinder that can be £100 to £300 a year for water you were already getting from the boiler.

Is a hot water cylinder jacket worth it? The Energy Saving Trust puts insulating a cylinder to 80 mm at around £45 a year saved on a gas-heated cylinder, and the jacket costs £15 to £25. On an immersion-heated cylinder the same lost heat costs three times as much, so the jacket saves around £130 a year. Either way it pays back in weeks.
