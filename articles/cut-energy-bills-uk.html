Cutting Your Energy Bills in the UK: What Saves Real Money

21 July 2026 · 6 min read · Checked July 2026

The short version
  • The price cap from 1 July to 30 September 2026 is £1,663 a year for a typical dual fuel direct debit household. That figure fell from £1,862 partly because Ofgem cut its assumed usage: households now use 7% less electricity and 17% less gas than at the previous review.
  • The next cap is announced in late August and applies from 1 October 2026. Analysts expect a rise, with gas unit rates up around 5% and electricity unit rates broadly flat.
  • The cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not your bill. Use more, pay more. There is no ceiling on what you can be charged in total.
  • Heating is the prize. Space and water heating is roughly 80% of a typical home's energy. Everything you do to the kettle and the lightbulbs is a rounding error next to the thermostat.
  • The single biggest saving available to most people is switching off the standard variable tariff, which is what the cap applies to. Fixed deals have been available below cap through 2026.

1. What the price cap actually caps

This is the most widely misunderstood number in British household finance, so it is worth being precise.

The cap sets a maximum unit rate in pence per kWh and a maximum daily standing charge. It does not cap your bill. The widely reported £1,663 figure is what a household using Ofgem's assumed typical volumes would pay over a year. Use double that energy and you pay roughly double.

Two consequences follow.

The standing charge is unavoidable. You pay it every day whether you use anything or not, on both fuels. For a low usage household it can be a third of the bill, which is why cutting consumption has a floor.

The cap only applies to standard variable tariffs. If you are on a fixed deal, the cap is irrelevant to you until that deal ends. Roughly a third of households never leave the variable tariff, and they are the ones the cap exists to protect and the ones who could most easily beat it.

Ofgem price cap, 1 July to 30 September 2026. The October cap is announced in late August 2026 and applies from 1 October. Check ofgem.gov.uk for the current figure before acting on any of this.

2. Where your money really goes

People cut the wrong things because the visible energy use is not the expensive energy use.

UseShare of typical home energy
Space heatingAbout 60 to 65%
Hot waterAbout 15 to 20%
Cooking, appliances, lighting, electronicsThe remaining 15 to 25%

So a thermostat turned down one degree beats every gadget in the house. Standby power, which gets enormous attention, is typically £30 to £80 a year across a whole home. Worth doing. Not worth thinking about first.

3. The free changes, ranked by saving

  1. Turn the thermostat down 1°C. Around £90 to £140 a year. Nothing else free comes close. Most homes are heated to 21 to 22°C; 18 to 19°C is the recommended range for a healthy adult household. Do not do this if someone is elderly, very young or unwell.
  2. Set the boiler flow temperature to 60°C. Around £90 to £120 a year on a combi boiler. Most are installed at 70 to 80°C, which stops a condensing boiler from actually condensing, throwing away roughly 6 to 9% of its efficiency. This is the single most overlooked free change in British homes.
  3. Only heat rooms you use. Turn radiator valves down in spare rooms, keeping them just above frost setting. £50 to £120.
  4. Time the heating properly. Heating an empty house is pure waste. Two well timed blocks beat all day at a lower temperature in most homes. £60 to £150.
  5. Shorter showers. Cutting from ten minutes to five saves roughly £60 a year per person in a household with a combi boiler.
  6. Wash at 30°C and skip the tumble dryer where you can. A tumble dryer is one of the most expensive appliances in the house at roughly 60p to 90p per cycle.
  7. Give the freezer some room and defrost it. A frosted freezer works considerably harder.

Total realistic saving from the free list, for a typical three bedroom home: £250 to £500 a year, without spending anything.

5. Things that are not worth it

Being honest about this matters more than the savings list.

  • Energy saving plugs and voltage optimisers sold online. The ones marketed to consumers for £30 to £80 do essentially nothing on a modern domestic supply. Several have been the subject of ASA rulings.
  • Replacing a working boiler purely to save energy. A new boiler is £2,000 to £3,500. If yours is a working condensing boiler, the payback is measured in decades. Replace it when it fails, not before.
  • Heated clothes airers, as an energy saving. They genuinely beat a tumble dryer, at roughly 4p to 8p an hour. They do not beat a clothes horse, which is free.
  • Solar, on the payback argument alone, if you are moving within five years. Typical payback is currently around 8 to 14 years depending on system size, export rate and how much of your own generation you use. That can be a good long term investment. It is not a quick saving.
  • Switching supplier for the sake of it. Cheaper deals exist, but check exit fees and whether the fix is genuinely below the cap over its whole term, not just today.

7. Help you might be entitled to

  • Warm Home Discount. A one off deduction from your electricity bill for eligible low income households. Applied automatically for most people who qualify.
  • Winter Fuel Payment. Eligibility rules changed recently and are politically contested, so check the current position on gov.uk rather than relying on what you remember.
  • Cold Weather Payment. Triggered automatically when the local temperature is recorded or forecast at zero or below for seven consecutive days.
  • ECO4 and the Great British Insulation Scheme. Free or heavily subsidised insulation for qualifying households, based on benefits, income or property EPC band.
  • Priority Services Register. Free, and not means tested. If you are of pensionable age, disabled, chronically ill or have young children, register with your supplier for extra support during outages.
  • Supplier hardship funds. Several of the large suppliers run grant schemes that can clear energy debt outright. British Gas runs one open to non customers.

If you are in arrears, contact your supplier before it escalates. They are obliged to offer a payment plan you can afford, and a plan agreed early is far better than a prepayment meter installed under warrant.

Myths that cost people money

"Leave the heating on low all day, it is cheaper than heating from cold." This is wrong for almost every home. Heat leaks out continuously in proportion to the temperature difference, so a warm empty house leaks all day. The Energy Saving Trust position is unambiguous: heat when you need it. The exception is a very well insulated home with a heat pump, where a lower, steadier flow temperature genuinely is more efficient.

"The price cap means my bill cannot go above £1,663." It means nothing of the sort. It caps the rate, not the total.

"Turning appliances off at the wall is the big win." It is £30 to £80 a year. Do it. Just do the thermostat first.

The honest bottom line

Two changes, both free, both taking under fifteen minutes: drop the boiler flow temperature to 60°C and drop the thermostat by one degree. Together that is typically £200 to £250 a year, and it is more than every gadget, plug and clever device on the market combined.

Everything after that is refinement. Draught proofing and a cylinder jacket for £65. Then check whether a fix beats the cap.

And the plain part: if your bill is high because your home is cold, badly insulated and you are already rationing, none of this is your fault and there is a limit to what behaviour change can do. The insulation schemes and the hardship funds exist for exactly that, and they are underclaimed.

Frequently asked

When does the next price cap change? Every three months. The October 2026 cap is announced in late August 2026 and applies from 1 October.

Should I fix now? Nobody can answer that honestly for you, because it depends on where wholesale prices go. What you can do is compare the fixed rate against the current cap over the whole fixed term, and check the exit fee. If a fix is meaningfully below cap and you value certainty, it is a defensible choice. This is general information, not financial advice.

Do smart meters save money? Not by themselves. They save money if seeing the numbers changes your behaviour, and they are required for most time of use tariffs, which can be worth a lot if you can shift usage.

Is a heat pump worth it? With the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant it can be, in a well insulated home with a correctly sized system and a low flow temperature. In a draughty solid wall house with existing small radiators, it will disappoint and cost more to run than gas. The insulation comes first, always.
