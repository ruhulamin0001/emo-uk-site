The MOT test is capped at £54.85. The bit that costs you money is not capped at all

DVSA sets a maximum fee for the test itself and a garage cannot go above it. Nothing caps the repairs, and the gap between those two facts is where the money goes. The retest rules decide whether you pay the fee once or twice.

Ruhul Amin Published 6 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The maximum MOT fee is £54.85 for a car and £29.65 for a standard motorcycle, and you do not pay VAT on the fee (GOV.UK, Getting an MOT, page updated 2 September 2026).
  • The cap is on the test, not on the repairs. A garage can charge whatever it likes for the work that follows a failure.
  • Leave the vehicle at the test centre for repair and a partial retest within 10 working days is free. Take it away and the rules change.
  • Bring it back before the end of the next working day and a long list of items is retested for nothing, including lamps, mirrors, seatbelts, wheels and tyres, and windscreen wipers.
  • Testing early costs you cover. You can test up to a month minus a day early and keep your renewal date. Test earlier than that and the new expiry runs from the test, not from the old date.

1. What the cap actually is

GOV.UK is unambiguous: "There's a maximum amount MOT test stations can charge. This depends on the type of vehicle." For the two vehicles most people own:

  • Car, up to 8 passenger seats: £54.85
  • Standard motorcycle: £29.65

And a detail that gets missed: you do not pay VAT on the fee. So the cap is the cap, not a figure with 20% still to come.

The full table is longer than most drivers realise, and a few entries are worth knowing:

VehicleClassMaximum fee
Motorcycle, any engine size1 or 2£29.65
Motorcycle with sidecar1 or 2£37.80
Car, motor caravan, dual purpose vehicle4£54.85
Goods vehicle up to 3,000kg4£54.85
Private passenger vehicle, 9 to 12 seats4£57.30
Goods vehicle over 3,000kg up to 3,500kg7£58.60

Two things follow from the table. A motor caravan is class 4, so it is capped at the same £54.85 as a car, which surprises a lot of owners who have been quoted more. And a van up to 3,000kg design gross weight is also class 4, while a heavier one is class 7 at £58.60. If you drive a van, the weight on the plate decides the price.

Ambulances and taxis need their first MOT at 1 year rather than 3, as do private passenger vehicles with 9 or more seats. Everything else on the ordinary list is 3 years.

The practical use of all this: if a garage quotes you above your class maximum for the test, that is not allowed, and you can say so with the GOV.UK table open on your phone. Many garages charge below the cap, and the cap is a ceiling rather than a price.

2. What is not capped

Everything else.

The MOT is an inspection, not a service. When it fails, the garage quotes for repairs and that quote is a normal commercial price with no maximum attached to it, no VAT exemption, and no obligation on you to have the work done there.

Three things worth being clear about.

  • You can take the vehicle elsewhere for the repairs. A failure certificate does not tie you to the testing garage. Whether that is sensible depends on the retest rules below, because moving the car can turn a free retest into a paid one.
  • An advisory is not a failure. Advisories are things the tester thinks will need attention before long. They do not stop the certificate being issued and they do not have to be fixed now. A garage presenting a list of advisories as work that must be done today is selling, not testing.
  • Repair prices are not capped and not standard. A second quote for the same failure item is a reasonable thing to get, particularly on anything structural or on brakes and suspension where labour dominates the bill.

3. The three retest windows

This is the part that decides whether the £54.85 gets spent once or twice, and GOV.UK sets out three distinct situations.

You leave the vehicle at the test centre for repair. If it is retested within 10 working days you only need a partial retest and there is no fee for this. This is the cheapest route and it is the one people forget exists, because the instinct after a failure is to take the car home.

You take it away and bring it back before the end of the next working day. You will not have to pay again for a partial retest on one or more of a long list of items. The list is worth reading in full on GOV.UK, but it includes lamps (excluding headlamp aim), mirrors, seatbelts, seats, the horn, registration plates, doors, the fuel filler cap, the steering wheel, windscreen glass, wipers and washers, electrical wiring, tyre pressure monitoring, and wheels and tyres for anything other than a motorcycle.

That covers a very large share of ordinary MOT failures. A blown bulb, a worn tyre, a split wiper blade and a broken mirror are all on it.

You take it away and bring it back within 10 working days. You still only need a partial retest, but GOV.UK says you can be charged a partial retest fee for this. Cheaper than a full test, but not free.

Anything else. "In all other cases, you'll need to get a full retest and pay the full MOT test fee again."

So the decision at the moment of failure is a financial one, and it has a clear order of preference: leave it there if the garage can do the work, otherwise fix it and return by the end of the next working day, otherwise return inside 10 working days, and only after that does the full fee come round again.

4. Denise in Ashford: £54.85 or £109.70

Denise takes her car for its MOT in Ashford. It fails on a headlamp bulb and a worn front tyre. Both are on the next working day list.

Route one. She leaves the car at the test centre and asks them to do the work. The partial retest inside 10 working days is free. Total test cost: £54.85, plus whatever the bulb and the tyre come to.

Route two. She drives it to her usual garage, has the bulb and the tyre done that afternoon, and brings it back to the test centre the next morning. The partial retest is free because both items are on the list. Total test cost: £54.85.

Route three. She is busy, and the car goes back three weeks later. That is outside 10 working days, so it is a full retest at the full fee. Total test cost: £109.70.

The same two faults, the same two parts, and a £54.85 difference decided entirely by the calendar.

There is a second cost hiding in route three. Her MOT has expired, so she cannot drive the car normally. GOV.UK allows only two exceptions: driving it to or from somewhere to be repaired, and driving it to a pre-arranged MOT test. Everything else is off the road, and she cannot renew her vehicle tax either, because you cannot renew tax without a valid MOT.

Note also what the retest page says about those permitted journeys: the vehicle "still needs to meet the minimum standards of roadworthiness at all times or you can be fined". Driving to the garage is not a legal shield for a car that is genuinely unsafe.

5. The renewal date trap, and driving without an MOT

An MOT lasts a year, and there is a rule about how early you can renew it that quietly costs people money every year.

You can get an MOT up to a month minus a day before it runs out and keep the same renewal date. GOV.UK's own example: if your MOT runs out on 15 May, the earliest you can test and keep the date is 16 April.

Test earlier than that and the date moves. Their second example is the one to remember: MOT due to run out on 15 May, tested on 14 April and passed, so the new expiry becomes 13 April the following year. Testing one day too early cost a month and a day of cover, permanently, because every subsequent year now runs from the earlier date.

If you are the sort of person who books things early, that single day is worth checking before you book.

The fine. "You can be fined up to £1,000 for driving a vehicle without a valid MOT."

And the tax link. If your MOT has run out and your vehicle tax is due, GOV.UK's own sequence is: register the vehicle as off the road with a SORN first, then book the MOT, then tax it once it has passed. You cannot renew vehicle tax while the MOT is expired, so an expired MOT quickly becomes two problems rather than one.

Finally, if you think the test result is wrong, there is a formal appeals route through DVSA rather than an argument at the counter. GOV.UK has a page on test result appeals and problems, and it is free to use.

The verdict

The number to hold on to is £54.85 for a car and £29.65 for a motorcycle, with no VAT, and it applies to the test only. If your car fails, the cheapest thing you can do is leave it at the test centre for the repair, because the partial retest inside 10 working days is then free. If you have to take it away, get it back before the end of the next working day and check whether your failure item is on GOV.UK's free retest list, because most common ones are. And if you like booking early, do not book more than a month minus a day ahead, because that is the difference between keeping your renewal date and losing part of a year of cover for good.

Frequently asked

Can a garage charge more than £54.85 for a car MOT? Not for the test itself. GOV.UK publishes the maximum fee by vehicle class and £54.85 is the maximum for a class 4 car. Many garages charge less. What is not capped is any repair work, which is a separate commercial price.

Is the retest always free? No. It is free if you leave the vehicle at the test centre and it is retested within 10 working days, and free if you take it away and bring it back before the end of the next working day for one of the listed items. If you return within 10 working days having taken it away, a partial retest fee can be charged. Beyond that, it is a full test at the full fee.

Can I drive the car if it failed its MOT? Only if your existing MOT is still valid, or in two narrow situations if it has expired: driving to or from somewhere to be repaired, and driving to a pre-arranged MOT test. The vehicle must still meet minimum roadworthiness standards at all times, or you can be fined.

Do I have to fix the advisories? No. Advisories are not failures and the certificate is issued regardless. They are a warning about what is likely to need attention before the next test, which is useful information, not a bill you have to accept today.
