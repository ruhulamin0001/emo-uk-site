The Sausage Roll Is Not Wages: Reading a Mystery Shopping Fee Properly

Mystery shopping assignments are advertised with two numbers added together, and only one of them is money you get to keep. Separating them takes thirty seconds and changes the answer completely. There is also a tax trap in the half you do not keep.

Ruhul Amin Published 15 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Every assignment has two parts. A fee, which is your pay, and a reimbursement, which is your own money coming back for something you were told to buy. Only the fee is earnings.
  • Work the hourly rate on the fee alone. Travel time, the visit and the report all count as time. The reimbursement does not reduce any of that time.
  • The comparison number from April 2026 is £12.71 an hour for workers aged 21 and over, £10.85 for 18 to 20 year olds and £8 for under 18s.
  • You are almost certainly not a worker, so that rate does not protect you. It is a benchmark for deciding whether the work is worth doing, not a right you can enforce as a self-employed shopper.
  • The tax trap: HMRC measures the £1,000 trading allowance on GROSS income. Gross means the total before any expenses are taken off, so the reimbursement counts towards the threshold even though you spent it.
  • Over £1,000 gross and you must register for Self Assessment by 5 October in the following tax year.

1. The two numbers in every assignment

A mystery shopping job is advertised as a single figure surprisingly often, and that figure is usually two different things added together.

The fee. What the company pays you for doing the visit and filing the report. This is your income. It is frequently small.

The reimbursement. Money back for something the brief required you to buy, up to a stated limit. A coffee, a meal, a small purchase you then have to keep the receipt for. This is not pay. It is your own money returning to you, sometimes weeks later, in exchange for an item you may not have wanted.

The reason this matters is that an assignment advertised as "£25" might be a £6 fee and up to £19 of reimbursement. Your actual earnings are £6. The other £19 left your bank account first and came back second, and if you would not otherwise have bought the item, the reimbursement is not a benefit at all. It is a forced purchase you were refunded for.

Some briefs handle this honestly and print both numbers separately. Others do not. If an advert gives you one number, the first question to ask the company is what the fee is on its own. If they will not split it, that tells you something.

2. Doing the hourly sum honestly

Once the fee is separated out, the next mistake is counting only the time inside the shop.

The real time on a mystery shop is: travel there, waiting, the visit itself, travel back, and then the report. The report is the part people forget, and it is often the longest single block, because these forms want timings, staff descriptions, verbatim quotes and photographs, and they usually have to be submitted within a fixed window.

So the sum is:

Fee, divided by (travel + visit + report), minus any cost you were not reimbursed for.

That last term matters more than it looks. Fuel, parking and bus fares are frequently not reimbursed at all, and they are real money spent to earn the fee.

Here is the honest thing to say about that arithmetic: for a single visit that needs a car journey, it very often comes out below any wage you would accept from an employer. Not always. But often, and the adverts are not designed to make that visible.

3. What to compare it against

The obvious benchmark is the minimum wage, and it is worth using even though it does not legally apply to you.

From April 2026 the GOV.UK rates are:

AgeHourly rate from April 2026
21 and over£12.71
18 to 20£10.85
Under 18£8
Apprentice£8

Be clear about what that table is and is not. Mystery shoppers are normally engaged as self-employed contractors, not workers or employees. If that is your status, the National Minimum Wage does not apply to the assignment and you cannot enforce it. Employment status is decided by the reality of the arrangement rather than by the label on the contract, so it is not impossible for someone to be a worker, but the ordinary mystery shopping arrangement is not.

Use £12.71 as a decision rule you set for yourself, not as a right. If an assignment does not clear it on the fee alone, you are choosing to work for less than the legal floor that would apply if somebody employed you to do the same thing.

4. The trap in the half you do not keep

This is the part that catches people, and it is the exact opposite of what intuition says.

HMRC's guidance on the trading allowance gives you a tax exemption of up to £1,000 a year on trading income, including from casual services. If your annual gross trading income is £1,000 or less you generally do not need to tell HMRC, though you must keep records.

The word doing the damage is gross. HMRC defines it in the same guidance: "Gross income means the total amount you would put on your tax return before any allowances or expenses are taken off."

So if a company pays you £6 of fee and £19 of reimbursement in one payment, the amount that counts towards your £1,000 is not necessarily the £6 you kept. Reimbursed expenses are ordinarily part of your gross receipts, and the expense is then deducted separately. Under the trading allowance route there is no separate deduction, because using the allowance means you cannot also deduct expenses. The practical effect is that a shopper who thinks they earned £300 this year may have a gross figure several times higher.

Two consequences worth acting on:

  • Track fee and reimbursement separately from day one. You cannot reconstruct this in April.
  • If gross goes over £1,000, you must register for Self Assessment by 5 October in the following tax year. Above £1,000 you can either claim the allowance or deduct your actual expenses, and where the reimbursed spend is large, deducting real expenses is usually the better of the two.

One more clause worth knowing: you cannot use the trading allowance at all against income from your own employer, or your spouse's or civil partner's employer. Mystery shopping the company you work for is therefore a bad idea for more reasons than one. Worked example: Ade in Walsall

Ade takes a fast food assignment advertised at £22. Split, it is a £7 fee and up to £15 reimbursement for a specified meal.

The time. Twenty minutes driving each way, twenty-five minutes in the restaurant, and forty minutes on the report with the timings and the photographs. That is 105 minutes, or 1.75 hours.

The money he keeps. £7. Petrol and parking come to about £3 and are not reimbursed, so £4 net.

The rate. £4 divided by 1.75 hours is about £2.29 an hour, against a £12.71 benchmark. It is not close.

The version that works. He does the same shop on a day he was going to that retail park anyway, so travel time and fuel drop out. Now it is £7 for 65 minutes, about £6.46 an hour, and he wanted lunch regardless so the meal has real value to him. Still under the benchmark, but a completely different decision.

The tax note. Across the year Ade does 60 similar shops. He thinks he has earned £420 in fees, comfortably under £1,000. His gross receipts including reimbursements are closer to £1,300, which is over the threshold, and that is the figure HMRC's test is written against.

5. When it is genuinely worth doing

This page is not an argument that mystery shopping is worthless. It is an argument that the advertised number is not the number. There are three situations where the sums genuinely work.

When the journey is free. Assignments on a route you already travel remove the largest hidden cost. Shoppers who do this well cluster several visits into one trip.

When you actually wanted the thing. A reimbursed meal you were going to buy anyway is real value. A reimbursed item you did not want is not.

When the fee is the whole payment. Some assignments, particularly telephone and online ones, have no purchase at all. Those are cleanly comparable to any other paid hour, and they are the ones worth looking for.

What does not work is treating the reimbursement as income, taking assignments that need a special journey, or accepting a single number without asking for the split. The honest verdict

Mystery shopping pays a fee and refunds a purchase, and only the first of those is money. Split the advertised figure, divide the fee by all the time including the report, take off any travel you were not reimbursed for, and compare what is left with £12.71, the April 2026 rate for workers aged 21 and over.

On assignments needing a dedicated journey, that sum usually fails, and it fails by a wide margin rather than a narrow one. On assignments that sit on a journey you were making anyway, for something you wanted anyway, it can be reasonable.

Where this genuinely does not help you: it is not a route to a meaningful income, and anybody presenting it as one is counting reimbursements as earnings. And do keep the two figures apart in your records, because HMRC's £1,000 trading allowance is measured on gross income, which includes money that passed through your hands and straight back out again.

Frequently asked

Is the reimbursement taxable income? The fee is your earnings. Reimbursed expenses are ordinarily part of your gross receipts with the expense deducted separately, which matters because HMRC's 1,000 pound trading allowance is measured on gross income, defined as the total before any allowances or expenses are taken off. Keep the two figures separate and take advice if you are near the threshold.

Does the minimum wage apply to mystery shopping? Usually not, because mystery shoppers are normally engaged as self-employed contractors rather than workers or employees. Employment status depends on the reality of the arrangement rather than the contract label, but the ordinary arrangement is not employment. Treat 12.71 pounds as your own decision rule, not an enforceable right.

What if I do mystery shopping alongside a full time job? The trading allowance is separate from your employment income, so you can have up to 1,000 pounds of gross trading income and generally not need to tell HMRC. You cannot use the allowance against income from your own employer, or from your spouse or civil partner's employer.

Are there assignments without a purchase? Yes. Telephone and online assignments and some in-person visits pay a fee with nothing to buy. Those are the cleanest to evaluate, because the advertised figure and your earnings are the same number.
