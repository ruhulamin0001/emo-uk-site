12.07%, rounded up: how holiday actually builds up on irregular hours

If your hours change week to week, or you only work part of the year, your holiday is not 28 days. It is 12.07% of the hours you actually work in each pay period. Here is where that number comes from and how to check your employer has it right.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Irregular-hours and part-year workers accrue 12.07% of the hours worked in each pay period, and employers must use this system for leave years starting after 31 March 2024 (GOV.UK, page updated 20 August 2026).
  • It rounds up to the nearest hour if the entitlement is half an hour or more. GOV.UK's own example: 30 hours worked gives 3.621 hours, which becomes 4.
  • The 12.07% is not arbitrary. It is 5.6 weeks of statutory leave divided by the 46.4 weeks you actually work, which is 12.069%.
  • You can take the leave from the next pay period. It does not sit unusable until the end of the year.
  • Three situations let you carry over your whole entitlement: you were not given a reasonable opportunity to take it, you were not told you would lose it, or you did not get rolled-up holiday pay you were entitled to.

1. Who this applies to, and who it does not

There are two different systems, and the first thing to work out is which one you are in.

Regular hours. If you work the same hours every week, you get 5.6 weeks of statutory leave, which is 28 days for someone on a five-day week. In your first year an employer can use an accrual system giving you one twelfth of the total each month. GOV.UK's example: after three months you would have 7 days, because 28 divided by 12 times 3 is 7.

Irregular hours or part-year. If your hours vary, or you only work part of the year, your employer must use the percentage system instead. GOV.UK's wording is that "employers need to use a specific accrual system" for these workers. It is not optional and it is not a choice between methods.

Two things determine your entitlement: how often you get paid, which is your pay period, and how many hours you worked in that pay period. Nothing else.

The change applies to leave years that began after 31 March 2024. If your leave year started on or before that date, the old approach continued until it renewed, so by now essentially everybody is on the new system.

Term-time-only staff are the classic part-year case: a school kitchen assistant or a term-time teaching assistant paid across twelve months but working thirty-nine weeks. Zero-hours contracts, bank shifts in the NHS, agency work and seasonal retail all sit in the irregular-hours case.

2. Where 12.07% actually comes from

The figure looks made up. It is not, and knowing the derivation makes it much easier to sanity check.

Statutory holiday is 5.6 weeks a year. A year has 52 weeks. If you take your 5.6 weeks off, the number of weeks you actually work is 52 minus 5.6, which is 46.4.

So the leave you earn per week worked is 5.6 divided by 46.4, which is 0.120689, or 12.07%.

That is the whole of it. The percentage is applied to hours worked rather than weeks, because irregular workers do not have a standard week, but the logic is identical.

Now the rounding rule, which trips up payroll departments constantly. GOV.UK: "It must be rounded up to the nearest hour if the entitlement is 0.5 of an hour or more." Their example is 30 hours worked in a weekly pay period, which gives 30 times 12.07 divided by 100, or 3.621 hours, which rounds up to 4 hours.

Note what the guidance does not say. It requires rounding up at half an hour or more. It says nothing about rounding down below that, so where the fraction is under half an hour the entitlement is not rounded up and payroll systems handle the remainder differently. If yours rounds down every time, it is quietly shaving your leave, and it is worth asking how the fraction is treated.

3. Jade in Barnsley: three months of a zero-hours contract

Jade works bank shifts for a care provider in Barnsley. She is on a zero-hours contract and is paid monthly, so her pay period is a calendar month.

One9611.587212 hours (fraction is over half, rounds up)
Two748.93189 hours (rounds up)
Three12014.48414.484 hours (fraction is under half, no rounding up required)

Across the quarter Jade has accrued about 35.5 hours of paid leave, which on her shift pattern is roughly three shifts. She can take month one's leave from month two onwards, because GOV.UK says the leave can be taken from the next pay period.

Two checks anyone can do with a payslip and a calculator:

  1. Multiply the hours on your payslip by 0.1207. If your leave balance moved by less than that, ask why.
  2. Check the pay period matches. A weekly-paid worker accrues weekly. A monthly-paid worker accrues monthly. Employers sometimes run the calculation annually, which delays your access to leave you have already earned.

A third check worth doing once a year: add up your total hours for the year and multiply by 0.1207. Compare that with the total leave you were credited. Small rounding differences are normal. A gap of several hours is not.

4. Rolled-up holiday pay and the payslip line to look for

For irregular-hours and part-year workers there is a second option available to employers: rolled-up holiday pay. Instead of paying you when you take leave, they add the holiday pay to every payslip as you go.

GOV.UK's calculate leave page confirms this exists and, importantly, treats it as something you are entitled to receive properly. It lists "they did not receive rolled-up holiday pay that they were entitled to" as one of the situations in which a worker can carry over their whole leave entitlement into the next year.

The practical consequence is that you should be able to see it. If your employer is using rolled-up holiday pay, it should appear on your payslip as an identifiable element rather than being folded silently into your hourly rate. If you look at your payslip and cannot find it anywhere, that is the question to ask, in writing.

Two things worth understanding about rolled-up pay:

  • It does not remove your right to the time off. You still accrue the leave and you are still entitled to take it. What changes is when you get paid for it.
  • It makes budgeting harder, not easier. Because the money arrives with your normal pay, taking a week off later means a week with no pay at all. Plenty of people on rolled-up arrangements never take their leave for exactly this reason, which is the outcome the rules are trying to prevent.

For workers who are paid when they actually take leave rather than through rolled-up pay, the rate of that holiday pay is a separate calculation based on average earnings, and GOV.UK's holiday pay page covers it.

5. Carrying leave over, and the three cases where you keep the lot

The general rule is in your contract. GOV.UK's default position: a worker with 28 days can carry over a maximum of 8 days. If you get more than 28 days, whether the extra can be carried is up to your employer, so check the contract or the handbook.

Then there are the protected cases.

Sickness. If you could not take leave because you were off sick, you can carry over up to 20 of your 28 days if you work regular hours all year, and up to 28 days if you work irregular hours or only part of the year. That second figure is more generous and applies to exactly the workers this article is about.

Family-related leave. If you could not take your leave because you were on maternity, paternity, adoption or parental leave, you can carry the untaken leave into the next year. Your entitlement also keeps building while you are on that leave, which is worth knowing.

The three big ones. GOV.UK says a worker can carry over their whole leave entitlement if any of these apply:

  • they did not receive rolled-up holiday pay they were entitled to
  • they were not given a reasonable opportunity to take leave
  • they were not told they would lose their leave if they did not take it by the end of the leave year

That last one deserves emphasis. "Use it or lose it" is only enforceable if your employer actually told you, and told you in a way you can point to. Plenty of employers simply never mention it. If yours did not, your leave did not vanish on 31 December.

The verdict

If your hours vary, holiday is 12.07% of the hours you work in each pay period, rounded up when the fraction is half an hour or more, and available to take from the next pay period. Multiply the hours on your payslip by 0.1207 once and compare it with what you were credited: that single check catches most errors. If your employer uses rolled-up holiday pay, find it on the payslip, and if you cannot, ask in writing. And before you accept that untaken leave expired at the end of the year, check whether anyone ever actually told you it would.

Frequently asked

Does 12.07% apply if I am on a zero-hours contract but work the same shifts every week? The test is whether your hours are wholly or mostly variable under your contract, not what happened to occur in practice. If your contract does not guarantee hours, you are normally an irregular-hours worker and the percentage system applies. If in doubt, ask your employer which system they are using you on and get the answer in writing.

Can my employer pay me instead of letting me take the time off? Not for your statutory leave while you are still employed. Payment in lieu of untaken statutory holiday is only permitted when the job ends. Rolled-up holiday pay is a different thing: it pays you as you go but you still have the right to take the time.

I am term-time only. Am I a part-year worker? Usually yes, if there are periods in the year where you do no work and are not paid. Someone paid in twelve equal instalments across the year but only working during term time is the standard part-year case, and the percentage system applies to them.

What if my employer refuses to correct my leave balance? Put the calculation in writing, showing the hours and the 12.07% sum, and ask for a written response. Acas gives free advice on the helpline and can help before anything formal. An unlawful deduction from wages claim to an employment tribunal has a strict time limit, so do not let it drift.
