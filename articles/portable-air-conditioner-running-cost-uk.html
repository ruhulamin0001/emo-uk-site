A Portable Air Conditioner Costs 18 Times What A Fan Costs

Which sounds damning until you multiply it by the number of nights Britain is actually too hot to sleep. The running cost is not the reason to hesitate over one of these. The reason is a hose, and a piece of buying advice that runs directly against every energy label you have ever been told to read.

The short version

1. Why the box does not tell you the wattage, and how to work it out

Portable air conditioners are sold on BTU, which measures how much heat the machine can move, not how much electricity it uses to move it. Those are different numbers and the second one is the one on your bill.

Manufacturers rarely print the electrical input on the front of the box, but they nearly always publish two figures that let you calculate it: the cooling capacity and the EER, or energy efficiency ratio. EER is simply cooling out divided by electricity in.

Take De'Longhi's Pinguino PACEM82K.1, whose specification sheet on De'Longhi's own UK site states, as checked on 11 September 2026:

  • Cooling capacity 9,400 BTU/h, which is 2.4 kW, for rooms up to 80 cubic metres
  • Energy efficiency label A
  • EER 2.6
  • Air flow 340, 370 and 400 cubic metres an hour on three speeds
  • Noise 47 to 50 dB
  • Refrigerant R290

So the electrical input is 2.4 kW divided by 2.6, which is 0.923 kW, or 923 watts. De'Longhi listed this model at £599.99 on its own site on the day we checked, though it was out of stock, and retail prices move.

That 923 watts is the number everything below is built on, and you can do the same sum for any unit you are considering in about ten seconds. If a listing gives you BTU but no EER, that is a listing to walk away from.

2. Cost per hour, per night, per hot week, per summer

At the Ofgem price cap rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, electricity is 26.32p per kWh, with no VAT on electricity across that period.

For the summer comparison we use a realistic British pattern: twenty nights a year where it is hot enough that you genuinely want cooling, at eight hours each, which is 160 hours. Anyone who used one in 2022 or 2025 may want to double that; anyone in a north facing flat in Scotland can halve it.

The last row is the one that should settle the argument for most people. Across a whole British summer, the portable air conditioner costs £36.76 more to run than a fan. Three pounds a month. If you are choosing between them, the electricity is not the deciding factor and anyone telling you these machines will wreck your bill is thinking of a Mediterranean climate.

The purchase price is a different matter. £500 or £600 against £30 for a fan is a real gap, and at twenty nights of use a year that is £25 to £30 per night of use across a ten year life, before a single unit of electricity.

The split system row is context. At an SEER of around 7 it costs a third of the portable per hour, because it puts the hot half of the machine outside where it belongs. It also needs an F-Gas qualified installer and a hole in your wall, which is why most people buy the portable.

3. Why the efficient model is the wrong buy in this country

This is the section that matters, and it goes against the advice you get on every other appliance on this site.

De'Longhi's range includes the PAC EX100 Silent, rated A++ and drawing about 700 watts against the A-rated model's 923. That is a genuine 24 per cent improvement and it is exactly the kind of thing we normally tell people to buy.

Here is the payback sum.

Saving per hour: 223 watts × 26.32p per kWh = 5.87p an hour.
Saving per summer: 0.223 kW × 160 hours × 26.32p = £9.39 a year.
Typical price premium for the efficient model: around £200.
Payback: £200 divided by £9.39, which is 21.3 years.

A portable air conditioner is a compressor, a fan and a plastic case. It is not going to see 21 British summers. So on running cost alone, the A++ premium does not pay back and you should not buy it for that reason.

Why does this differ from a fridge or a washing machine? Hours. A fridge runs 8,760 hours a year and a small efficiency gain is multiplied by all of them. This runs 160. Efficiency ratings are most valuable on the appliances you never switch off, and least valuable on the ones you use for three weeks. The same trap catches people buying an expensive A-rated television, which we looked at separately.

There are still reasons to buy the premium model. It is quieter, which in a bedroom is the whole point, and quieter units tend to have better window kits and dual hoses. Buy it for the noise rating in decibels. Do not buy it for the letter on the label.

4. The hose problem, and what actually fixes it

Every single hose portable air conditioner works the same way. It draws air from the room, passes it over a hot condenser, and blows that now very warm air out of the window down the hose.

That air has to be replaced. The machine is removing several hundred cubic metres an hour, so several hundred cubic metres of outside air are pulled in to take its place, through the letterbox, under doors, around the loft hatch and every other gap. On the hottest day of the year, that replacement air is hot. The machine still cools the room, because it removes more heat than the incoming air brings in, but it is working against itself, and the effect is largest exactly when you need it least.

What helps, in order of how much:

  • Seal the window kit properly. The concertina panel supplied with most units is a token effort and leaves gaps you can see daylight through. Foam board cut to the opening, or a proper zip-in fabric seal, is a £20 to £40 fix that makes a bigger difference than anything else on this list.
  • Shut the internal door of the room you are cooling. This reduces the volume the machine is fighting and limits how far it can pull replacement air from.
  • Buy a dual hose unit if you can. These draw condenser air from outside and return it outside, so the room is never depressurised. They are less common and cost more, and the benefit is real.
  • Keep the hose short and straight. A long coiled hose radiates the heat it is carrying straight back into the room it just left. Manufacturers supply them long because rooms vary, not because long is better.
  • Close the curtains before it gets hot, not after. Solar gain through a south facing window on a clear July day is far more heat than a 2.4 kW machine can remove. Blocking it costs nothing.

That last point is worth taking seriously before spending £600. External shading, light coloured blinds and closing up the house during the day are free, and in a typical British heatwave lasting three or four days they do most of the work. The same fabric that keeps heat in during winter, covered in our page on thermal curtains against blinds, keeps it out in summer.

5. Worked example: Dev in Leicester

Dev's top floor flat hits 28 degrees in the bedroom on hot nights and he has stopped sleeping properly. He is looking at a 9,400 BTU portable unit at around £550 and a premium quiet model at £760.

He counts the nights. Checking back through the previous three summers, he identifies roughly 18 nights a year where he would definitely have used it, and maybe another 10 where he might have. He budgets for 25 nights, 8 hours each, so 200 hours.

Running cost on the standard unit: 0.923 kW × 200 hours × 26.32p = £48.59 a year.
Running cost on the premium unit: 0.700 kW × 200 × 26.32p = £36.85 a year.
Saving: £11.74 a year. On a £210 premium, that is a payback of 17.9 years.

So he buys the standard unit, and spends £35 of the difference on a proper zip-in window seal instead of the supplied concertina panel. He also moves the machine so the hose runs 900mm straight to the window rather than looping across the floor.

Over ten years his total cost of ownership is £550 for the machine plus £486 of electricity: £1,036, or £4.14 for every night he uses it. He decides that is a fair price for sleeping in July, and he is deciding it with the real number in front of him rather than a vague fear about the bill.

What surprised him was the dehumidifying side. The same unit removes up to 43 litres a day of moisture, which in an autumn flat with drying laundry is a useful second job. If damp rather than heat is your real problem, read PIV units against dehumidifiers instead.

6. The honest verdict

The running cost should not stop you. £38.87 for a British summer, and £36.76 more than a fan across the same season. That is three pounds a month and it is not a reason to sweat through August.

The purchase price should make you think. At around twenty nights of use a year, a £550 machine costs you £25 to £30 a night of use over its life. Try a £30 fan, blackout blinds and closing the house up during the day for one summer first. For a lot of people that is enough, and it is 5 per cent of the cost.

If you do buy, buy on noise, not on the energy label. The A++ premium takes 21 years to pay back in this climate and the machine will not last that long. Decibels are what you will notice at two in the morning.

And spend £30 on a proper window seal. It is the highest value thirty pounds in this entire article.

If you are buying

Two numbers decide it: the EER, which lets you calculate the wattage yourself, and the noise rating in dB, which is what you will actually live with. A dual hose design is worth paying for if you can find one. The supplied window kit is almost always the weakest part of the package, so budget separately for a better seal.

Frequently asked

Is R290 refrigerant dangerous? R290 is propane, and it is used because it has a very low global warming potential compared with the older refrigerants it replaced. The charge in a domestic portable unit is small and the machines are designed and certified around it, but it is why these units carry ventilation and storage instructions and why you should not store one in a sealed cupboard or attempt to repair the sealed circuit yourself.

Do evaporative coolers work instead? They cool air by evaporating water, which raises humidity. In a dry climate that is a good trade. In a British summer, where a hot day is often a humid one, adding moisture to a bedroom makes it feel worse, with a damp problem as a bonus.

Can I vent the hose into another room or a bucket? No. The hose carries the heat the machine removed plus the heat the compressor generated, so venting indoors makes the house hotter overall. If you cannot open a window at all, a portable unit is the wrong product for that room.

What size do I need? De'Longhi's own guidance is around 9,000 BTU, or 2 to 2.5 kW, for rooms up to 85 cubic metres, 9,000 to 12,000 BTU up to 100, and up to 13,000 BTU above 120. Oversizing is not free: a larger unit draws more and short cycles, which leaves the room clammy because it stops before it has dehumidified.
