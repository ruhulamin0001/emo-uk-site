Four Chrome Extensions a UK Freelance Writer Might Actually Pay For, Checked on the Web Store on 5 September 2026: Users, Last Update, What Data They Read, and the Price

Lists of productivity extensions are usually copied from other lists, which is how people end up installing something last updated in 2023 that reads every page they open. This one was built by opening each listing on the Chrome Web Store on the day of writing and copying down the user count, the version, the last update date and the data the developer declares it handles. Four extensions made the cut. The most useful section may be the one on how to check the next one yourself.

1. How each listing was checked

Every Chrome Web Store listing carries the same block of facts under the heading Details: the version number, the date it was last updated, the size, and the developer's name and address. Above that, next to the rating, it shows the user count. Below it, under Privacy, the developer must declare which categories of data the extension handles, from a fixed list that includes personally identifiable information, personal communications, location, web history, user activity and website content. We opened each of the four listings on 5 September 2026 and copied those fields. Nothing in this guide about the extensions themselves comes from anywhere else.

The user count matters less than people think, because a big number tells you an extension is popular, not that it is safe. The last update date matters more. An extension that has not been updated in a year or two is either finished, which is rare, or abandoned, and an abandoned extension with broad permissions is a standing risk because whoever controls the developer account can push a new version to every installed copy. Section 6 explains how to use that date.

Grammarly is the default grammar and tone checker and its listing says it works across Gmail, Google Docs, LinkedIn and more than 500,000 other sites. The free tier corrects grammar, spelling and punctuation and gives tone suggestions. Pro adds sentence rewrites, tone and formality controls, plagiarism and AI content detection and a larger allowance of generative prompts. The listing also mentions an opt-in beta assistant called Go that works alongside you in Chrome, which you can leave switched off.

LanguageTool does a similar job with a different emphasis: it supports more than 25 languages, distinguishes British from American English, and its listing says no account is needed and that text is checked by sending it to languagetool.org over an encrypted connection. For a writer who works in British English and occasionally another language it is the stronger free tier of the two.

Claude in Chrome is not a grammar checker. Anthropic's listing describes it as an extension that reads the page you are on, then clicks, types, navigates and fills forms while you watch, with a side panel that is now the same Cowork product as the desktop app. For a writer that means research across a dozen tabs, pulling quotes and figures into a document, filling a client's content management system, and scheduled browser tasks. It needs a paid Claude plan, and the listing says the Cowork side panel is on Max and Team with Pro rolling out. Its 2.8 rating from 1,600 reviews is the lowest here and worth reading before you install; the Claude plans guide explains what the paid tiers include.

Toggl Track puts a timer into web tools such as Notion, Trello and Google Docs so you can start tracking without opening a new tab, and adds a Pomodoro timer, idle detection and reminders. For a freelancer it is the tool that tells you what an article actually took, which is the number every rate decision depends on, as the freelance rates guide argues at length. Its listing declares only user activity, and it is open source.

4. What the data declarations mean for client work

Three of the four declare that they handle personal communications and website content. In plain terms, a grammar checker has to read what you type to check it, and a browser agent has to read the page to act on it. That is not sinister, but it has a consequence for freelance work: if a client's contract says their material must not be shared with third parties, then running it through an extension that sends text to a server for checking may breach that clause. Some clients care, most have never thought about it, and the safe course is to ask before you paste a draft under NDA into anything.

Claude in Chrome goes further and declares web history, because it can open and navigate tabs. Anthropic's own help centre lists fifteen Chrome permissions the extension asks for, including debugger, which is what lets it click and type, and tabs. Its listing also warns that websites can hide instructions that try to redirect the agent, and recommends the ask-before-acting mode on sites you do not know. For a writer that means keeping it away from your banking tab and client portals until you trust it, which is the same advice you would give anyone about a new assistant.

Toggl Track declares only user activity, which is the timer data you deliberately create. If you want a productivity extension with the narrowest possible view of your work, that is the one.

5. A worked example in Carlisle

Hannah in Carlisle writes product copy and case studies for three B2B clients, about 20 billable hours a week at GBP 30 an hour. She has all four extensions on free tiers for a month and keeps Toggl running on every job. Her log shows LanguageTool catching things she would have caught on a second read anyway, saving perhaps ten minutes a week. Claude Free saves her more, mainly on first drafts of case study structures and on summarising client call notes, but she hits its limit twice on busy days.

She upgrades one thing: Claude Pro at GBP 17.71, which also unlocks the Chrome extension and Cowork for the client portal work. Break-even at her rate is 35 billed minutes a month. Her Toggl data for the following month shows the drafting stage of a 1,200-word case study fell from about 2 hours 10 minutes to about 1 hour 25 minutes on average across four pieces, which is roughly three hours saved in the month, or GBP 90 at her rate against GBP 17.71 spent. She keeps Grammarly on free, and uninstalls nothing, because every listing showed an update within the last month.

Her subscription is an allowable expense against self-employed profit. The GBP 1,000 trading allowance is irrelevant at her income, and she is already registered for Self Assessment; the tax rules guide covers the thresholds for anyone starting out.

6. How to spot an abandoned extension

Open the listing and scroll to Details. If the Updated date is more than twelve months ago, treat it as abandoned unless you have a specific reason to trust it. All four here were updated within the last four weeks of writing, which is what you want to see. Then read the Privacy section and ask whether the categories declared make sense for the job: a timer does not need your web history, and a grammar checker does not need your location, though both Grammarly and LanguageTool declare it and neither explains why on the listing.

Finally, look at the developer block. Grammarly, LanguageTool and Toggl identify themselves as traders under EU rules, with a business address and a D-U-N-S number. Anthropic's listing is marked non-trader, which the store notes means EU consumer rights do not apply to that contract, a point worth knowing even though the company itself is well known. A listing with no developer address, no website and no recent update is one to leave alone regardless of its user count.

The honest verdict

Install LanguageTool, Claude and Toggl Track on their free tiers, run Toggl on every job for a month, and let the timer tell you which one deserves money. For most UK writers the first and often only upgrade worth GBP 17.71 is Claude Pro, because it turns the extension into something that does research and portal work rather than just checking commas. Skip paying for two grammar checkers, keep NDA material out of anything that sends text to a server unless the client has agreed, and re-check the Updated date on every extension you keep at least twice a year.

Frequently asked

Is Grammarly free? The free tier is, and the Web Store listing and grammarly.com/plans both say so. Pro was shown at GBP 10 a month on the plans page on 5 September 2026, with a seven-day trial.

Does Claude in Chrome work on the free Claude plan? No. The listing says it is available on all paid plans, and the Cowork side panel within it is on Max and Team with Pro rolling out. Pro is USD 20 a month before tax, about GBP 17.71 with VAT.

Why is the Claude extension rated 2.8? We do not know; the store shows the rating and 1,600 reviews, and we report the number as it appeared on 5 September 2026. Read the recent reviews before installing, as you would with any tool that can act in your browser.

Are these extensions safe with client work under NDA? Three of the four declare that they handle personal communications and website content, which means text you write or read passes through their servers. Ask the client before using them on confidential material.
