Working age benefits went up 3.8%. Pensioners got 4.8%. Here is why

Three different uprating figures apply for the year ending March 2027, and most coverage quotes only one of them. Which applies to you depends entirely on which benefit you claim.

Ruhul Amin Published 19 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Working age benefits rose by CPI at 3.8% for the financial year ending March 2027, including the main Universal Credit elements and Housing Benefit personal allowances.
  • The basic and new State Pension rose by 4.8%, in line with average weekly earnings under the triple lock.
  • The Universal Credit standard allowance got more than 3.8%. The Universal Credit Act 2025 requires September CPI plus a further 2.3%.
  • Disability premiums also rose 3.8%, as did premiums for ESA claimants in the Support Group and the Savings Credit maximum.
  • The single UC standard allowance for someone 25 or over is £400.14 a month for 2026/27.

1. Three different rates, one tax year

For the financial year ending March 2027, three separate uprating figures are in play. The DWP's own Housing Benefit circular A14/2025 sets them out.

  • 3.8% for working age benefits, being CPI.
  • 4.8% for the basic State Pension and the full rate of the new State Retirement Pension, in line with average weekly earnings under the triple lock.
  • More than 3.8% for the Universal Credit standard allowance specifically, because of a separate legal requirement explained below.

When a headline says "benefits go up by X", it has picked one of these three and dropped the others. That is why the number in the news so often fails to match the number that arrives.

2. Who got which figure

The 3.8% CPI uprating applies to working age benefits, specifically the main rates and certain premiums and additions. Per the circular, that includes:

  • the main elements of Universal Credit
  • Housing Benefit personal allowances
  • premiums paid to disabled people receiving working age benefits
  • premiums for Employment and Support Allowance claimants in the Support Group
  • the Savings Credit maximum

The 4.8% figure is a pensioner figure. It comes from the triple lock, which takes the highest of average weekly earnings growth, CPI, and 2.5%. This year earnings won, at 4.8%, which is why pensioners received a full percentage point more than working age claimants on the same set of prices.

That gap is not an accident or an error. It is what the triple lock is designed to do, and it is worth understanding rather than being surprised by it every spring.

3. Why Universal Credit is different

The Universal Credit standard allowance does not follow plain CPI.

The Universal Credit Act 2025 requires the Secretary of State to increase the standard allowance by at least the annual CPI increase to September, and then by a further 2.3%. The four rates of standard allowance are legislated to rise above inflation in each year from 2026/27 through to 2029/30.

So a Universal Credit claimant reading "benefits up 3.8%" and applying that to their whole award will get the wrong answer twice over. The standard allowance part went up by more than 3.8%. The other elements of their award, such as housing and child elements, generally went up by 3.8%.

The result is that two claimants with the same total award can see different percentage increases, purely because their awards are built from different elements.

It is also worth naming what a percentage uprating does not reach. Some figures in the benefits system are fixed cash amounts that are not uprated annually at all, and they quietly lose value every year the prices move. The £16,000 capital limit that ends entitlement to means tested benefits is the clearest example: it is a cash figure, and a saver whose modest savings drift upwards can lose an award without their circumstances improving in any real sense. An uprating headline tells you nothing about those.

4. A worked example: what £400.14 a month actually is

Take the published 2026/27 rate for the Universal Credit standard allowance for a single claimant aged 25 or over: £400.14 a month.

Over a year that is £400.14 multiplied by 12, which is £4,801.68.

Expressed weekly, and this is where people often get confused, it is not simply divided by four. Universal Credit is paid monthly and assessed monthly. £4,801.68 divided by 52 is about £92.34 a week, but no week ever contains that payment, because the payment arrives once a month.

That distinction matters when comparing Universal Credit to legacy benefits quoted weekly, or when budgeting against weekly outgoings like rent paid to a private landlord.

We are deliberately not publishing a headline "you are £X better off" figure here. The increase any individual saw depends on the mix of elements in their award, their earnings in the assessment period, and any deductions being taken. Publishing a single number would be tidier and would also be wrong for most readers.

One further point on timing, because it causes real confusion every April. The new rates apply from a date in April, but Universal Credit is assessed over monthly periods that rarely line up with the calendar. If your assessment period straddles the uprating date, the first payment after April can be calculated partly on the old rates. That is not an error, and it corrects itself in the following period.

5. How to check what you are actually getting

The reliable route is your own statement, not a news article.

For Universal Credit, your monthly statement in your online account itemises every element of your award and every deduction. That is the only document that tells you your figure. If the total moved by less than you expected, the usual explanations are deductions for advances or debts, or a change in earnings in the assessment period, rather than the uprating being wrong.

The full set of rates for the year ending March 2027 is published on GOV.UK. If you think your award is wrong, the free routes are Citizens Advice and, for Universal Credit specifically, the Help to Claim service. You do not need to pay anybody to check a benefit calculation.

One honest caveat. For a household whose rent has risen faster than 3.8%, an uprating in line with CPI does not restore the position, and no amount of explaining the mechanism changes that. Knowing which figure applies to you at least tells you whether to query the payment or to look elsewhere for help.

Frequently asked

Why did pensioners get 4.8% and I got 3.8%? The State Pension uses the triple lock, which takes the highest of average weekly earnings growth, CPI, and 2.5%. Earnings growth was the highest at 4.8%. Working age benefits are uprated by CPI, which was 3.8%.

Did the Universal Credit standard allowance really go up by more? Yes. The Universal Credit Act 2025 requires the standard allowance to rise by at least September CPI plus a further 2.3%, and to rise above inflation each year from 2026/27 to 2029/30.

What is the Universal Credit standard allowance for a single person over 25? £400.14 a month for 2026/27, per the published GOV.UK benefit and pension rates.

When do the next rates apply? From April 2027, driven by the September 2026 CPI figure that the ONS publishes on 21 October 2026.
