One of These Costs Nothing and the Other Costs £680. That Is Not the Part That Decides It

A Debt Relief Order is free. GOV.UK states it in five words on the page itself. Bankruptcy is £680, payable before your application is even looked at. And yet the price is close to irrelevant, because for most people only one of the two doors is open at all.

1. The DRO fee is gone, and what that changed

The GOV.UK page on Debt Relief Orders carries one sentence that most write-ups on this subject have not caught up with: "You do not need to pay for a DRO."

For years there was a fee, and a great deal of published advice still quotes it. If you are reading a comparison that says a DRO costs £90 and bankruptcy costs £680, you are reading something written against the old rules. The DRO is free.

Bankruptcy is £680. That is the figure on the GOV.UK application page, for England and Wales, and the application is online only. Scotland and Northern Ireland run different systems entirely, through the Accountant in Bankruptcy and through nidirect respectively.

Two details about that £680 matter more than the number. You cannot submit the application until the full fee is paid, so it is not a cost you settle afterwards out of relief. And you can pay it in instalments, which is the government acknowledging the obvious problem with charging £680 to someone who has no money. It usually takes up to 28 days to get a decision once you submit, longer if the adjudicator comes back with questions.

So on price alone the DRO wins by £680. If price were the deciding factor this article would be one paragraph long. It is not, and the reason is the next section.

2. The six tests, and why they decide it rather than the price

A DRO is not the cheap version of bankruptcy. It is a different product, built for one specific situation: no money, no assets, no property, and a debt that is large by the standards of your life but small by the standards of the insolvency system. You have to pass every one of these:

You owe less than £50,000.

You have less than £75 a month spare income. That is spare after rent, bills and essentials.

You have less than £2,000 worth of assets.

You do not own a vehicle worth £4,000 or more.

You have lived or worked in England and Wales within the last 3 years.

You have not applied for a DRO within the last 6 years.

GOV.UK also lists, separately and in slightly different words, that a DRO is one way to deal with debts if you owe less than £50,000, do not have much spare income, and do not own your home.

Read down that list against your own situation and you will usually find the answer decides itself. Own a house with any equity in it, and you are out. Drive a car worth £4,500, and you are out unless you sell it, which for a lot of people means losing the job that the car gets them to. Owe £62,000, and you are out. Have £120 a month spare, and you are out.

That is why the price comparison is a trap. Most people who read a DRO versus bankruptcy article are not choosing between two options. They are finding out which one they qualify for. Bankruptcy has no upper debt limit, no asset ceiling and no spare income test in the same shape, which is exactly why it costs money and why it reaches people the DRO cannot.

There is one more structural difference and it is easy to miss. You cannot apply for a DRO on your own. GOV.UK says so directly: you must contact an approved debt adviser to apply. Bankruptcy you do yourself, through the online service. So the DRO route starts with an appointment you have to get, and the bankruptcy route starts with a payment you have to make.

3. What a DRO actually stops you doing

For 12 months you stop making payments towards the debts in the DRO, and interest stops too. In exchange you accept restrictions. They are worth reading before you agree to them rather than after.

While the DRO is running you cannot:

  • borrow more than £500 without telling the lender about your DRO
  • act as the director of a company
  • create, manage or promote a company without the court's permission
  • manage a business without telling the people you do business with
  • open a bank account without telling the bank or building society

GOV.UK is blunt about the consequence of ignoring these: you may be prosecuted if you break any of them.

The company director restriction is the one that catches people out. If you run any kind of limited company, even a dormant one, a DRO ends that for its duration. For a self-employed person trading as a sole trader the restriction is softer but still real: you have to disclose the DRO to the people you trade with.

Some debts are not included and you keep paying them. GOV.UK names rent and bills, and "certain debts not included in the DRO, such as student loans or court fines". A DRO is not a clean slate. It is a line drawn round a particular set of debts.

A DRO can also be cancelled if your financial circumstances change, and extended if you do not follow the restrictions. It is a supervised 12 months, not a switch you flick.

4. Worked example: Yusuf in Walsall runs both numbers

Yusuf is 38, rents, and owes £19,400 across two credit cards, an overdraft and a catalogue account. After rent, council tax, energy and food he has about £40 a month left. He has no savings. He owns a nine year old car he uses for work.

Test one, the debt. £19,400 is under £50,000. Pass.

Test two, spare income. £40 is under £75. Pass.

Test three, assets. Under £2,000, once the car is counted separately. Pass.

Test four, the car. This is the one that decides his case. A nine year old car has to be worth under £4,000. If a dealer would give him £3,200 for it, he passes and keeps the car. If it is worth £4,300, he fails, and his choice becomes sell the car and lose the way he gets to work, or go the bankruptcy route.

Test five and six. Lived in England for 20 years, never had a DRO. Pass.

If he passes: the DRO costs him nothing, runs 12 months, and at the end the £19,400 is gone. At £40 a month, paying off £19,400 without any interest at all would take over 40 years. That arithmetic is the whole case for a DRO and it is why the eligibility limits exist.

If he fails on the car: bankruptcy costs £680, which at £40 a month spare is 17 months of his entire disposable income. That is what the instalment option is for, and it is also why this decision needs a free adviser rather than a website.

Either way the credit file outcome is the same: 6 years. That is worth knowing before anyone tells you one route is gentler on your record than the other.

5. What neither of them does

Neither is a secret. A DRO goes on the Individual Insolvency Register and is removed 3 months after the DRO ends. Bankruptcy has its own public record. If you are hoping either is private, they are not.

Neither clears every debt. Student loans and court fines are named on the GOV.UK DRO page as debts that carry on. Rent and bills carry on too, because they are for things you are still using.

Neither is the only option. The same GOV.UK guide covers Breathing Space, Debt Management Plans, administration orders and Individual Voluntary Arrangements. Breathing Space in particular is worth knowing about, because it buys time without any of the six month or six year consequences attached to the two options here.

Neither applies in Scotland or Northern Ireland in this form. The DRO eligibility test itself requires having lived or worked in England and Wales, and the bankruptcy service is England and Wales only.

And neither should be chosen off an article. The DRO route legally requires an approved debt adviser anyway, and that advice is free. StepChange, Citizens Advice and National Debtline all do it without charging. Anyone who wants a fee to arrange a free order is selling you something.

The honest verdict

If you meet all six DRO conditions, the DRO is almost certainly the right route, and the fact that it now costs nothing removes the last argument against it. Twelve months, no payments, no interest, and the debt is written off at the end. Against £680 and a broader process, that is not a close contest.

But the six conditions are strict and they are not negotiable, and the honest thing to say is that a lot of people reading this will fail at least one. Owning a home fails it outright. A car worth £4,000 or more fails it. Debt over £50,000 fails it. If any of those is you, bankruptcy is not the expensive alternative you settled for. It is the route that was built for your situation, and the £680 can be paid in instalments.

What should not drive the decision is the price gap, and that is the trap the headline sets. Free is only better if free is available. The useful first step is not choosing between them. It is a free appointment with an approved debt adviser, who is the only person who can start a DRO anyway, and who will tell you within an hour which of these doors is actually open.

Frequently asked

Is the DRO really free now? Yes. The GOV.UK page states "You do not need to pay for a DRO". If you see a fee quoted, check the date on what you are reading.

Can I apply for a DRO myself? No. GOV.UK says you cannot apply for a DRO on your own and you must contact an approved debt adviser. That advice is free from the main debt charities.

How long does each stay on my credit record? A DRO stays on your credit record for 6 years, and GOV.UK notes this is the same as other debt relief options. It comes off the Individual Insolvency Register 3 months after the DRO ends, which is a different register from your credit file.

What happens to my car? For a DRO the test is whether it is worth £4,000 or more. Below that you can keep it. Bankruptcy handles vehicles differently and depends on value and whether it is needed for work, which is a question for the adjudicator and for your adviser rather than for a general guide.

Does a DRO stop interest? Yes. GOV.UK says you stop making payments towards your debts, including interest, for 12 months, and after 12 months you do not need to pay the debts or follow the restrictions.
