The 28 Days You Have to Serve Before Your First Order, and the Registration Nobody Budgets For

If you sell, cook, store, handle, prepare or distribute food you are a food business, and that is true from your own kitchen and true if every sale happens through a social media message. Registration is required at least 28 days before you start, which is the part that catches people who plan to start next week.

The short version

  • Register at least 28 days before trading in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The FSA recommends waiting until 28 days out rather than registering too early.

1. Who counts as a food business

The Food Standards Agency's guidance on GOV.UK, last updated 25 June 2026 and read on 13 September 2026, sets the test out as a list of verbs. You need to register with your local authority if you sell food, cook food, store or handle food, prepare food, or distribute food.

Then it closes the obvious escape routes. Registration applies to all types of business that sell food and drink, regardless of where they operate from. It includes businesses trading from home, from a mobile unit, or from temporary premises such as a stall or a van, and it applies if you only sell food or drink online.

The FSA also defines what it means by distance selling: any selling that happens without face to face contact with the consumer. A cake ordered through an Instagram message and collected from your doorstep is distance selling. A stall at a school fair is temporary premises. Both are in scope.

It goes further than most people expect at the far end too. Companies involved with food distribution, brokerage or food supply that operate from an office should also register as a food business, and the FSA says this applies even if no food is kept at the premises.

The GOV.UK page names no registration fee anywhere, and the route it gives is the FSA's own register service. If someone is offering to register you for a fee, that is their fee, not a government one.

2. The 28 day rule, and how to time it

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland you are required to register at least 28 days before trading. The FSA adds advice that is easy to skim past and worth following: do not register too early, wait until 28 days before you are ready to start.

The reason is practical. Registration starts a relationship with your local authority's food safety team, and an inspection can follow. Registering six months before you are ready invites that inspection into a kitchen that is not yet set up.

So the 28 days is not dead time. It is the window in which you are supposed to get ready. The FSA publishes a getting ready to start your food business checklist, and it is the right thing to work through during those 28 days rather than after them.

Scotland is separate. Information and advice for new food businesses there comes from Food Standards Scotland, and you contact your Scottish local authority directly.

3. Worked example: Aoife in Reading

Aoife wants to sell brownies through Instagram, with collection from her house and occasional local delivery. She plans to start selling in two weeks, for a Christmas run.

Is she in scope? Every part of it. She sells food, she cooks food, she prepares food, she operates from home, and the sales happen without face to face contact, which is the FSA's own definition of distance selling.

What her plan gets wrong: two weeks is not enough. Registration is required at least 28 days before trading, so a first sale two weeks out is a first sale before she is allowed to trade. The clock, not the baking, is the binding constraint.

What the timeline should be: register today with Reading Borough Council, spend the 28 days working the FSA checklist and getting the kitchen right, and take the first order on day 29. Same Christmas run, two weeks later, done properly.

The second registration she has not thought about: if she later takes a monthly stall in a neighbouring borough, that is a second location, and each site registers with the local authority in which it sits. One business can mean more than one registration, and the second one has its own 28 days.

What it costs her in cash: nothing. The GOV.UK page names no fee. What it costs her is a month of lead time and a kitchen that has to stand up to an inspection, and those are the real prices.

4. Childminders, charities and the special cases

Two groups have their own route, and both are worth knowing because the assumption in each case runs the wrong way.

Childminders in England do not register separately. The details you provide to Ofsted, or to your childminder agency, are also used to register you as a food business with your local authority. You still have to comply with food safety and hygiene regulations, and the FSA spells out what that covers: meals, snacks, drinks apart from mains tap water, reheated food provided by a parent or carer, and food that you cut up and prepare. Wales and Northern Ireland differ, and childminders in Scotland complete a specific childminders food business registration form and send it to their local authority.

Charities and food banks may need to register even though the food is free. The FSA says you may need to register as a food business if you, or your operation, provide food to the community, even if it is free, 28 days before you start providing food. There is separate FSA guidance for food banks and charities, and a link to it was added to the page on 25 June 2026.

If you are genuinely unsure whether you are in scope, the FSA's answer is to contact your local food safety team rather than guess, and it publishes a finder for that.

The verdict

Registration itself is not the hard part and the GOV.UK page attaches no fee to it. The hard part is everything the registration leads to: hygiene standards, a food hygiene rating that will be published, and an inspection of a domestic kitchen that was not designed to be inspected. Anyone selling this as a weekend side hustle with no overhead has skipped that.

So the honest advice is to treat the 28 days as the planning window rather than a delay. Work the FSA checklist, sort the kitchen, then register, then start. Doing it in that order is what turns the inspection into a formality instead of a problem.

Where this does not help you: it covers registration only. Allergen labelling, the rules on prepacked food for direct sale, and insurance are separate subjects with their own requirements, and none of them is optional either.

Frequently asked

Do I need to register if I only sell through Instagram? Yes. The FSA includes businesses trading online, for example via social media or a website, and describes distance selling as any selling that happens without face to face contact with the consumer.

Is there a fee to register? The GOV.UK food business registration page names no fee and links to the FSA register service. Any charge you are asked for by a third party is theirs, not the government's.

What if I trade in two different council areas? You register each site with the local authority in which it is located. One business can mean more than one registration.

Does a charity bake sale need registering? Possibly. The FSA says you may need to register if you or your operation provide food to the community, even if it is free, 28 days before you start.

What if I have already started selling without registering? Register now rather than later, and contact your local food safety team. The requirement is to register at least 28 days before trading, so trading first is not the intended order, and the sensible move is to fix it promptly rather than hope.
