1. What a Child Trust Fund is, and whether you have one

Child Trust Funds were government-backed savings accounts opened for children born in the UK between 1 September 2002 and 2 January 2011. The government paid in a starting voucher, family could top it up, and the money was locked away until the child turned 18. The scheme is closed to new applicants, but around 6.3 million accounts were opened.

The two things that matter for you are simple. If you were born inside that window, there is very likely an account with your name on it. And if you are 18 or over, the money is yours to withdraw today, with no conditions attached to what you spend it on.

People born at the start of that window turned 18 in 2020. People born at the end of it will turn 18 in 2029, so accounts are still maturing. HMRC's chief customer officer put the age range plainly: if you are between 15 and 24, this is worth checking.

Two common misunderstandings are worth clearing up now. You do not need to remember your parents opening it, because in many cases they never did. Where a family did not open an account with the voucher, HMRC opened one for the child automatically with a provider chosen for them, which is exactly why so many of these are unclaimed: nobody in the household ever chose the provider or saw a statement. And it does not matter whether your family had money. The government contribution went to every eligible child.

2. The scale of the problem, and why it happened

The FCA published the current numbers on 1 September 2026, drawing on HMRC data. 760,000 matured Child Trust Funds remain unclaimed, worth an average of £2,000 each.

Multiply those two figures and you get roughly £1.52 billion sitting in accounts whose owners are not using them. That multiplication is ours, not the FCA's, and averages hide a wide spread: some accounts hold a few hundred pounds, others hold several thousand.

Why does this happen? Three reasons, and none of them are the account holder's fault.

The account was opened for you, not by you. Where the voucher went unused, HMRC allocated the child to a provider. No one in the family picked it, so no one remembers it.

Eighteen years is a long time. Families move house. Providers merge, rebrand or get acquired. Statements go to an address the family left in 2011.

Turning 18 does not come with a letter you will definitely receive. The FCA has explicitly flagged this as a problem it is investigating: cases where young adults cannot be contacted when they turn 18 and risk losing touch with the savings altogether. It has launched a review into Child Trust Funds covering exactly this, plus whether firms are delivering fair value under the Consumer Duty and whether vulnerable young adults face barriers to accessing their money. That review reports in 2027.

3. How to find yours, free, in about ten minutes

There are two routes and both cost nothing.

If you know who holds the account, contact that provider directly and ask to withdraw or transfer. You will need identification, which is normal and is not a fee.

If you have no idea where it is, use HMRC's free online tracing tool on GOV.UK. HMRC's own suggestion is to search "find my Child Trust Fund" on GOV.UK. You give HMRC your details, HMRC tells you which provider holds the account, and you then go to that provider to prove your identity and get the money.

What you will need to hand: your National Insurance number if you have it, your date of birth, and your current address history. If you are a parent or guardian looking on behalf of a child under 18, you can also use the tool.

That is the entire process. There is no application to write, no eligibility test to pass, and nothing to argue about. The money is already yours; the only question is which company is holding it.

4. The £400 problem, and the protection you do not get

Here is the part that made the FCA issue a press release rather than a quiet web page.

Companies advertise, including on social media, offering to "find" and claim your Child Trust Fund for you. The FCA says it has seen cases where customers were charged £400 to locate the account, and has seen some firms charging a monthly subscription for what is a one-off tracing service.

Look at that against the average pot. £400 on a £2,000 account is 20% of the money, for a task HMRC does for free in minutes. A monthly subscription for a one-off job is worse, because there is nothing recurring to deliver.

But the fee is not the most important part. This is:

Tracing a Child Trust Fund might be offered by firms regulated by the FCA. But the tracing service itself is not an activity that generally needs FCA authorisation. This means firms offering this service may not be covered by the FCA's cap on claims management fees, and customers may not be able to take complaints to the Financial Ombudsman Service.

Read that twice. A firm can look regulated, can genuinely be FCA-regulated for something else entirely, and the tracing service you are buying can still sit outside both the fee cap and the Ombudsman. If it goes wrong, the usual free route to redress may simply not be available to you.

The FCA's director of insurance, Chris Knight, put it directly: tracing and accessing your own Child Trust Fund costs nothing, so think twice about handing over a chunk of your savings to a claims firm for a job you can do yourself.

Worked example: Kofi in Nottingham

Kofi is 21, born in 2005, and starting his final year at university. He has never heard of a Child Trust Fund. An advert on his feed offers to find "forgotten government savings" and asks for a £400 fee, payable on success.

If his account holds the average £2,000, that fee leaves him with £1,600. The firm has done nothing HMRC would not have done for free, and because tracing generally sits outside FCA authorisation, if the firm mishandles the job Kofi may have no route to the Financial Ombudsman Service.

If he uses the HMRC tool instead, he spends about ten minutes, pays nothing, and keeps £2,000.

The difference is £400 for one evening's admin. Put another way, that is roughly the whole of a month's rent contribution in a shared house, or a term of books, handed over for a form he could fill in himself.

The uncomfortable part: Kofi would never have known the account existed without the advert. That is the genuine service these firms provide, and it is why they work. The answer is not to ignore the advert. It is to note the information, close it, and go to GOV.UK.

5. What to do once you have got it

This site is not going to tell you what to do with your own money, and a lump sum arriving at 18 or 21 is a personal decision. But three points are worth knowing before you decide.

You do not have to take it as cash. A matured Child Trust Fund can usually be transferred into an adult ISA rather than withdrawn, which keeps it outside Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax. If you do not need the money this month, that is the low-effort option.

If you do take it as cash, interest on it becomes taxable in the normal way once it is sitting in an ordinary savings account, subject to the allowances covered in our guide to tax on savings interest. For most 18 to 24 year olds the allowances will cover it comfortably, but it is worth understanding rather than assuming.

Check what it is invested in before you move it. Many Child Trust Funds are stakeholder accounts holding shares, and their value moves with markets. If yours is invested rather than in cash, the balance you see today is not guaranteed, and that cuts both ways.

6. The honest verdict

This is one of the rare articles on this site where the answer really is "there is free money and you should go and get it". If you were born between 1 September 2002 and 2 January 2011, spend ten minutes on GOV.UK today. The average unclaimed account holds £2,000 and there are 760,000 of them.

Do not pay anybody to do it. Not £400, not a percentage, and certainly not a monthly subscription for a one-off task. The service is free from HMRC, and the FCA has warned that the firms selling it may sit outside both its fee cap and the Financial Ombudsman Service, so you would be paying a premium price for less protection than you would normally have.

And if you are a parent of someone born in that window, forward this to them. The whole reason 760,000 of these are unclaimed is that the account was usually opened by HMRC rather than by the family, so nobody in the house ever knew it existed.

Frequently asked

I was born in that window but my family had no money to pay in. Do I still have one? Almost certainly yes. The government contribution went to every eligible child regardless of household income, and where the family did not use the voucher HMRC opened an account with a provider on the child's behalf. Family contributions were optional on top.

What if I have moved and changed my name? That does not stop you. The HMRC tracing tool works from your details and National Insurance number, and the provider's identity checks are designed to handle name changes. You may be asked for supporting documents such as a marriage certificate or deed poll.

Can I get it before I turn 18? No, other than in specific circumstances such as terminal illness. From 16 you can take over managing the account, choosing where it is invested, but you cannot withdraw until 18.

Is the money taxed when I take it out? No. Child Trust Funds are free of Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax, and taking the money out is not a taxable event. What changes is that once the cash sits in an ordinary savings account, any interest it then earns is taxable in the normal way.
