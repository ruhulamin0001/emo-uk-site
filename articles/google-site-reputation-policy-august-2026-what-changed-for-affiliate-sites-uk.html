Google Rewrote Its Site Reputation Policy on 28 August 2026. Affiliate Links Are Now Named as Fine, and the Enforcement Change Does Not Apply in the UK

Google's site reputation policy is the rule that killed the coupon pages and "best casinos" widgets bolted onto newspaper sites in 2024. On 28 August 2026 Google published an update to it, and on 30 August the enforcement changed. Most of the coverage has been about the European Commission and the Digital Markets Act. For a one-person UK affiliate site the interesting part is smaller and more useful: the policy page now lists what Google does not treat as a breach, and affiliate links are on that list. Here is what changed, what it does not do, and where the UK sits.

  • What changed on 28 August 2026: Google's Search Central blog post "Update to the Site Reputation Policy" says that "following discussion with the European Commission" it is "adjusting our enforcement approach within the European Economic Area (EEA) and clarifying the criteria we consider when applying the policy".
  • From 30 August, inside the EEA a manual action no longer suppresses the affected section for EEA searchers; instead "the affected section of the site may be separated in our systems so that, over time, it ranks independently from the rest of the site". Outside the EEA, the action still directly affects results for that section, and the rest of the site is untouched.
  • The UK is not in the EEA. For searchers in the UK, and for a UK site's UK traffic, enforcement is as it was. The clarified criteria apply everywhere.

Editorial columns, forums and syndicated news are on the same list.
  • Our verdict: nothing in this update changes what a single-author site with its own reviews and rel="sponsored" links needs to do. The risk it describes is renting your domain's authority to someone else's content, and if you write your own pages it is not your problem.

1. What the policy says now

The site reputation policy sits in Google's spam policies documentation, and the wording on 5 September 2026 defines it narrowly: it "applies where third-party content is published on a host site mainly because of that host's already-established ranking signals, which it has earned primarily from its first-party content. The goal of this tactic is for the content to rank better than it could otherwise on its own." Third-party content means content "created by an entity that's separate from the established host site", and the page gives examples: users of the site, freelancers, white-label services, and "content created by people not employed directly by the host site". It then says the thing that matters most for anyone reading nervously: "Having third-party content alone isn't inconsistent with the site reputation policy; it's only inconsistent if the third-party content is published on a host site mainly because of that host site's already-established ranking signals."

The examples of a breach are the ones you would expect: an educational site hosting sponsored payday loan reviews written by a third party that distributes the same page across the web, or a medical site hosting a low-quality "best casinos" page that is not integrated with the site. The examples of what is not a breach are the useful part. Wire and press release services. Syndicated news. Forums and comment sections. "Columns, opinion pieces, articles, and other work of an editorial nature." Advertorials whose purpose is to reach the publication's own readers. And, in full: "Using affiliate links throughout a page, with links treated appropriately, or embedding third-party ad units throughout a page." Google adds that the examples are non-exhaustive and that it "generally applies a presumption that individual pages (including new pages) match the overall quality of other pages" on the site.

2. What changed on 28 and 30 August 2026

The Search Central blog post of 28 August 2026, "Update to the Site Reputation Policy", recaps the 2024 launch, then says: "Following discussion with the European Commission, we are adjusting our enforcement approach within the European Economic Area (EEA) and clarifying the criteria we consider when applying the policy." Google records its own reservation, that "an overbroad application of the DMA could prevent us from addressing real threats to the integrity of our search results".

So inside the EEA the mechanism has changed from suppression to separation: the bolted-on section stops borrowing the host's authority and must rank on its own. The definition of the offence has not changed; the documentation now spells out the non-breach examples in the policy page itself.

3. Where the UK sits

The EEA is the 27 EU member states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway. The United Kingdom left the EU in 2020 and was never in the EEA on its own account. Google's post frames the enforcement change entirely in terms of where the searcher is: "a manual action regarding our site reputation policy will have a different effect for those searching in the EEA than outside of it". For a UK site whose readers are in the UK, the new EEA treatment does not apply to the traffic that matters; a manual action would still directly affect the flagged section for UK searchers, exactly as it did last month. If a share of your readers are in Ireland, Germany or France, those readers see the separated-ranking version. And because pages are "viewed by people globally", Google notes a single page can be under an action for one audience and not for another.

4. What the update does not do

It does not change what counts as a breach. It does not touch the separate core ranking updates, the helpful content signals or the thin affiliation policy, which lives on the same page and targets copied merchant descriptions rather than affiliate links as such. It does not say affiliate content is exempt from everything; the words are "with links treated appropriately", which in practice means rel="sponsored" or nofollow on paid links and a disclosure the reader can see, both of which our disclosure rules guide covers. It does not protect a site that publishes freelance or white-label content under its own name if that content exists mainly to ride the domain's authority; that was the policy's target in 2024 and remains its target. And it does not create a mediation route for UK-only sites; the mediation offer is written for the EEA arrangement and eligibility is not defined in the post.

5. A worked example in Norwich

Leah runs a one-person site in Norwich reviewing home coffee kit, with Amazon Associates links and an Awin programme. Every review is hers, with her own photographs, a disclosure at the top of each page and rel="sponsored" on every affiliate link. In August a content agency offered her £400 a month to host a "best online casinos UK" section under her domain, written by them, linked from her footer.

Run the policy over both halves. Her reviews are first-party content, and even the affiliate links throughout them are named in the policy as not a breach, provided the links are treated appropriately, which hers are. The casino section is the textbook breach: third-party content, unrelated to the site, placed there because her domain has earned ranking signals for coffee. For her UK readers a manual action on that section would suppress it directly; for any Irish readers it would be separated to rank on its own, which for a section with no standing of its own means it would not rank at all. Either way the £400 buys a manual action notice in Search Console and a reconsideration request, and the update of 28 August changes none of that. She declines, keeps writing her own reviews, and the policy does not apply to her.

6. The honest verdict

Google's 28 August 2026 update changes how site reputation manual actions bite inside the EEA from 30 August, and it clarifies the policy's examples everywhere. The UK is outside the EEA, so for a UK site's UK readers the enforcement is unchanged. The clarification is the useful part: affiliate links throughout a page, treated appropriately, are named as not a breach.

The policy is about renting a domain's authority to somebody else's content. If you write your own pages, disclose, and mark affiliate links as sponsored, this update asks nothing new of you. If you have been offered money to host a section you did not write, it is the reason to say no.

Frequently asked

Does the site reputation policy ban affiliate links? No. Google's spam policies page lists "using affiliate links throughout a page, with links treated appropriately" among things not considered inconsistent with the policy. Treated appropriately means marked as sponsored or nofollow and disclosed.

What changed on 30 August 2026? Inside the European Economic Area a manual action under the policy no longer directly suppresses the affected section; Google may instead separate it to rank independently. Outside the EEA the action directly affects the affected section as before. Source: Google Search Central blog, 28 August 2026.

Does the EEA change apply to UK sites? Not for UK searchers. The UK is not in the EEA, and Google's post ties the change to where the searcher is. A UK site's readers in EEA countries see the separated-ranking treatment.

What does the policy actually target? Third-party content published on an established site mainly to benefit from that site's ranking signals, such as a sponsored coupon or casino section written by an outside company. Editorial content, forums, syndicated news and your own affiliate reviews are not the target.
