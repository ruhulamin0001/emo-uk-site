A Clubcard Point Is Worth 1p and a Nectar Point Is Worth Half That. What the Two Schemes Actually Pay on a £120 Weekly Shop

Both big supermarkets now run their loyalty schemes on the same design: scan the card, collect points, and pay less on the shelf than the person who did not. The points are what the adverts talk about. They are also the smaller half of the deal. Here is what a point is worth at each, what a year of ordinary shopping earns, and where the real money sits.

1. What a Clubcard point is worth

Tesco's rule is simple and it is printed on its own site: spend £1 in store or online and collect 1 point. Points are turned into vouchers, and 150 points become a £1.50 voucher, which makes a point worth exactly 1p when spent on a Tesco shop or on fuel at a Tesco filling station. Vouchers are issued in £1.50 blocks, so a balance of 149 points buys nothing until the 150th arrives.

The second tier is the Reward Partners. Exchange a voucher with one of Tesco's hundred or so partners, from days out and rail tickets to restaurants and streaming subscriptions, and the voucher is worth twice its face value: a £5 voucher becomes £10 of partner credit. That makes a point worth 2p, but only on something a partner sells. The multiplier was 3x until June 2023, when Tesco cut it to 2x, so anyone remembering the old rate should recalculate.

Other collection rates on the site: 1 point per 2 litres of Tesco fuel, 1 point per £1 with Tesco Mobile, and 1 point per £4 on a Tesco Bank credit card. Clubcard Prices, the member-only shelf prices, are separate from points and are covered below.

2. What a Nectar point is worth

Nectar's rule at Sainsbury's is also on its site: collect 1 point per £1 on qualifying spend in Sainsbury's supermarkets and online, and 1 point per litre of fuel at Sainsbury's petrol stations. On the spending side, 500 points are worth £2.50, and points are spent in multiples of £2.50 in store or online. So a Nectar point is worth 0.5p, half a Clubcard point.

Nectar has its own partners, including Argos, eBay and Esso, where points can be collected and in some cases spent, and it runs bonus offers in the app that can multiply points on particular items. Those bonuses are real but they are unpredictable, so the honest base rate is the one on the site: half a penny per pound spent. Your Nectar Prices, the member-only shelf prices, work the same way as Clubcard Prices and are, again, separate from points.

3. A year of ordinary shopping at each

Take a household spending £120 a week on groceries, £6,240 a year, and scanning the card every time. At Tesco that is 6,240 points, £62.40 in vouchers on our arithmetic, or £124.80 of partner credit if every voucher is exchanged at 2x. At Sainsbury's it is 6,240 points, £31.20 to spend in store.

Put as a rate of return, Clubcard pays 1% of grocery spend back, or 2% through partners. Nectar pays 0.5%. On fuel, 1,000 litres a year earns 500 Clubcard points (£5) or 1,000 Nectar points (£5): identical.

Those numbers are not nothing, but they are small. £62.40 a year is £1.20 a week. The difference between the two schemes on the same basket, £31.20 a year, is well under one week's shopping. Any price gap between the two shops that runs above half a percent on your basket wipes the points difference out.

4. The bigger half: member prices

Both schemes now attach discounts to the card rather than to the points. Clubcard Prices and Your Nectar Prices show a lower shelf price to members on selected items, and the saving is taken at the till when the card is scanned. Neither retailer publishes an average saving per basket, and the items change weekly, so no honest article can give you a percentage. What can be said is the arithmetic: a single item on a member price saving £1 is worth the same as £100 of points-earning spend at Tesco or £200 at Sainsbury's.

That is the whole reason to scan the card, and it has a consequence the points adverts do not mention. If the member price is only available to card holders, the non-member price is not really the price; it is a penalty for not scanning. Comparing two supermarkets on their shelf prices without the cards now understates both.

5. A worked example in Luton

Fatima in Luton does one big shop a week for a family of four, about £120, and fills the car with 40 litres of petrol a week. Her nearest Tesco and Sainsbury's are the same distance away. She wants to know whether the loyalty scheme should decide which one she uses.

On points alone, on our arithmetic: a year at Tesco earns 6,240 points on groceries plus 1,040 on fuel (2,080 litres at 1 point per 2 litres), so 7,280 points, £72.80 in vouchers or £145.60 at Reward Partners. A year at Sainsbury's earns 6,240 plus 2,080 on fuel, so 8,320 points, £41.60 to spend in store. Tesco wins the points contest by £31.20 a year in store, or £104 if she uses every voucher at 2x.

Then she does the thing that matters and prices her actual basket at both, cards scanned, member prices applied. It comes out £4 a week cheaper at one of them. £4 a week is £208 a year, which is more than the entire points difference and more than the total points earned at either. The basket decided it, and the points were a rounding error on the decision. The only place the 2x rate changed anything was that she already paid for a streaming service and a couple of family days out each year, and routing those through Clubcard vouchers was worth about £70 a year that she would otherwise not have had.

6. The traps: expiry, partner value and shopping for points

Clubcard vouchers and Nectar points both have expiry rules, and both retailers say so in their terms. Check the date on your Clubcard vouchers when they arrive and the expiry rules in the Nectar app, because a lapsed voucher is a scheme that paid 0%.

The 2x partner rate is only worth 2p a point if the partner is somewhere you would have spent the money anyway, at a price you would have paid. A £10 voucher for a £20 day out you would not otherwise have taken is not a saving; it is a £5 voucher spent. Check the partner's price against buying direct before you exchange, because partner prices are not always the cheapest available.

And the biggest trap of all is shopping for points: driving further, buying the bigger pack, choosing the branded item, or picking the shop with the better scheme rather than the better prices. At 1p a point, an extra £5 spent to earn 5 points is £4.95 lost. Loyalty schemes are designed by retailers to change where you shop; the arithmetic says they should not be allowed to.

7. The honest verdict

A Clubcard point is worth 1p, 2p at Reward Partners. A Nectar point is worth 0.5p. On £120 a week that is £62.40 a year against £31.20, and the gap is smaller than one week's shopping.

The member prices are the bigger half at both, and neither publishes the average saving. Price your own basket at each with the card scanned. That number, not the points, should decide where you shop.

Scan the card every time, use 2x only on things you already buy, and never drive past a cheaper shop for points. Anything else is paying the supermarket to run its own loyalty scheme.

Frequently asked

How much is a Tesco Clubcard point worth? 1p when spent at Tesco, because 150 points become a £1.50 voucher. Vouchers exchanged with Reward Partners are worth 2x, so 2p a point on partner purchases.

How much is a Nectar point worth? 0.5p at Sainsbury's, because 500 points are worth £2.50. Points are spent in multiples of £2.50.

Which scheme is better for fuel? Neither. Tesco gives 1 point per 2 litres and Sainsbury's 1 point per litre, which at 1p and 0.5p a point are both half a penny per litre.

Should I switch supermarket for the better loyalty scheme? No. The points difference on a £120 weekly shop is about £31 a year. A price difference of £1 a week on your basket outweighs it. Price your own basket at both with the cards scanned.
