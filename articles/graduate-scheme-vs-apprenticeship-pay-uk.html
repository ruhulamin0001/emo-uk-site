Five Years After School, One Of Them Is £107,000 Up And Owes Nothing

Comparing a degree with an apprenticeship usually turns into an argument about values. It does not have to. Take the statutory minimum wage rates, take the Plan 5 student loan rules, and the five year comparison produces a single number: the graduate starting salary at which the two routes draw level. It is higher than most graduate schemes pay.

The short version

  • Five years of apprentice and post apprentice wages at the statutory minimum comes to about £107,500, with no student debt and no repayments.
  • The break-even graduate starting salary is about £56,600, because the graduate has two earning years against the apprentice's five, and loses 9% of everything over £25,000.
  • The apprentice minimum wage is £8 an hour from April 2026, the same as the under 18 rate, and it applies to apprentices under 19 or in the first year whatever their age.
  • A 21 year old apprentice in year one gets £8. A 21 year old who is not an apprentice gets £12.71. That is £9,184 a year on full time hours, and it is GOV.UK's own example.
  • Plan 5 repayments are 9% of income over £25,000, with interest currently 4.1%, and the loan is repaid whether or not the degree helped.
  • The five year window flatters the apprentice, and that is the honest limit of this arithmetic. Graduate earnings curves usually steepen after year five.

1. The numbers both routes actually run on

Everything below uses figures published by GOV.UK, and nothing else. No assumed graduate premium, no survey averages, no projections.

Minimum wage rates from April 2026:

  • 21 and over: £12.71
  • 18 to 20: £10.85
  • Under 18: £8
  • Apprentice: £8

Who gets the apprentice rate: apprentices aged under 19, or aged 19 and over and in the first year of their apprenticeship. Once an apprentice is 19 or over and has completed the first year, they move to the minimum wage for their age.

Student loan Plan 5, which covers students who started courses in England from August 2023 onward: repayment threshold £25,000 a year, or £2,083 a month. You repay 9% of income over the threshold. Interest is currently 4.1%, and GOV.UK notes that interest is still applied even when you are not working or are below the threshold.

Two modelling choices, stated up front so you can disagree with them. Both routes are costed on a 37.5 hour week across 52 weeks, which is 1,950 hours a year. And rates are held at April 2026 levels for all five years, which understates both sides, since the minimum wage rises each April and salaries generally rise too. Holding them flat keeps the comparison honest rather than accurate, because forecasting one side and not the other is how these comparisons usually get rigged.

2. Five years as an apprentice, at the legal floor

Take someone who starts a two year apprenticeship at 18 and stays in work afterwards, paid the statutory minimum at every stage. This is the floor, not the expectation: many apprentices are paid above it, and the number below is therefore pessimistic.

  • Year 1, age 18, apprentice rate £8: 1,950 hours, £15,600
  • Year 2, age 19, past the first year, so the 18 to 20 rate of £10.85: £21,157
  • Year 3, age 20, £10.85: £21,157
  • Year 4, age 21, £12.71: £24,784
  • Year 5, age 22, £12.71: £24,784

Five year total: about £107,482.

Student debt: none. Student loan repayments: none. The whole of that figure is earned income, taxed normally, with the £12,570 Personal Allowance applying as it would to anyone.

The single largest jump in that table is year one to year two: £5,557, from finishing the first year of the apprenticeship and turning 19. Nothing about the job changed. The rate rule did.

3. Five years as a graduate, and the break-even salary

Now the graduate. Three years of study from 18, then a graduate role at 21, so years four and five are the earning years. Part time student work is excluded, as is any part time work by the apprentice, to keep the comparison symmetrical.

Rather than assume a graduate salary, work out the one that makes the routes equal. Call it G.

Over the two earning years the graduate takes home 2G, less student loan repayments of 9% on everything over £25,000 in each year, which is 0.18 times (G minus 25,000).

Setting that equal to the apprentice's £107,482:

2G minus 0.18 times (G minus 25,000) equals 107,482
1.82G plus 4,500 equals 107,482
1.82G equals 102,982
G equals about £56,584

So on these assumptions a graduate would need to walk into a role paying roughly £56,600 at 21 for the two routes to be level on cash five years after leaving school. Very few graduate schemes start there.

Note what is doing the work. It is not mainly the debt. Even at zero repayments the graduate would need £53,741 to match, because the dominant factor is simply that the apprentice has been paid for five years and the graduate for two. The loan adds around £2,800 to the gap, not the whole of it.

Now the honest counterweight, because this arithmetic has a shelf life. A five year window is chosen by the apprentice's advocate, and it flatters them. Graduate salary curves typically steepen after the first few years while minimum wage progression, by definition, tracks the legal floor. Extend the window to ten or fifteen years and many degree routes overtake. The correct reading is not that one route wins; it is that the apprentice is a long way ahead at 23 and the graduate is betting on the slope afterwards. Whether that bet is good depends on the degree, the field and the person, and nobody can tell you that from a table.

Two more things the arithmetic leaves out, both real. The graduate carries a debt with 4.1% interest applied even in years when nothing is repaid, which matters for mortgage affordability conversations long before it is cleared. And the apprentice's five years include four years of workplace experience, which is worth something in the labour market that no table captures.

4. The year one apprentice pay trap

One rule catches people every year, and GOV.UK spells it out with its own example.

The apprentice rate of £8 applies to apprentices who are under 19, or 19 and over and in the first year of the apprenticeship. Age alone does not get you off it.

GOV.UK's example: an apprentice aged 21 in the first year of their apprenticeship is entitled to a minimum hourly rate of £8. And its companion example: an apprentice aged 21 who has completed the first year is entitled to £12.71.

That is a gap of £4.71 an hour, or £9,184 a year on 1,950 hours, turning purely on whether twelve months of the apprenticeship have elapsed.

The practical consequences, if you are considering an apprenticeship at 21 or older:

Know the date your first year ends and check your payslip in the month after it. The uplift is not automatic in every payroll and it is the kind of thing that goes unnoticed for months.

Ask what the employer pays, not what the law requires. Many pay above the apprentice minimum, especially for older starters, and it is a perfectly reasonable question before you accept.

Weigh the first year against what you are giving up. A 21 year old moving from a minimum wage job into year one of an apprenticeship takes a pay cut of £9,184 for that year. It may well be worth it for the qualification and the trajectory. It should be a decision taken with the number in front of you, not discovered in the first payslip.

If you think you are being paid below the rate you are entitled to, GOV.UK publishes a minimum wage calculator and Acas runs a helpline.

5. Worked example: Kelsey and Rob, both 18 in Stoke

Kelsey and Rob leave the same sixth form in Stoke at 18. Kelsey takes a two year engineering apprenticeship. Rob goes to university for three years and joins a graduate scheme at 21.

Kelsey, five years, at the statutory minimum throughout: £15,600, then £21,157, £21,157, £24,784, £24,784. Total £107,482. No debt, no repayments. Her employer in fact pays above the minimum from year two, so this understates her.

Rob, on a £30,000 graduate scheme: two years at £30,000 is £60,000. Plan 5 repayments are 9% of £5,000 over the threshold, which is £450 a year, so £900 over the two years. Net £59,100.

At 23, Kelsey is £48,382 ahead and Rob has a loan accruing 4.1%.

How long to close it? Suppose Rob then out-earns Kelsey by £6,000 a year, gross. After Plan 5 takes 9% of that difference, he closes the gap by about £5,460 a year. At that pace it takes roughly nine more years, putting him level somewhere around age 32, before counting the outstanding loan itself.

Change the assumption and the answer changes sharply, which is the point. If Rob out-earns her by £12,000 a year he is level around age 27. If he out-earns her by £3,000 he is not level until his late thirties, and Plan 5 loans are written off after a set period, so a substantial part of his debt may never be repaid at all. Check the current write-off period on GOV.UK, because it differs by plan.

What this is not. It is not an argument against university. It is the shape of the trade: a large, certain head start for the apprentice, against an uncertain slope for the graduate. Anyone who tells a 17 year old that one of those is obviously correct is not being straight with them.

The most useful thing either of them can do is find out what people five and ten years into their actual intended field are earning, rather than comparing against an average. The figures in this article set the floor and the break-even; the slope is the bit that decides it, and it is specific to the work.

The honest verdict

Five years after school, an apprentice paid nothing but the legal minimum has earned about £107,500 and owes nothing. For a graduate to be level at the same point they would need to have started on roughly £56,600, and most graduate schemes do not.

Most of that gap is not the student loan. It is three years of earning against three years of not earning. The debt adds about £2,800 to it over the two years, which is real but secondary.

The honest caveat is the window. Five years is chosen by whoever wants the apprentice to win. Graduate earnings usually steepen later, and over ten or fifteen years many degree routes overtake. So the real question is not which is better, it is how confident you are about the slope in your particular field, and that is worth researching properly rather than guessing.

And if you are 21 or over and considering an apprenticeship, put the year one number in front of you first. The apprentice rate is £8 whatever your age in that first year, against £12.71 for a 21 year old who is not an apprentice. That is £9,184 for twelve months, and it is a decision, not a surprise.

Frequently asked

Where does the £56,600 come from? It is the graduate starting salary at which two years of graduate earnings, less Plan 5 repayments of 9% over £25,000, equal five years of apprentice and post apprentice earnings at statutory minimum rates. It is derived from GOV.UK figures, not from any survey of actual graduate pay.

Is the apprentice minimum wage really the same as the under 18 rate? Yes. From April 2026 both are £8 an hour. The apprentice rate applies to apprentices under 19, and to those aged 19 and over who are in the first year of the apprenticeship.

Does an apprentice pay back a student loan? Not for the apprenticeship itself, which is funded differently and does not create a student loan. An apprentice who previously took out a loan for another course still repays that one under its own plan rules.

Does this mean an apprenticeship is better? On five year cash, on these assumptions, yes and by a wide margin. Over a longer horizon it depends entirely on the earnings slope in the specific field, which this arithmetic cannot tell you. Use the numbers as the floor of the comparison, then research pay at five and ten years in the actual career you are choosing between.
