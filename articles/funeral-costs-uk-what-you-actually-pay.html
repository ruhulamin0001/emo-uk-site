A Funeral Bill Has Two Halves, and Only One of Them Moves When You Shop Around

Funeral quotes are usually presented as one number, which hides the fact that they are two different things added together. Knowing which half is the funeral director's own fee and which half is money passed straight through to somebody else tells you exactly where to push and where there is no point.

The short version

  • Funeral Expenses Payment can cover burial fees for a particular plot and cremation fees in full, including the cost of the doctor's certificate. Those are the third party charges.
  • Everything else is capped at £1,000. That is funeral director's fees, flowers and the coffin, all inside one £1,000 ceiling.
  • If there was a pre-paid funeral plan, the cap drops to £120. GOV.UK states this outright, and it is the single biggest trap on the page.
  • Moving the body is only covered if it moves more than 50 miles within the UK. Under 50 miles it is not a claimable item.
  • Whatever you get is deducted from money you receive from the estate, though not from a house or personal things left to a surviving spouse or civil partner.
  • Scotland is different. Funeral Support Payment replaced Funeral Expenses Payment there and is applied for through mygov.scot.

1. The two halves of a funeral bill

Every funeral quote is made of two components, and funeral directors themselves separate them on the invoice, though not always on the first quote.

Disbursements. These are third party charges the funeral director pays on your behalf and passes through. The cremation fee or the burial fee for the plot, the doctor's certificate where one is needed, the minister or celebrant, the newspaper notice. The funeral director has no control over most of these and, in a straightforward arrangement, no margin on them either.

The funeral director's own fee. Collection and care of the body, the coffin, the hearse and bearers, staff time, the arrangement of the service. This is the part that is priced by a business competing for your custom, and it is the part that varies by hundreds of pounds between two firms in the same town.

The practical consequence is simple. Ringing round for a cheaper cremation fee is close to pointless, because the crematorium sets it and every firm in the area is quoting you the same one. Ringing round for a cheaper funeral director's fee is not pointless at all. Ask every firm to quote the two halves separately, in writing, and compare only the second half.

There is also a third option that sits outside this structure entirely and is worth knowing exists: a direct cremation, with no service at the crematorium, which the family can follow with a separate memorial at a time and place of their choosing. It is a cheaper product because it uses less of everything in the second half of the bill, not because it cuts corners on the first.

2. What Funeral Expenses Payment actually pays

Funeral Expenses Payment, also called Funeral Payment, is for people on certain benefits who are arranging a funeral. GOV.UK lists what it can help pay for:

  • burial fees for a particular plot
  • cremation fees, including the cost of the doctor's certificate
  • travel to arrange or go to the funeral
  • the cost of moving the body within the UK, if it is being moved more than 50 miles
  • death certificates or other documents

Then the line that defines the whole scheme: "You can also get up to £1,000 for any other funeral expenses, such as funeral director's fees, flowers or the coffin."

Read the two lists against section one and the design becomes obvious. The first list is the disbursements half, and it is covered. The second half, the funeral director's own fee, is where the £1,000 ceiling bites. If the funeral director's fee, coffin and flowers come to £2,600, the payment contributes £1,000 of it and you are finding £1,600.

GOV.UK does not pretend otherwise: "The payment will not usually cover all of the costs of the funeral." How much you get also depends on other money available, for example from an insurance policy or the estate.

On payment mechanics, there is a detail worth using. If you have already paid, the money goes into your account. If you have not yet paid, it goes directly to the funeral director. Telling the funeral director you have claimed, before you settle the bill, therefore changes who carries the wait.

3. The two caps that catch people

The pre-paid plan cap, and it is severe. GOV.UK: "If the deceased had a pre-paid funeral plan, you can only get up to £120 to help pay for items not covered by their plan." Not £1,000. One hundred and twenty pounds.

The logic is that the plan has already bought the funeral. The problem in practice is that plans vary enormously in what they include, and a plan that covers the funeral director's services but not the cremation fee can leave a real gap that the £120 does not begin to close. If you are arranging a funeral where a plan exists, get the plan schedule out and read what it excludes before you commit to anything on top, because state help against those exclusions is capped at £120.

The estate deduction. GOV.UK: "Your Funeral Expenses Payment will be deducted from any money you get from the deceased's estate." The estate here includes money or property they had, but not a house or personal things left to a widow, widower or surviving civil partner. So the payment is not a grant on top of an inheritance. Where there is money in the estate, it is closer to a cash-flow bridge than a subsidy.

And one geographic trap. In Scotland the scheme is Funeral Support Payment, which replaced Funeral Expenses Payment, and it is applied for through mygov.scot with its own rules and its own amounts. Do not read the England, Wales and Northern Ireland figures across.

4. Worked example: Marek in Grimsby

Marek is arranging his mother's funeral. He receives Universal Credit. The quote he has been given is one number: £3,450.

Step one, split it. He asks the funeral director to itemise. The answer comes back as disbursements of £1,180, being the cremation fee and the doctor's certificate, plus the funeral director's fee, coffin and cars at £2,270.

Step two, apply the rules. The £1,180 falls in the covered list. The £2,270 falls under the £1,000 ceiling. So the claim contributes £1,180 plus £1,000, which is £2,180, and Marek is looking at £1,270 of his own money.

Step three, the bit that actually changes the number. Since the £1,000 cap is fixed, every pound he can take off the funeral director's half is a pound straight off his own contribution. He gets two more itemised quotes. The second firm's equivalent half is £1,760. That is £510 less, and because the cap is fixed, his own outlay falls from £1,270 to £760. The disbursements did not move at all between the two quotes, which is exactly what section one predicts.

The counterfactual worth stating. If his mother had held a pre-paid plan, the ceiling on the non-disbursement side would have been £120, not £1,000, and the entire exercise above would have been about reading the plan schedule instead.

5. Where pushing back works

Ask for the itemised price list. Funeral directors in the UK are required to publish a standardised price list, which is what makes the two halves comparable between firms. Ask for it rather than for a bespoke quote, because a bespoke quote is designed around what you said you wanted rather than around what things cost.

Decide about the coffin separately. It is in the capped half and the price range is wide.

Ask what happens if you decline the limousines. Cars are in the capped half. Family transport is often the easiest thing to drop without anybody feeling it.

Do not shop around on the crematorium fee. It is set by the crematorium. If a firm quotes you a different figure for the same crematorium, ask why.

Check whether there is a public health funeral. Where nobody is able to arrange or pay for a funeral, the council has a duty to arrange one. It is a limited service and it is rarely the answer people want, but it exists and no one should be borrowing at high interest without knowing that.

The honest verdict

The useful thing to know about funeral costs is structural rather than numerical. Half the bill is fixed by third parties and half is a business's own price, and the state help is built along exactly that line. Burial and cremation fees covered, everything else capped at £1,000, or at £120 where a pre-paid plan exists.

That means the entire saving available to you sits in the second half, and the whole method is to get itemised quotes and compare only that half. On an ordinary funeral, that is worth several hundred pounds.

Where this page does not help: it will not make the disbursements cheaper, and it does not apply in Scotland, where Funeral Support Payment is a different scheme. If money is the immediate problem rather than the eventual one, ask the funeral director about payment timing and ask the council about a public health funeral before borrowing.

Frequently asked

Do I have to be on benefits to get Funeral Expenses Payment? Yes. It is for people receiving certain qualifying benefits who are arranging the funeral, and GOV.UK sets out the list on its eligibility page. It is not a universal payment and there is no version of it for people not on those benefits.

Does it cover travel? Travel to arrange or go to the funeral is in the covered list, as are death certificates and other documents. Moving the body within the UK is covered only if it is being moved more than 50 miles.

What if the funeral costs more than everything the payment covers? Then you pay the difference, and GOV.UK says plainly that the payment will not usually cover all of the costs. This is why comparing the funeral director's own half of the quote is the practical step, because the capped half is the only half you can influence.

Is there separate help for a child's funeral? Yes. GOV.UK runs a separate route for help with a child's funeral costs, which sits alongside Funeral Expenses Payment rather than inside it. Check that page rather than assuming the rules above apply.
