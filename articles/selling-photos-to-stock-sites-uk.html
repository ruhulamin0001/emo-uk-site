The Rate Is Not a Secret. It Is Published, and It Is 33 Percent of a Very Small Number

Stock photography is usually discussed with anecdotes, which is odd, because the biggest library in the business publishes its contributor royalty rate, a worked example and a minimum payment on a public help page. Once you have those three numbers the question answers itself.

Ruhul Amin Published 15 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Adobe Stock pays 33% on photos, vectors and illustrations, and 35% on video. Those are the published standard royalty rates, last updated on 13 July 2026.
  • The rate is applied to the net price per licence, not to a list price. Discounts come off before your share is calculated.
  • Adobe's own worked example is the useful bit. A plan at US$29.99 a month for 10 photo licences gives a royalty of US$0.99 per licensed photo.
  • There is a published floor on the largest subscription. On plans of 350 or more items a month, the minimum is US$0.33 per licensed item for contributors with 0 to 999 lifetime licences, rising to US$0.36 and then US$0.38.
  • So the unit of income is cents, not pounds. Everything else in this decision is a question of how many licences you can realistically generate.
  • It is a portfolio business, not a photography business. One excellent photograph is worth less than a large library of adequate ones, which is the part most people find out too late.

1. The published rates

Adobe publishes this on a help page rather than burying it in a contract, and the page is incorporated by reference into the Adobe Stock Contributor Agreement. From its royalty rates page, last updated 13 July 2026:

Content typeRoyalty rate
Photos, vectors, illustrations33%
Video35%

The footnote under that table matters as much as the table. The rate "is applied to the net price per licensed content, based on the U.S. purchase price and including any discounts".

Read that carefully. Your share is a third of what the customer actually paid after discounts, in US dollars, not a third of any headline price. On a heavily discounted enterprise plan, a third of the net is a small number even before you convert it to sterling.

Adobe also notes that content licensed through custom agreements and non-standard plans is paid either at those same 33% and 35% rates on the attributable amount, or at a flat rate.

2. Adobe's own worked example, and the floor under it

This is the single most useful thing on the page, because it is Adobe's arithmetic rather than anybody else's.

Adobe's example: "if the plan costs US$29.99 per month for 10 monthly photo licenses, then the royalty amount (rounded up to the nearest cent) would be US$0.99 per licensed photo (US$29.99 / 10 photos x 33%)."

Ninety-nine US cents. That is the number for a small subscription plan, and it is the company's own illustration.

Underneath it there is a published floor for the largest plans. On the Adobe Stock large subscription, meaning 350 or more standard items a month, the minimum royalty per licensed standard item is:

Lifetime licencesMinimum royalty per licensed item
0 to 999US$0.33
1,000 to 9,999US$0.36
10,000 and aboveUS$0.38

Adobe describes these as ensuring "experienced Contributors with larger portfolios receive consistent baseline compensation as their work is licensed through high-volume subscription plans", which is a fair description and also tells you what the high-volume end of the market pays: about a third of a dollar per download.

So the working range for a contributor is roughly 33 cents at the volume end to around a dollar on a small subscription, with higher amounts on extended and enhanced licences that are far rarer.

3. Turning that into an hourly rate

Here is the arithmetic nobody in this niche wants to put on the page. Every assumption below is labelled, so you can replace it with your own.

Take the £12.71 National Living Wage for people aged 21 and over from April 2026 as a benchmark, and take Adobe's US$0.99 example as the per-licence figure, which is generous relative to the 33 cent floor.

Ignoring exchange rates for a moment and treating a dollar as roughly a unit of currency, clearing one hour at the benchmark needs somewhere in the region of 13 licensed downloads. At the 33 cent floor it needs closer to 39.

Now put an hour of work against that. An hour will not produce 13 sellable, keyworded, correctly released images from scratch. For most people an hour produces one or two finished images, or covers the keywording of rather more. So a single image has to be downloaded repeatedly over years to repay the hour that made it.

That is the whole economics of the thing, and it is why the honest answer to "is stock photography worth it per hour" is no, not on any single image, and not in the first year.

4. Why this is a portfolio business

Once the unit is cents, the only variable that matters is how many licensed downloads you generate, and that is a function of library size and searchability rather than of photographic quality.

Three consequences follow, and they are uncomfortable for people who came to this as photographers.

Keywording is the job. An unfindable image earns nothing regardless of how good it is. Time spent on metadata converts to income more directly than time spent shooting.

Dull sells. The images that license repeatedly tend to be generic, well-lit and easy to place: ordinary people doing ordinary things, with copy space. Distinctive personal work is usually the worst performing part of a portfolio.

Releases are a hard gate. Recognisable people need a model release and much private property needs a property release. No release means no commercial licence, and it is not a formality you can add later once the shoot is over.

There is also a structural point worth stating plainly. Different libraries pay differently and several operate tiered rates that depend on volume, so the figures above are Adobe's and should not be read across to anyone else. If you want the general shape of how long any of this takes to pay, the timeline on a first affiliate sale covers the same lag between work and money in a different market.

Worked example: Dermot in Newry

Dermot has a good camera and about 40 hours spare over three months. He uploads 300 images, which is roughly eight minutes an image across shooting, editing, keywording and upload.

Assume 2% of his library is licensed in a given month. That is an assumption, not a measurement, and it is at the optimistic end for a new portfolio. It gives six downloads a month.

At Adobe's US$0.99 example, that is about US$6 a month. At the 33 cent floor it is about US$2.

Against 40 hours of work, the first year returns something in the region of US$24 to US$72. Per hour, that is a fraction of a pound.

What changes it. Nothing he does to any individual image. Only two things move the number: a much larger library, and a much higher licence rate per image, which comes from being findable rather than from being better.

The honest reading. If Dermot enjoys the process and treats the money as a small annuity on work he wanted to do anyway, this is fine. If he needs the 40 hours to produce income, he should sell those 40 hours almost any other way.

5. When it is still worth doing

Three cases survive the arithmetic honestly.

You already shoot. If the images exist and the alternative is a hard drive, then keywording and uploading is genuinely incremental income for a marginal amount of extra work. The shooting hours are already spent.

You are building for years, not months. A portfolio in the thousands, added to steadily, does eventually produce a monthly figure that means something. The people earning from stock are almost always people who have been doing it for a long time.

Video, at the margin. Adobe's rate on video is 35% rather than 33%, and clip prices are typically higher than photo prices, so the per-licence figure is better. The production cost per asset is also higher, so this is not free money, but the unit economics are less brutal.

What does not work is the plan most people arrive with: shoot for a few weekends, upload a couple of hundred images, and wait for it to become a side income. The published rates say why.

The honest verdict

Stock photography does not clear a reasonable hourly rate, and you do not have to take that on trust because the numbers are published. Adobe pays 33% on photos and 35% on video, its own example puts a subscription licence at US$0.99, and its floor on the largest plans is US$0.33 per download.

At those unit prices an hour of work at the UK's April 2026 benchmark of £12.71 needs something in the region of 13 to 39 downloads to repay it, and an hour does not produce 13 sellable images. The gap is closed only by time and library size, not by skill.

So the honest verdict is: a poor use of hours you could sell elsewhere, and a reasonable thing to do with images you already have. If you go in, go in knowing the unit is cents, that keywording matters more than the photograph, and that releases are a gate you cannot go back and fix.

Where this does not help you: it is Adobe's rate card, and other libraries pay differently, several on volume tiers. Check the contributor terms of whichever library you are actually uploading to before you plan around any of these figures.

Frequently asked

What does Adobe Stock actually pay per download? The published rate is 33% of the net price for photos, vectors and illustrations and 35% for video. Adobe's own example on that page works out at US$0.99 per licensed photo on a US$29.99 plan covering 10 licences, and the published minimum on its largest subscription is US$0.33 per licensed item for contributors with under 1,000 lifetime licences.

Do other stock libraries pay the same? No, and the figures above should not be read across. Several libraries operate tiered rates where your percentage depends on cumulative volume, and terms change. Read the contributor agreement of the library you are uploading to.

Do I need model releases? For commercial licensing of recognisable people, yes, and much identifiable private property needs a property release too. This is not a formality you can add afterwards, so it has to be handled at the time of the shoot.

Is video better than photos? On rate, marginally: Adobe pays 35% on video against 33% on other content, and clip prices tend to be higher. Production cost per asset is higher too, so it improves the unit economics without changing the basic shape of the business.
