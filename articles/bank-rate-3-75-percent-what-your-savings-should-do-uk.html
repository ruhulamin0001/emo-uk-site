Your Savings Rate Is Not Waiting for the Bank of England, and Neither Should You Be

Bank Rate has been sitting at 3.75% and the Monetary Policy Committee votes again at noon on Thursday 17 September 2026. Most savers spend that week watching the wrong number. The rate on your own account, and the point at which the taxman starts taking a cut of the interest, matter far more than the vote does.

1. Where Bank Rate actually is, and what happens on 17 September

Bank Rate is 3.75%. The Monetary Policy Committee held it there at its meeting on 30 July 2026, voting 6 to 3, with three members arguing for an immediate rise to 4%. That split matters more than the headline. A 6 to 3 hold is not a committee drifting towards cuts.

The next decision is announced at 12pm on Thursday 17 September 2026. The August inflation figures are published the morning before, at 7am on Wednesday 16 September, so the Committee votes with that print in hand. There is one more piece of context worth knowing: the Bank of England was granted exceptional pre release access to the ONS labour market bulletin at 10am on Monday 14 September 2026, so that the data were available for the MPC meeting held that day. The Committee has already seen the jobs numbers you are reading about now.

What a 3.75% Bank Rate does not do is set the rate on your savings account. It sets the rate at which commercial banks borrow from and deposit with the Bank of England. Everything after that is a commercial decision made by your bank. The same commercial logic is why banks pay cash to move your current account while paying next to nothing on the balance already sitting in it.

2. Why your account did not move last time either

The gap between Bank Rate and what an ordinary easy access account pays is not a scandal, it is the business model. A bank pays what it needs to pay to hold on to the deposits it wants. If it already has plenty, it pays less.

That is why the pattern is so consistent. New accounts, and accounts with a bonus rate attached, get priced close to Bank Rate. Accounts you opened three or four years ago and have not touched since get repriced downwards quietly, often in stages, and the notification arrives by email or in an app message you did not open.

So the honest answer to "should I wait for the 17th before moving my money" is no. Nothing about the decision changes what your existing bank has already chosen to pay you. If the rate is poor today, it will still be poor on the 18th.

3. The balance at which tax starts eating the gain

This is the part that gets left out of most savings coverage, and it is the part that actually determines whether chasing another 0.3 percentage points is worth the afternoon.

The Personal Savings Allowance lets a basic rate taxpayer earn 1,000 pounds of savings interest a year with no tax on it. A higher rate taxpayer gets 500 pounds. An additional rate taxpayer gets nothing at all. Above the allowance, interest is taxed at your marginal rate. That tax is the reason to look at the cash ISA allowance changes coming in 2027 before the allowance year runs out.

Turn that into a balance. At a rate of 3.75%:

  • A basic rate taxpayer uses up the full 1,000 pound allowance at roughly 26,700 pounds of savings.
  • A higher rate taxpayer uses up the 500 pound allowance at roughly 13,300 pounds.

Past that point, every extra pound of interest is taxed at 20% or 40%. A higher rate taxpayer with 30,000 pounds in a taxable easy access account is giving away 40% of the interest on everything above about 13,300 pounds. That is a far bigger number than the difference between the best and worst easy access rates on the market. There is more detail on how HMRC collects it in our guide to tax on savings interest in the UK.

4. Worked example: Farhana in Luton

Farhana is a higher rate taxpayer. She has 30,000 pounds sitting in an easy access account that she opened in 2023 and has not looked at since. It now pays 2.1%.

At 2.1%, she earns 630 pounds of interest in a year. Her Personal Savings Allowance as a higher rate taxpayer is 500 pounds, so 130 pounds of that is taxable at 40%, costing her 52 pounds. She keeps 578 pounds.

She moves the same 30,000 pounds to an account paying 3.75%. Now she earns 1,125 pounds. 500 pounds is covered by the allowance, 625 pounds is taxed at 40%, costing 250 pounds. She keeps 875 pounds.

The move is worth 297 pounds a year to her after tax. Note what happened on the way: her gross interest went up by 495 pounds, but the tax took 198 pounds of that back. That is the bit the "switch and earn more" headlines do not show you. It is still clearly worth doing. It is just worth doing with the real number in front of you.

If Farhana had used a cash ISA for part of that balance, the interest would sit outside the tax calculation entirely. Whether that is the right move depends on what rate the ISA pays, because a taxable account at a much higher rate can still win.

5. What is actually worth doing this week

Four things, in order of how much money they are worth.

  1. Find out what your current account actually pays. Not what it paid when you opened it. Log in and read the rate. This takes five minutes and is the single highest paid five minutes in the list.
  2. Work out whether you are over the Personal Savings Allowance threshold. The two balances above are the ones to check yourself against.
  3. If you are over it, look at an ISA for the excess before you look at chasing a slightly better taxable rate.
  4. Ignore the announcement on the 17th unless you are about to fix. If you are choosing between a one year fixed savings bond today and one next week, that is the only situation where the decision genuinely changes the sums. A regular saver is a different calculation again, and we have walked through what an 8% regular saver actually pays separately, because the headline rate on those is not what you earn.

One thing not to do: do not move money into a fixed term bond purely because you expect a cut. Three of nine MPC members voted for a rise at the last meeting. Nobody on that committee is signalling what the comment sections think they are.

The honest verdict

Bank Rate at 3.75% is background noise for most savers. The two numbers that decide how much you actually keep are the rate on your own account, which your bank sets for its own reasons, and the Personal Savings Allowance threshold, which turns into a balance of about 26,700 pounds for a basic rate taxpayer and about 13,300 pounds for a higher rate taxpayer at current rates.

If your money is in an account you opened years ago and have not checked, that is worth more of your attention this week than the vote on Thursday. If your money is already in a competitive account and you are under the allowance, then the truthful answer is that the 17 September decision does not require you to do anything at all.

Frequently asked

Will my savings rate go up if the Bank of England raises Bank Rate on 17 September? Not automatically. Banks are not obliged to pass on a rise and historically pass rises on to savers more slowly and less completely than they pass on cuts. Fixed rate bonds usually reprice first because they are competing for a specific pot of money.

Should I wait until after the decision to open a fixed rate savings account? That is the one case where waiting can be rational, because a fixed bond locks in a rate for the whole term. Even then the market usually prices an expected move in before the announcement, so the gain from waiting a few days is often small.

Do I have to declare savings interest to HMRC? Banks report interest to HMRC directly. If you are employed, HMRC usually collects any tax due by adjusting your tax code rather than asking you to file a return. If you already file a Self Assessment return, the interest goes on it.

Does the Personal Savings Allowance apply to ISA interest? No, and it does not need to. Interest inside a cash ISA is tax free and does not use up any of your Personal Savings Allowance, so the allowance stays available for interest earned outside the ISA.
