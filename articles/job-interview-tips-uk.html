Six in Ten Interviewers Decide Within Fifteen Minutes. What Happens in Those Minutes

Getting the interview is the hard part, and you have already done it. What follows is a short, oddly predictable process where most of the outcome is settled early, and where the most common reasons for rejection are entirely preventable the night before.

1. The odds, so you can calibrate

The average UK job advert now attracts around 340 applicants, a 182% increase since 2021, and employers report roughly 280 applications per role. Of those, only about 2% are invited to interview.

Two useful consequences. First, if you have an interview, you are not there by accident and you can stop treating it as a plea. Second, the process is slow because volume is high. UK employers typically run 2 to 3 rounds and the average hiring process takes 56 days. A fortnight of silence after your first round is ordinary, not a signal.

2. What happens in the first fifteen minutes

60% of interviewers form their decision within the first 15 minutes. That is not fair, but it is measurable, and you can prepare for it.

Those minutes are almost always taken up by three things: how you arrive and settle, "tell me about yourself", and "why this role". Which means you can rehearse the exact material that carries most of the weight.

Tell me about yourself. This is not an invitation to narrate your life. Ninety seconds, three parts: where you are now, one concrete thing you have done that matches this job, and why you are sitting here. Write it out, say it aloud six times, then stop rehearsing so it does not sound recited.

Why this role. The wrong answer is about you, "I am looking for a new challenge". The right answer connects something specific about them to something specific about you. That requires research, which is the next section and the one that fails most often.

You cannot control that they decide in fifteen minutes. You can control that the fifteen minutes contains your best material rather than your warm up.

3. The research that prevents 47% of rejections

47% of rejections are attributed to candidates being unable to answer what they know about the company. Nearly half. This is thirty minutes of work the night before.

What to actually find, in order of usefulness:

  • What they sell and to whom. Be able to say it in one plain sentence, without the marketing language from their homepage.
  • Something that changed recently. A new product, an office opening, a funding round, an acquisition, a change in the law that affects them. Companies House filings and their news page take ten minutes.
  • Who you would be working for. Look up the interviewer on LinkedIn. Not to flatter them, but so you know whether you are talking to a specialist or a generalist and can pitch accordingly.
  • Their competitors. Naming one, and saying what makes this company different, puts you above almost everyone else in the pile.
  • The job advert itself. Read it three times and mark the words that repeat. Those are the requirements they actually care about, and they will be tested.

4. STAR, and how to build three answers that cover everything

Competency questions, the "tell me about a time when" ones, are answered with STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. It works because it forces you to finish with an outcome, which is the part people skip.

  • Situation. One sentence of context. Where, when, what was going on.
  • Task. What specifically was your responsibility. Not the team's, yours.
  • Action. What you did, step by step. This should be the longest part.
  • Result. What happened, with a number wherever one exists, and what you would do differently.

The efficient way to prepare. Do not write twenty answers. Write three strong stories and learn them properly: one where you solved a hard problem, one where you handled a difficult person, and one where something went wrong and you dealt with it. Between them, those three cover the overwhelming majority of competency questions, and you reframe the same story to fit the question you get.

A warning worth taking seriously: some employers now run AI assisted screening on video interviews that analyses structure and delivery. Whether or not you like that, a clearly structured STAR answer scores better than a rambling one under both human and machine assessment.

5. The questions you ask, which are marked

Not asking good questions is the single most common candidate mistake, at 38%. Most people treat the end of the interview as the bit after the assessment. It is part of the assessment.

Prepare five, because two or three will get answered during the conversation.

  • What does good look like in this role after six months?
  • What is the biggest challenge facing the team right now?
  • Why is this role open? Growth, replacement, or restructure. The answer tells you a lot.
  • How is performance reviewed here, and how often?
  • What would you change about how the team works if you could?

Do not ask anything answered on their website, and do not lead with holiday, hours or working from home in a first interview. Those are fair questions and they belong at offer stage, when you have leverage.

6. Talking about money without losing the offer

If asked what you are looking for before you have an offer, you have two decent options. Give a researched range with a reason: "Based on similar roles in this area I would expect somewhere between £32,000 and £36,000, though I am more interested in the fit." Or redirect once: "I would rather understand the role properly first, but what range has been budgeted?"

Two things worth knowing. UK employers cannot ask about your salary history as a screening tool in the way some countries allow, and you are not obliged to volunteer it. And the moment for the real negotiation is after "we would like to offer you the role", not before.

7. A worked preparation session, ninety minutes

Leila, 34, interviewing for an operations manager role in Cardiff on Thursday. She has one evening.

0 to 20 minutes: the company. Their site, their news page, one Companies House filing. She learns they opened a second site in Bristol four months ago. That single fact will appear in three of her answers.

20 to 35 minutes: the advert. Read three times. The words that repeat are "process improvement", "stakeholder", and "cost control". Those are the three things that will be tested.

35 to 65 minutes: three STAR stories. One on a process she rebuilt, with the number: handling time down from 9 minutes to 5. One on a supplier dispute she handled. One on a project that overran and how she recovered it. Written out in bullets, not scripts.

65 to 80 minutes: the opening ninety seconds. Where she is now, the process improvement story in one line, and why this company, which is the Bristol expansion and what that means for operations.

80 to 90 minutes: five questions, and the practical bits. Route, arrival time, who to ask for, and a charged phone.

What ninety minutes bought her. The 47% rejection risk, gone. The 38% mistake, gone. Her strongest material now sits inside the fifteen minutes where 60% of the decision is made, rather than emerging at minute forty when the interviewer has already made their mind up.

Mistakes that cost UK candidates offers

  • Talking too much, 33%. Answer, then stop. If they want more they will ask. Silence after your answer is their thinking time, not your cue to keep going.
  • Appearing disinterested, 32%. This is rarely genuine disinterest, it is usually nerves reading as flatness. Asking questions of your own is the most reliable fix.
  • Criticising a previous employer. Even when it is deserved. Say what you are moving towards, not what you are escaping.
  • No examples. "I am good at problem solving" is worth nothing. The story with a number is the evidence.
  • Not testing the video setup. For a remote interview, join five minutes early on the actual platform. Camera at eye level, light in front of you rather than behind.
  • Skipping the follow up. A short thank you email the same day, referencing one specific thing discussed, costs five minutes and most candidates do not send one.

The honest verdict

Interview preparation has poor returns beyond a point, and most people put their effort in the wrong place. Rehearsing forty answers is worse than knowing three well. Learning the company properly, and having your best material ready for the first fifteen minutes, is where the evidence says the outcome is decided.

Do the ninety minutes. Company research, the repeated words in the advert, three STAR stories, a ninety second opener, five questions. That covers the 47% rejection cause and the 38% mistake, which between them account for more failures than any weakness in your experience.

And when it goes badly, remember the base rate. Two to three rounds, 56 days, hundreds of applicants. Several good candidates lose to one slightly better fit, and that is not a verdict on you.

Frequently asked

What should I wear to a UK interview? One notch smarter than the team's daily dress. If you cannot tell, smart trousers and a plain shirt or blouse is never wrong. Ask the recruiter, they will tell you.

Should I take notes in the interview? Yes, and take a printed copy of your own CV and the advert. It reads as prepared, not as unable to remember.

Is it acceptable to ask why I did not get it? Yes, once, briefly and without arguing. Roughly half of employers will give you something useful, and it costs nothing to ask.

How long should I wait before chasing? They usually tell you. If not, a week after the stated date is reasonable. With a 56 day average process, chasing after three days works against you.
