82 Days Left, and 3.6 Million Company Owners Still Owe Companies House a Filing They Have Never Heard Of

Companies House published its latest verification figures on 30 July. Directors are just over halfway there. The people who own those same companies are miles behind, and the gap is not laziness. It is a second filing that the confirmation statement does not cover, on a fourteen day window keyed to something as arbitrary as your birthday.

1. What the official numbers actually show

Most coverage quotes a round number, usually "over a million people have verified". Companies House publishes something far more useful and almost nobody reads it: a quarterly release breaking compliance down by appointment type. The April to June 2026 release gives the position as at 30 June.

Directors: 4,710,086 appointments verified, 55.33%. Individual PSCs: 2,571,827, or 41.86%. LLP members: 82,686, or 49.47%. Note the wording: appointments, not people. One person sitting as director and PSC of two companies counts as four.

Work backwards and roughly 3.80 million director appointments and 3.57 million PSC appointments were still unverified at the end of June. The run rate is what matters. PSC verifications rose 1,193,810 in the 91 days to 30 June, about 13,100 a day. Clearing the remainder in the 141 days to 18 November needs roughly 25,300 a day, close to double what was achieved. Directors need about 26,970 a day against 21,677 achieved, a far smaller stretch. The two groups are not in the same position, and the reason is structural.

2. Why PSCs are so far behind: it is two filings, not one

Verification has two steps that people routinely collapse into one. First you prove who you are and receive an eleven character personal code. Second you connect that code to each role you hold. The first is a one off. The second is per role, per company.

For directors, step two is forced. Your company cannot file its confirmation statement unless every director is verified and their code supplied. Miss it and the filing is blocked, which you notice immediately. For PSCs, nothing is forced. The code goes in through a separate service, "Provide identity verification details for a PSC", on its own clock. Nothing stops you, nothing bounces. You quietly fall out of compliance.

That is the whole explanation for the 13.5 percentage point gap. Not that shareholders care less, but that one path has a hard gate and the other does not. The people most exposed are the ones this site usually writes for: the sole director who is also sole shareholder, filed the confirmation statement, saw it accepted, and reasonably concluded they were finished.

3. Three traps in the timing

Trap one: your deadline is probably not 18 November. Companies House guidance for existing directors names no date at all. It says you provide your code "as part of your company's next confirmation statement". If yours falls due in September, that is your deadline. The November backstop exists because every company will have hit a confirmation statement by then, not because it is a date to aim at.

Trap two: filing early does not move the PSC window. If you are both director and PSC of the same company, your PSC window is fourteen days starting the day after the confirmation statement date. Companies House states directly that filing the confirmation statement early does not change those dates. File six weeks ahead, tick it off, and you sail straight past a window that opens later.

Trap three: if you are a PSC but not a director of that company, your window is your birthday. You must provide your code within the first fourteen days of your birth month. Companies House's own example: date of birth 22 January, window opens 1 January. Someone born in December has until 14 December 2026, which sits after the November backstop. Your actual dates are on the company's public register entry.

4. Worked example: Nadia in Stevenage

Nadia Rahman is sole director and sole shareholder of a small design company. Her confirmation statement date is 12 September 2026. Being organised, she verifies through GOV.UK One Login in late August, gets her code, and files the confirmation statement on 1 September, eleven days early, paying the £50 online fee. She marks it done.

She is not done. As a PSC her second window runs 13 to 26 September 2026, the fourteen days beginning the day after her confirmation statement date. Filing early did not move it. Nothing will block her, and the first she is likely to hear is a letter to her home address, because Companies House sends PSC non-compliance letters to the residential address rather than the company's.

What that letter costs depends entirely on what she does next. A warning notice gives at least 28 days, and Companies House states that if you take the required action within 28 days beginning the day after the notice date, you will not receive a financial penalty. Act on it and the bill is nil. Ignore it and a first minor offence is £250, a second £500, a third £750, a fourth or later £1,000. Serious and very serious offences start at £500 and £750.

The gap between the good outcome and the bad one is not £250 of skill. It is opening one envelope.

5. What to do this week, and what it costs

Verifying costs nothing. GOV.UK One Login routes you to an app, online security questions, or a Post Office visit depending on your documents. The Post Office route is free too: enter your photo ID details online, download the customer letter, then take that letter and your original photo ID, not a photocopy or a phone picture, to any participating branch. The letter expires, usually around fifteen days from the confirmation email, and missing that date means starting the identity check again. An accountant or solicitor registered as an Authorised Corporate Service Provider can do it for you and may charge, which buys convenience rather than compliance.

Three things worth doing today:

  1. Look up your company on the free Companies House register and read your confirmation statement date. That, not November, is your working deadline.
  2. Check whether you are recorded as a PSC as well as a director. Own more than 25% of the shares or voting rights and you almost certainly are. Then find your fourteen day window on the register entry.
  3. Director of more than one company? Repeat both steps for each. One personal code, a separate connection filing for every role.

The honest verdict

There is nothing to buy here. Verification is free and takes minutes for most people. If someone emails offering to "protect your director status" for a fee, that is the fee talking.

What is worth twenty minutes is the check. The real risk is not the £250, it is that doing most of the job feels exactly like doing all of it. A confirmation statement that goes through cleanly is evidence your director role is compliant, nothing more. If you also own the company, that is one box of two.

And be fair to the numbers. 55% of directors with five months left is not a disaster. The PSC figure at 41.86%, needing close to double the achieved run rate, is what will decide whether November 2026 is uneventful or messy.

Frequently asked

I am a sole trader, does any of this apply to me? No. Companies House only touches limited companies, LLPs and similar registered entities. Never incorporated, nothing here for you. Your equivalent deadline is HMRC's, a different system entirely.

Do I have to verify again for each company? No. You verify once and get one personal code, and Companies House says not to verify again unless told to. But you connect that code to every role, at every company, separately.

What if I genuinely could not do it in time? Make a representation in writing within the window the notice gives. Companies House says it is likely to accept where circumstances were exceptional and tied to the due date, and that accepted representations usually pause enforcement for up to two months. The registrar can also extend a PSC due date by up to fourteen days.

What happens if I ignore it entirely? Continuing to act as a director without verification is an offence under section 167M of the Companies Act 2006, and Companies House says the company may be in breach too. Its stated routes are financial penalties, referral to the Insolvency Service and prosecution. You would also be unable to register a new company or be appointed to an existing one.
