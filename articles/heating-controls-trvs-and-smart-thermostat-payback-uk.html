1. What the £100 a year is, and what it is not

The Energy Saving Trust's heating controls page, updated 10 February 2026 and read again on 5 September 2026, gives one saving and two costs. Installing heating controls could save you £100 a year in Great Britain and £110 in Northern Ireland. Adding a programmer, thermostat and TRVs on all radiators costs around £600 if you have no controls at all. Adding TRVs to all your radiators costs around £370 if you already have a programmer and thermostat.

A home with no controls, meaning a boiler on a switch and radiators on plain valves, spends £600 and saves £100: six years. A home that already has a programmer and thermostat spends £370 on TRVs and gets some part of the £100, because the other two controls were already delivering the rest. The Energy Saving Trust does not publish the split; what we can say is which control does what.

The programmer decides when the heating runs. Without one the boiler runs whenever the switch is on, including all night and all day while the house is empty. The room thermostat decides how warm the house gets before the boiler stops; without one the boiler runs until you turn it off, and the house overheats. Thermostatic radiator valves decide how warm each room gets, so the spare bedroom does not have to be as warm as the living room. A modern system should have all three, and building regulations require a boiler interlock, meaning the programmer and thermostats together switch the boiler off when nothing needs heat, on any new or replacement system.

The number that puts all of this in proportion is on the same page: turning a room thermostat down by one degree, from 22 to 21, saves £120 a year in Great Britain. That is more than the £100 from installing every control in the article. A thermostat is worth having because it is the control you turn down; the saving is in the setting, and the hardware is what makes the setting possible.

3. The smart thermostat question, answered by what is on your wall

A smart heating control, in the Energy Saving Trust's words, can do everything conventional controls do, connected to the internet with more functionality: app control, learning your habits, and geo-fencing that turns the heating off when your phone leaves the house. Then the sentence that matters: whether smart heating controls will save you money will depend on how you currently control your heating.

Translate that into the two situations. No programmer, no thermostat, or a 1990s dial that nobody has touched: a smart thermostat is a programmable room thermostat with an app, it delivers the programmer and thermostat share of the £100, and at £150 to £250 it pays back in two to four years. Buy it, or buy a plain programmable thermostat for half the price and the same saving. A working programmer and a thermostat you already set sensibly: the smart box is competing for what is left, which is the difference between your schedule and a schedule that adapts to you. Our smart home article looked at the trial evidence and found typical UK homes save 8 to 15 per cent of heating from smart controls, against the 30 per cent in the adverts. On a typical gas heating bill under the October 2026 cap that 8 to 15 per cent is roughly £50 to £100 for a household that was running the heating loosely, and close to nothing for one that already times it tightly and turns it down.

The honest test: do you come home to a house you have heated all afternoon for nobody? If yes, geo-fencing earns £40 or more a year and the box pays in four or five. If no, the payback is never and the app is a convenience, not a saving.

One more thing: a smart thermostat controls the boiler, not the radiators. For room-by-room control you need TRVs, or smart TRVs at £40 to £70 a radiator, and that is a separate purchase.

4. The settings that save more than the hardware

The room thermostat at the lowest comfortable temperature, which the Energy Saving Trust puts between 18 and 21 for most people. Each degree is worth £120 a year at the top of that range. Do not turn it up when it is cold outside; the house heats to the set temperature regardless, it just takes longer, so set the programmer to start earlier in winter instead.

The TRVs at the lowest number that keeps each room comfortable, with unused rooms low and doors closed. Never put a cover over a radiator with a TRV: the trapped warm air fools the valve into thinking the room is warm and it shuts the radiator down.

The boiler flow temperature down, on a combi with no hot water cylinder, to 60 or lower; the boiler condenses more and burns less gas for the same warmth. On a system with a cylinder, no lower than 65 so the cylinder reaches 60 and kills bacteria.

The cylinder thermostat, if you have one, at 60 to 65. Hotter is wasted, cooler is unsafe.

Those four settings are worth more than any control you could buy. If you have all four and they are set sensibly, keep your £370.

5. Fitting, installers and the renter's version

A programmer and a wired thermostat mean boiler control wiring: three quotes from Gas Safe or OFTEC registered installers. Fitting TRV bodies means draining the system and is a plumber's job. Replacing a head on an existing body is a hand-tightened collar; check for the standard M30 x 1.5 thread first, and buy an adapter for older Danfoss RA bodies.

Renting: TRV heads screw onto existing bodies without tools, need no permission and leave with you. A smart thermostat touches the landlord's boiler wiring and needs written consent.

6. Worked example: Meera in Leicester

Three-bed 1970s semi, gas combi boiler on a manual switch, one 1980s dial thermostat in the hall that reads 25 and does nothing, radiators on plain valves, out at work 8 to 6

Meera's heating is either on or off, and she turns it off at the boiler when she leaves for work and on when she gets home. The house is cold at 6pm and hot by 9.

What she is missing. A programmer that works, a thermostat that works, and any way to make the two bedrooms she does not use less warm than the living room. That is the Energy Saving Trust's no-controls case.

Option A, the full conventional set. Programmer, new room thermostat and TRVs on all eight radiators, installed by a Gas Safe plumber: the Energy Saving Trust's £600. Saving £100 a year. Payback six years.

Option B, smart thermostat plus TRVs. A smart thermostat replacing the dead dial and doing the programmer's job, about £220 fitted, plus the same TRVs at about £370: £590. The same £100, plus whatever geo-fencing adds on the days her shifts change, perhaps £20 to £40. Payback four to six years.

What decides it. Her shifts do change, so she takes option B. The first winter she sets the thermostat to 20, the two spare rooms to 2 on the TRV dial, and the flow temperature down to 60 on the combi. The one degree below her old habit is worth £120 a year on the Energy Saving Trust's figure, which is more than the hardware saves, and none of it was possible before the hardware existed.

7. The honest verdict

If your boiler runs off a switch and your radiators have no thermostatic valves, buy the full set: around £600 installed for £100 a year on the Energy Saving Trust's figures, six years, and you get the thermostat you can then turn down for another £120. If you already have a programmer and a thermostat, TRVs on the radiators in rooms you do not use are worth £370 only in a large house; in a small one, replace seized heads yourself for £10 to £20 each and stop there.

Buy a smart thermostat if it is replacing a programmer or thermostat you do not have, or if you routinely heat an empty house. Do not buy it to replace a working programmable thermostat that you already set properly; the saving the adverts promise is not there, and the app is a convenience, not a return.
