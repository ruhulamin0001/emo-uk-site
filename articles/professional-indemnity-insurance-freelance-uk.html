The Law Does Not Make You Buy This. Your Client’s Contract Does

Professional indemnity insurance sits in an odd place: compulsory in practice for many freelancers, compulsory in law for almost none. The thing that decides whether it is worth the money is not the premium. It is one word in the policy structure, and a six-year tail almost nobody budgets for.

The short version

  • Professional indemnity is not legally required for most freelancers. Unlike employers’ liability, no Act of Parliament makes you buy it. Two other things do: your professional body, if you have one, and the client contract in front of you.
  • It is claims-made, and that single word changes everything. Cover must be in force on the day the claim is made, not the day you did the work. Cancel the policy and last year’s work stops being covered.
  • That is why run-off cover exists. A breach of contract claim can be brought for six years under the Limitation Act 1980, so stopping work without run-off leaves a six-year tail exposed.
  • Price it against your own day rate, not against nothing. A £200 annual premium is under half a day at a £450 day rate. The break-even question is whether it saves you half a day of unbilled time a year.
  • The honest answer for some freelancers is do not buy it yet. If nobody is contractually requiring it and your work cannot cause a client financial loss, the premium is buying very little.

1. What it covers, in one paragraph

Professional indemnity insurance pays when a client says your advice, design, code, drawing, figures or recommendation caused them a financial loss, and they want that loss back from you. It covers the compensation and, usually more importantly, the cost of defending the allegation even when the allegation is wrong.

That last part is the one people undervalue. Most professional indemnity claims that go anywhere do not end in a payout. They end in months of correspondence, a solicitor’s time, and a negotiated walk-away. The policy pays for that process. You would be paying for it yourself out of billable hours.

2. What it does not cover

Freelancers routinely buy professional indemnity and assume it is general business insurance. It is not, and the gaps are specific.

  • Injury to a member of the public or damage to their property. That is public liability, an entirely separate policy. A client tripping over your laptop bag is not a professional indemnity matter.
  • Injury to your own employees. That is employers’ liability, which unlike the other two is compulsory by law.
  • Your own equipment. A stolen laptop is a contents or portable equipment policy.
  • Deliberate acts. Every policy excludes dishonesty and fraud, for obvious reasons.
  • Work done outside the described activity. If your policy says copywriting and you gave financial advice, expect an argument.

3. The word that decides everything: claims-made

Almost all professional indemnity in the UK is written on a claims-made basis. The policy responds to claims made against you while the policy is in force, regardless of when the work was done, subject to a retroactive date.

Compare that with motor or buildings insurance, which is written on an occurrence basis: the policy that was in force when the crash happened is the policy that pays, even if you cancelled it afterwards.

The consequence for a freelancer is blunt. If you let the policy lapse, you are not covered for work you did while insured. The cover you paid for in 2024 does nothing for a complaint that arrives in 2027.

Professional indemnity is not a record of the years you were protected. It is a live subscription, and the protection stops the day you stop paying, including for work already finished and invoiced.

Two things follow from that.

Retroactive date. A new policy usually covers past work back to a stated date, often the date you first took out continuous cover. If you switch insurer, make sure the new policy carries your original retroactive date across. If it is reset to today, every project you have ever delivered becomes uninsured.

Run-off cover. When you stop trading, retire, or take a permanent job, the exposure does not stop with you. Under the Limitation Act 1980, a claim for breach of contract can generally be brought within six years of the breach. Run-off cover keeps a claims-made policy alive for that tail without you doing any new work. It is usually cheaper than a full policy and it is the part almost nobody budgets for.

5. The arithmetic: is it worth it on your rate?

Here is a way to price the decision that does not depend on any premium figure going stale.

Express the premium as a fraction of your own day rate. If you charge £450 a day, a £200 annual premium is 0.44 of a day. A £400 premium is 0.89 of a day.

Now ask the break-even question directly: does having the policy save you at least half a day of unbilled time a year? Not in claims paid. In time. One serious client complaint, handled without insurance, absorbs days of reading, drafting and worrying that you cannot invoice for.

For most freelancers whose work can plausibly cause a client a financial loss, the answer to that question is obviously yes, and the decision stops being interesting. The interesting case is the freelancer for whom the answer is no.

Worked example: Isla in Dumfries

Isla is a freelance illustrator. She draws, delivers files, invoices, and moves on. Her day rate is £320. A quoted policy would cost her £190 a year, which is 0.59 of a day.

The case for buying. Two of her four regular clients are agencies whose standard contract requires £1m of professional indemnity. She cannot sign those contracts without it. That alone settles the question: the policy is a condition of roughly 60% of her income, so its real cost is zero and its real value is the work.

The case if those clauses did not exist. Suppose all her clients were small direct businesses with no contract requirement. What financial loss could a late or disliked illustration cause? Usually a reprint bill or a missed campaign date, which are commercial arguments settled by redoing the work. On that profile, £190 a year buys cover for a scenario that is rare and small.

The number that changes it. The year she takes a brand identity commission and the client builds signage, packaging and a shopfront on her artwork, the possible loss stops being a reprint bill. That is the year she needs the policy, and it needs to have been in force already, because the cover is claims-made.

Her decision. She keeps the policy running continuously, because the premium is 0.59 of a day and a gap in cover would reset her retroactive date and orphan four years of past work. Continuity is worth more than the annual saving from cancelling in a quiet year.

6. Frequently asked

Is professional indemnity a legal requirement? Not in general law. It becomes compulsory when a regulator requires it for your profession, or when a client contract requires it. Employers’ liability is the one that is required by statute, and only if you have employees.

Can I claim the premium as a business expense? Business insurance taken out wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the trade is normally an allowable expense. That reduces the real cost by your marginal rate, so a £200 premium costs a basic rate sole trader roughly £140 after relief.

What limit do I need? The honest answer is whatever your contracts specify, and £1m is the figure most commonly written into UK agency and public sector contracts. Buying below your contract requirement is buying a policy that does not let you sign the work.

Do I still need it once I stop freelancing? For the tail period, yes, through run-off cover. A claim can arrive years after the last invoice, and a cancelled claims-made policy will not answer it.

7. The honest verdict

Buy professional indemnity if a client contract or a regulator requires it, or if a mistake in your work could cost a client real money rather than an inconvenience. On those facts the premium is a rounding error against a day rate, and the decision is not close.

Do not buy it yet if you are early, your clients are small and direct, no contract asks for it, and the worst realistic outcome of a bad job is redoing the job. Put the money into the work and buy cover the month a contract first demands it.

Whichever you choose, understand the claims-made point. Once you start, the expensive mistake is not the premium. It is a gap in cover, or a switch that quietly resets your retroactive date.
