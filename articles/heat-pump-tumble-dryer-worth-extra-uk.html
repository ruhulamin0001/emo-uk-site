1. The two label figures, and why they are comparable

Every tumble dryer sold here carries an energy label with a weighted annual energy consumption figure, calculated over the standard cotton programme at full and partial load, on the assumption of 160 cycles a year. It is the closest thing to a fair test that exists, because both machines were measured the same way.

ModelTypeWeighted annual energyPer cycle (ours)Connection ratingStandard cotton cycle
Hotpoint C HD C82 WWGD UK, 8 kgCondenser561.1 kWh3.51 kWh2,700 W142 minutes
Hotpoint C HSD 82M WW UK, 8 kgHeat pump265.0 kWh1.66 kWh900 W209 minutes

Those are the manufacturer's figures from its own spec pages, read on 3 September 2026. We chose two Hotpoint machines deliberately, so that brand, capacity and test method are all held constant and the only thing that changes is the technology. The condenser sits in class G on the rescaled label and the heat pump in class E, which tells you how hard the new scale is: a machine using less than half the energy of its sibling is still two grades off the top.

The energy price is the Ofgem cap unit rate for Direct Debit customers from 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p per kWh. If you are on a fixed tariff, substitute your own rate; everything below scales in proportion.

We are not quoting the purchase prices. They move week to week, and a stale price on a money page is worse than none. What we will do is assume a premium, and show you what happens to the payback if the premium is bigger or smaller than that.

The heat pump machine uses 47% of the energy of the condenser. That is the whole argument, and it does not depend on a single assumption of ours. The Energy Saving Trust's own estimate, for Northern Ireland at a 30.8p rate and 160 cycles, is a saving of about £85 a year against a top-rated condenser, which is consistent with our £78 at the lower GB rate.

Why is it so much lower? A condenser dryer heats air with a resistive element, passes it through the clothes, then cools it to condense the water out, and throws the heat away. A heat pump dryer runs a small refrigeration circuit that recovers the heat from the wet air and uses it again, so most of the energy goes round in a loop rather than out. That is also why it draws 900 W rather than 2,700 W, and why it takes longer.

3. The payback on the premium, at three usage levels

Payback is the extra you pay divided by the annual saving. We assume the heat pump model costs £200 more than the condenser. If your quotes show a bigger gap, the payback lengthens in proportion; if the heat pump model is on offer, it shortens.

For a family that dries most loads, the premium comes back inside two winters and the machine then saves well over £100 a year for the rest of its life. For a single person doing two loads a week it is four to six years, which is still inside a sensible lifespan for the machine but no longer an obvious win, particularly if the cheaper condenser would have done.

The number you need before you buy is therefore not the price. It is your own loads per year. Count a fortnight and multiply by 26.

4. Cycle time, warranty, and where the heat goes

An hour longer, every load. The standard cotton programme is 209 minutes on the heat pump machine and 142 on the condenser, both Hotpoint's figures. That is 67 extra minutes a load, or 178 hours a year at 160 loads. If you run the dryer in the evening and need the towels before bed, that is a real cost, and it is the reason some households buy a heat pump dryer and then use the faster programmes on it, which erode the saving. The energy figures above are for the standard programme; a quick programme on either machine uses more per kilogram.

Warranty and repair. Hotpoint gives one year on labour and ten years on parts across both machines, so the manufacturer is not treating the heat pump as the riskier product. Bosch is currently offering five years on its heat pump dryers bought between 1 April 2026 and 31 March 2027 if you register within three months. A heat pump dryer has a compressor and a sealed refrigerant circuit that a condenser does not, and out of warranty that is the expensive failure. Buy the longer warranty if you can get it for nothing, and register it.

Where the heat goes. A condenser dryer in a small utility room turns it into a sauna, and in winter that warmth is not entirely wasted, because it is heat you did not buy from the boiler. A heat pump dryer keeps most of its heat inside itself. The effect on the sum is small, a few pounds a winter in the condenser's favour, and it reverses in summer. We have left it out of the tables.

The room it lives in. Heat pump dryers dislike the cold. Hotpoint's model has a specific programme for use down to 0 degrees in a garage or outbuilding; older heat pump models often did not, and would stall or run for hours in a cold garage. If the dryer lives somewhere unheated, check the spec for a cold-room rating before you buy.

The heated airer. If you would genuinely dry most loads on a rack, neither dryer is the cheapest option, and we have done that sum separately in our heated airer versus tumble dryer article. This article is for the household that is going to own a dryer either way.

5. Worked example: Kwame in Croydon

Two-bed flat, two adults, no outdoor drying, condenser dryer has just died

Kwame and his partner do about four loads a week and dry nearly all of them, because the flat has no garden and the windows stream if they use a rack. That is roughly 200 loads a year. They are on a standard variable tariff.

Condenser replacement. 200 loads at 3.51 kWh is 702 kWh, £185 a year at 26.32p.

Heat pump replacement. 200 loads at 1.66 kWh is 332 kWh, £87 a year. Saving £98 a year.

Payback on a £200 premium: about 2 years. On a £300 premium, about 3 years. Over a ten-year life, the heat pump machine is roughly £700 to £800 cheaper to own in total, before any repair.

The cost they had not priced. 200 loads at an extra 67 minutes is 223 hours a year of dryer time. They run it after dinner, so the practical change is that towels go in at 7pm and come out at half past ten rather than half past nine. They decide that is fine on weekdays and buy the heat pump model. If they had needed two loads dried back to back every evening, the answer might have been different.

6. The honest verdict

If you dry three or more loads a week, buy the heat pump dryer. On the manufacturer's own label figures it uses less than half the electricity of the condenser equivalent, the premium comes back in about two and a half years at 160 loads, and after that it is £78 a year or more in your pocket for as long as it lasts.

If you dry fewer than two loads a week, the saving is under £50 a year and the payback runs to four to six years. That is still likely to come good, but it is a marginal purchase, and a condenser on offer would be a defensible choice. Either way, count your loads before you shop, check the cold-room rating if it lives in a garage, and register the warranty.

Frequently asked

How much does a heat pump tumble dryer cost to run? The Hotpoint 8 kg model is rated at 265 kWh a year on the 160-cycle label basis, which is about £70 a year or 44p a load at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p.

How much cheaper is a heat pump dryer than a condenser? On like-for-like manufacturer figures, 296 kWh a year, about £78 at 160 loads. The Energy Saving Trust's estimate is £85 a year at Northern Ireland's higher rate.

Do heat pump dryers take longer? Yes. The standard cotton cycle is 209 minutes against 142 for the condenser on the two Hotpoint models compared, an extra 67 minutes a load.

Can I put a heat pump dryer in a garage? Only if the model is rated for it. The Hotpoint model has a cold-room programme down to 0 degrees; many older heat pump models were not designed for unheated rooms. Check the spec sheet.
