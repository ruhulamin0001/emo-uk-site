A Water Butt Takes 12 Years To Pay Back

The rain is free, there is a great deal of it, and the butt costs almost nothing. So why is this one of the slowest paybacks on the whole site? Because the constraint is not the supply. It is that tap water is remarkably cheap and a garden does not drink very much.

The short version

  • Britain gets 1,163mm of rain a year on the Met Office 1991 to 2020 average. A 10 square metre shed roof could collect nearly 10 cubic metres of it. Supply is not the problem.
  • The problem is demand. A normal garden, watered through a normal summer, uses somewhere around 1.5 cubic metres of water that a butt could have supplied instead.
  • On a metered supply that is worth £6.26 a year. A £75 butt, stand and diverter kit therefore pays back in about 12 years.
  • On an unmetered supply it is worth exactly nothing, because your bill is set by the property's rateable value and using less changes it by zero.
  • Buy one anyway, but for the right reason. Butts are exempt from hosepipe bans, rainwater is better for acid loving plants, and many water companies sell them subsidised at around half price, which halves the payback too.

1. How much rain a roof actually catches

The Met Office puts UK average annual rainfall at 1,163mm across the 1991 to 2020 period. That is an average across the whole country, and it hides an enormous range: the west of Scotland and Cumbria get several times what Essex and Cambridgeshire get. Substitute your own region if you know it, and expect the drier south east to be nearer half the national figure.

Collection is simple arithmetic. Roof area in square metres, times rainfall in metres, times a runoff efficiency of about 0.85 for losses in the gutter and to evaporation.

  • A 10 m² shed or garage roof: 10 × 1.163 × 0.85 = 9.89 cubic metres a year
  • Half a 60 m² house roof feeding one downpipe: 29.66 cubic metres a year

Both of those are far more than any garden needs. A standard 210 litre butt is 0.21 cubic metres, so a shed roof fills it forty seven times over in a year.

Which sounds like the case is made, and it is not, because the butt can only hold 210 litres at a time and most of that 9.89 cubic metres arrives in the months when nobody is watering anything. In November the butt fills once and then overflows for the rest of the month. The rain is not the constraint. It never was.

2. What a garden actually drinks, which is the real limit

Here is the sum almost nobody does before buying a butt.

A typical small to medium garden with beds, pots and a few vegetables gets watered perhaps twice a week from May to September, which is about 40 sessions. Two full ten litre cans a session is 80 litres, and a fair number of people do rather less than that.

40 sessions × 80 litres = 3.2 cubic metres a year at the enthusiastic end. But a 210 litre butt cannot supply all of it, because in a genuinely dry July the butt empties in ten days and does not refill. Across a normal British summer, with dry spells and wet ones, a single butt realistically displaces 1 to 2 cubic metres of tap water. We use 1.5.

Now the value of that water. Severn Trent's published metered charges for 2026/27 are £2.5503 per cubic metre for water supply and £1.6255 per cubic metre for used water, together £4.1758 per cubic metre. Most companies charge the used water element on your measured supply volume, so a litre you do not draw saves you both charges. Some allow deductions where water genuinely does not return to the sewer, so check your own company's terms, and if in doubt use the supply figure alone as the cautious number.

1.5 cubic metres × £4.1758 = £6.26 a year.

That is the whole saving. Six pounds and twenty six pence. It is a genuinely tiny number, and it is small for a reason worth understanding: mains water in Britain costs about four tenths of a penny per litre. It is one of the cheapest things you buy. Which is exactly why the arithmetic on water saving devices is always thin, and why our page on low flow shower heads only works because that device saves hot water, where the energy to heat it costs several times more than the water itself.

Two things to take from that table. Buying a bigger butt makes the payback worse, not better, because the extra capacity costs money and only adds water in the shoulder weeks. And the subsidised route is the only one that gets this inside a sensible timeframe. Most water companies in England and Wales sell butts to their own customers at a substantial discount, and some give them away. Check yours before buying anything at full price, because it roughly halves the payback in one phone call.

And note the unmetered row. It is the same trap that appears everywhere in water saving: if you are not billed by volume, saving volume is worth nothing. If you have more bedrooms than people, it is worth checking whether a water meter would save you money in its own right, and that decision is worth far more than the butt.

4. The three reasons that are not about money

We would still tell most gardeners to get one, and here is the honest case that does not pretend to be a payback.

Hosepipe bans. When a temporary use ban is in force you cannot use a hosepipe on the garden, but water from a butt is your own and is not covered. In a drought summer a full butt is the difference between a garden that survives and one that does not, and no arithmetic captures what that is worth to somebody who has spent five years on a border.

Rainwater is better for some plants. Mains water in hard water areas is alkaline and full of calcium. Rhododendrons, camellias, azaleas, blueberries and most ericaceous plants dislike it, and it visibly marks the leaves of houseplants. Rainwater is soft and slightly acidic and they prefer it. Gardeners who grow these will tell you this is the real reason to have a butt and they are right.

Surface water and flooding. Every litre in a butt is a litre not going into the drain during a downpour. On its own that is trivial. Across a street it is not, and some water companies now offer rebates or reduced surface water drainage charges for properties that manage their own runoff. Worth asking about.

5. Worked example: Rosie in Neath

Rosie has a metered supply, a small back garden with a greenhouse, and a 12 square metre garage roof with one downpipe. South Wales is considerably wetter than the national average, so her roof is comfortably capable of filling anything she puts under it.

She prices a 210 litre butt, stand and rainwater diverter at £72 from a garden centre. Then she checks her water company's website and finds the same size butt offered to customers at £32, delivered.

She measures her actual watering for one season rather than guessing: a watering can holds 9 litres, and she logs 71 cans between May and September. That is 639 litres, or 0.64 cubic metres, which is well under our 1.5 estimate and a useful reminder that most people water less than they think.

At £4.1758 per cubic metre her saving is £2.67 a year.

£32 divided by £2.67 is 12.0 years. On the garden centre butt it would have been 27 years.

She buys the subsidised one, and she is glad she did the following August when a temporary use ban comes in and the butt keeps her tomatoes alive. The payback never really mattered. What mattered was not paying £72 for something available at £32, and that is the finding worth taking away.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy a water butt to save money. It saves about £6 a year and takes over a decade to pay for itself. We would rather say that plainly than dress up six pounds as a saving.

If you are unmetered, it saves you nothing at all, and no amount of collecting will change a bill that is set by your property's rateable value.

Check your water company's website before buying one at retail price. Most sell them subsidised to their own customers, often at around half, and that single check is worth more than the choice of butt.

And buy one anyway if you garden seriously. Hosepipe ban exemption, better water for acid loving plants, and a full butt in August are all worth having. Just call it gardening, not saving.

If you are buying

Your water company first, always. If you are buying retail, the parts that actually matter are a proper rainwater diverter that fits your downpipe diameter, a stand tall enough to get a watering can under the tap, and a lid that seals, because an open butt breeds mosquitoes and collects leaves. A linking kit lets you add a second butt later without new plumbing.

Frequently asked

Can I use butt water in the house? Not for drinking, washing or anything touching food, and not plumbed into the house without a great deal of care, because rainwater off a roof carries bird droppings, debris and whatever has been on the tiles. Proper rainwater harvesting systems that flush toilets exist, need filtration and a separate pipework colour code, and cost thousands. That is a different product with a different and considerably worse payback.

Is rainwater safe for a vegetable patch? Generally yes and it is what most allotment holders use, with the sensible precaution of watering the soil rather than the leaves of anything you eat raw, and not using water that has stood stagnant for months in a warm butt. Keep the lid on and the water moving through the season.

Does a water butt freeze and split? It can. Leave a butt full through a hard winter and expanding ice will bulge or crack the plastic and can shear the tap. Drain it down to about half in late autumn, or at minimum open the tap so the water level cannot reach the point where the tap is fixed.
