You Can Ask From Day One. The Eight Reasons They Can Say No Are The Whole Game.

Every employee in Great Britain has the legal right to request flexible working from their first day in a job. What almost nobody explains is that this is a right to ask, not a right to receive, and the eight business reasons an employer may use to refuse are set out in law. If your request does not engage with those eight, you are writing into the wind.

The short version

  • It is a day-one right. Employees can make a statutory application from their first day in a job. No qualifying period.
  • You can request four things: the number of hours you work, when you start or finish, the days you work, and where you work.
  • Two applications in any 12-month period. That is the limit, so a badly aimed first request costs you half your year's allowance.
  • The employer must decide within 2 months of the request, unless you agree a longer period, and must discuss it with you before refusing.
  • There are exactly eight lawful reasons to refuse, listed in full below. An employer does not have to prove one; they have to give one.
  • A tribunal reviews the process, not the decision. The claim is that the request was not handled in a reasonable manner, not that the answer was wrong.
  • Northern Ireland has different rules. This covers England, Scotland and Wales.

1. What the right actually is, and what it is not

All employees have the legal right to request flexible working, and they can do it from their first day in a job. GOV.UK calls this "making a statutory application", and the word statutory is doing real work: it triggers a legal process with deadlines attached, rather than a chat with your manager.

You can ask to change four things:

  • the number of hours you work
  • when you start or finish work
  • the days you work
  • where you work

That last one is the reason most people are reading this in 2026, and it is worth noting that "where you work" is on the statutory list in exactly the same way as the other three. A request to work from home two days a week is not a favour you are asking for. It is a statutory application.

Now the part that gets skipped. This is a right to ask, not a right to receive. The employer's legal duties are to deal with the request in a "reasonable manner", to discuss it with you before refusing, and to decide within two months. They are not obliged to agree, and if they refuse for one of the eight reasons in the next section, they have complied with the law.

GOV.UK gives examples of what handling a request in a reasonable manner looks like: assessing the advantages and disadvantages of the application, discussing possible alternatives, and offering an appeal process. Acas publishes a Code of Practice on flexible working requests that fleshes this out.

2. The eight lawful reasons for refusal

This is the list, in full, exactly as GOV.UK sets it out. An employer can refuse an application for any of the following:

  1. extra costs that will damage the business
  2. the work cannot be reorganised among other staff
  3. people cannot be recruited to do the work
  4. flexible working will affect quality
  5. flexible working will affect performance
  6. the business will not be able to meet customer demand
  7. there is a lack of work to do during the proposed working times
  8. the business is planning changes to the workforce

Three observations that change how you should write your request.

The list is broad. Reasons four and five, quality and performance, are close to unfalsifiable in advance. An employer who says "we think this will affect performance" has given a lawful reason. This is why treating the request as a negotiation rather than an entitlement is the realistic posture.

The employer must give a reason, not prove it. There is no statutory requirement to produce evidence that the reason is correct. What the law requires is that the process was reasonable and that the reason given is one of the eight.

Every one of the eight is about the business, not about you. None of them refer to your circumstances, your length of service or how much your manager likes the idea. That is the single most useful thing to know before writing, and it is the basis of section 4.

3. The clock, the limit and the paperwork

The process has hard edges and they are worth knowing precisely.

RuleWhat it means
Day-one rightYou can apply from your first day. No qualifying service period.
Two applications per 12 monthsYou get two statutory applications in any twelve-month period. Spend them carefully.
Decision within 2 monthsThe employer must discuss the request and decide within two months, or longer if you agree to an extension.
Discussion before refusalThe employer must discuss the request with you before they can refuse it.
Contract change within 28 daysIf agreed, the contract should be changed as soon as possible and no later than 28 days after approval.
Two missed meetingsIf you miss two meetings about the application or an appeal without good reason, the employer can treat the request as withdrawn, though they must tell you they are doing so.

Your application has to be in writing, by email or letter, and it must include four things:

  • the date of the request
  • a statement that this is a statutory request for flexible working
  • details of how you want to work flexibly and when you want to start
  • a statement saying if and when you have made a previous application, including its date

Miss the words "statutory request" and you risk your letter being treated as an informal chat with none of the deadlines attached. It is one sentence and it is the difference between a legal process and a conversation.

Acas publishes a free flexible working request letter template, and employers may ask you to use a standard form instead.

4. How to write a request that engages the real test

Most rejected requests fail for the same reason: they explain why the change would help the employee, in a process where the only lawful grounds for refusal are about the employer.

Your childcare arrangements, your commute and your energy levels are all real and they may well persuade a decent manager. They are not what the legal test turns on. So write for both audiences: the human, and the eight reasons.

The practical method is to work backwards through the list and pre-empt the ones that could plausibly apply to your job.

Worked example: Rhian in Cardiff

Rhian works in a customer support team, five days a week in the office, and wants to move to three days at home and two in. Her first instinct is to write about the 90-minute round trip and her son's school pick-up.

Instead she writes about the eight reasons.

Customer demand and cover (reasons two and six): she names the two colleagues whose office days she would still overlap with, and proposes Tuesday and Thursday in the office because those are the team's busiest walk-in days.

Quality and performance (reasons four and five): she proposes a three-month trial with the same response-time and resolution metrics the team already reports, and says in writing that she is happy for the arrangement to end if those metrics move.

Extra costs (reason one): she confirms she already has a working setup at home and is not asking for equipment.

Then she adds two sentences about the school run, because her manager is a person, not a statute.

Why this works. She has not made refusal unlawful, because she cannot. What she has done is make each of the plausible reasons harder to state with a straight face, and given her manager a low-risk version to say yes to. A trial period is the single most effective element, because it converts a permanent decision into a reversible one.

She also uses her first of two applications this year on a proposal she has thought about, rather than firing off a vague one in frustration and having only one left.

5. If you are refused

Take the refusal in writing, with the reason stated. You are entitled to know which of the eight grounds is being relied on.

Appeal internally first. Offering an appeal is listed by GOV.UK as an example of handling a request reasonably, so most employers will have one. An appeal is also your chance to respond to the specific reason given, which you could not do before you knew it.

Consider a modified second request. You have two applications per twelve months. If the refusal was based on customer demand on Mondays, a second application that keeps Mondays in the office is a different proposition, not a repeat.

Understand what a tribunal actually decides. If an employer does not handle a request in a reasonable manner, you can take them to an employment tribunal. Note the wording: the claim is about the handling. A tribunal is not there to decide whether you should have been allowed to work from home. It is there to decide whether the process was reasonable, whether a reason from the statutory list was given, and whether the deadlines were met.

A separate and stronger route may exist. If your request relates to a disability, a reasonable adjustment claim under the Equality Act is a different legal question with a different test, and it is not limited to the eight reasons. The same is true if a refusal falls harder on one group than another. Those are areas to take proper advice on rather than rely on a general guide.

6. The honest verdict

The flexible working right is genuinely useful and genuinely limited, and most of the disappointment around it comes from people expecting the second thing to be the first. You can ask from day one. You cannot compel.

The practical advice is short. Put "statutory request" in the letter so the two-month clock starts. Write the request against the eight business reasons rather than against your own circumstances, because those eight are the only lawful grounds for saying no. Propose a trial period, because a reversible yes is far easier to give than a permanent one. And remember you only get two applications a year, so do not waste the first one.

If you are refused and the reason is one of the eight, that is the system working as designed rather than a wrong you can appeal on the merits. The tribunal route is about process, not about the answer.

Frequently asked

Does this apply to agency workers and the self-employed? The statutory right applies to employees. Workers and the genuinely self-employed are not covered, and employment status is decided by the reality of the relationship rather than the label on the contract. If you are unsure, GOV.UK has a separate employment status guide.

Can my employer change their mind after agreeing? Once agreed, the change is a change to your contract, and GOV.UK says the contract should be updated no later than 28 days after approval. Reversing it later is a contract variation, which normally needs your agreement. This is exactly why getting the outcome in writing matters.

Is a trial period allowed? Yes, and it is often the most productive route. It is not a statutory concept, so agree the terms in writing: how long, what is being measured, and what happens at the end. Be clear whether a successful trial becomes a permanent contractual change, because that is where disputes start.

What if I work in Northern Ireland? The rules are different. GOV.UK points to nidirect for Northern Ireland flexible working. Everything in this article covers England, Scotland and Wales.
