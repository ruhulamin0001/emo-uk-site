Your Agency Says £22 an Hour. The Umbrella Turns It Into £18.87.

Two identical jobs, both advertised at £22 an hour. One pays you through an umbrella company, the other through the agency's own payroll. The gap in your bank account after a year is £4,391. Here is exactly where it goes, and the rate you should be asking for instead.

1. What you are actually being offered

When a recruitment agency advertises a temporary role at £22 an hour and tells you it is paid through an umbrella company, that £22 is not your wage. It is the assignment rate, sometimes called the uplifted rate or the umbrella rate. It is the amount the agency pays the umbrella company for every hour you work. Your wage is whatever is left after the umbrella has covered the costs of employing you and taken its fee.

This is not a scandal and it is not hidden. HMRC's own guidance for workers says it in one sentence:

"You may not get paid the advertised pay rate. This is because it usually includes the umbrella company's operating costs."

The problem is that almost nobody reads that sentence before accepting the job, and agencies rarely volunteer a translation. So workers compare a £22 umbrella assignment against a £22 payroll job and conclude they are the same offer. They are not remotely the same offer.

Two things to be clear about before the numbers. First, umbrella employment is legitimate, and most umbrella companies operate correctly. Second, nothing here is a criticism of the deductions themselves. Employer National Insurance is a real cost that a real employer has to pay on your wages. The only question is who is funding it out of whose money, and in an umbrella arrangement the answer is you.

2. Where the money goes, line by line

The umbrella receives the assignment rate and works down to your gross pay. HMRC lists the deductions you should expect to see on the reconciliation statement: the umbrella's operating costs or margin, employer National Insurance, employer workplace pension contributions, holiday pay, and the Apprenticeship Levy where it applies.

Taking those in order, on 2026/27 rates:

The margin. The umbrella's own fee, typically £15 to £25 a week. It is charged every week you are paid, including weeks you barely work. It is also the only line here that is genuinely negotiable and genuinely varies between providers.

Employer National Insurance. 15% on earnings above the secondary threshold of £5,000 a year, which is £96.15 a week. This is the big one and it is the reason the gap is so large. The rate rose to 15% and the threshold fell to £5,000 in April 2025, and both are unchanged for 2026/27.

The Apprenticeship Levy. 0.5%, charged on employers with a pay bill above £3 million. Large umbrella companies comfortably clear that, so in practice it is nearly always passed on.

Holiday pay. This one is widely misreported, so be careful. Holiday pay is not a cost that disappears. It is your money, either rolled up into every payslip or held back and paid when you take leave. At the standard 12.07% accrual, of £707.73 of weekly gross pay about £76.22 is holiday pay and £631.50 is basic. Over a full year you get all of it. What you should watch is whether it is being rolled up, because rolled-up holiday pay means every week you take off is unpaid.

Employer pension contributions. If you are auto-enrolled and do not opt out, the employer contribution also comes out of the assignment rate. That money is genuinely yours, sitting in a pension, but it reduces the cash you see. The figures below assume no pension, to keep the comparison clean.

3. Worked example: Nasrin in Stevenage, £22 an hour

Nasrin is offered a 37.5 hour week at an assignment rate of £22 an hour, umbrella margin £22 a week, no pension. Weekly assignment income is £825.00.

Umbrella, working down to gross pay

Assignment income £825.00, less margin £22.00, less employer National Insurance £91.74, less Apprenticeship Levy £3.54.

Gross pay: £707.73 a week, which is £18.87 an hour on a £22 advertised rate. Over 52 weeks, gross pay of £36,802.

Income Tax £4,846 and employee National Insurance £1,939 come off that. Net pay: £30,017 a year, or £577.25 a week.

That is 70.0% of the assignment income she was quoted.

Agency PAYE at the same £22 an hour

Here the agency employs her directly and pays employer National Insurance out of its own margin, not hers. Gross pay is the full £825.00 a week, £42,900 a year.

Income Tax £6,066 and employee National Insurance £2,426 come off. Net pay: £34,408 a year, or £661.68 a week.

The gap is £4,391 a year, or £84.43 a week, for the same job, the same hours and the same headline number.

Now turn the arithmetic round, because this is the part you can actually use. To leave Nasrin with the same gross pay as £22 an hour of agency PAYE, the umbrella assignment rate has to be £25.61 an hour. That is a multiplier of roughly 1.16.

So when a recruiter quotes you an umbrella rate, divide it by 1.16 to see what it is worth in ordinary payroll terms. And when you are asked what rate you want, quote the umbrella number, not the payroll number. If you have been earning £22 an hour on a payroll job, asking for £22 through an umbrella is a pay cut of about £4,400 that you volunteered for.

These are 2026/27 figures for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Scottish income tax bands differ, so a Scottish taxpayer's net figures will not match, although the assignment rate arithmetic above the gross pay line is identical.

4. What changed on 6 April 2026, and what it does not fix

On 6 April 2026 the legal responsibility for operating PAYE on umbrella workers moved up the supply chain. HMRC's guidance, updated on 19 June 2026 to confirm the rules are now in force, is blunt about it: the agency with the contract to supply workers to the end client is responsible for making sure PAYE is operated correctly, and where there is no agency, that falls on the end client. If an umbrella company has not paid the correct PAYE, HMRC recovers it from them.

For a worker, that is genuinely good news, and it is the single most useful thing to understand about the current market. Agencies now have money at risk if they put you with a dodgy umbrella, which is why many have tightened their preferred supplier lists or moved workers onto their own payroll entirely.

What it does not fix. It does not change the arithmetic in section 3 by a single penny. Employer National Insurance still comes out of the assignment rate. It also does not make you safe from a scheme you agreed to. HMRC still says you are responsible for paying the correct amount of tax and National Insurance even though you are employed, and it warns specifically about any company promising you can keep 80%, 90% or 95% of your wages. There is no compliant umbrella that beats PAYE. If the take-home looks too good, the difference is usually a loan or a second untaxed payment, and the bill lands on you years later.

5. The two free things almost nobody asks for

The Key Information Document. Since 6 April 2020, under regulation 13A of the Conduct Regulations, an employment business must give you a Key Information Document before it agrees terms with you. Where you are paid through an umbrella, HMRC says it should name the umbrella company, state the minimum assignment rate paid to it, list what the umbrella will deduct, and state your minimum gross pay. That is the whole of section 3 of this article, in writing, specific to your job, before you sign anything. It is a legal entitlement and it is free. Ask for it by name and ask for it early.

HMRC's own calculator. Search results for umbrella take-home are dominated by calculators run by umbrella companies, which is a lead capture form wearing a maths costume. HMRC publishes a free one at gov.uk/guidance/work-out-pay-from-an-umbrella-company that works out gross and net pay from an assignment rate, with nobody to sell you anything. Use it to check a quoted rate before you accept, and use it again on your first payslip to check the deductions are what you were told.

The honest verdict

Umbrella employment is not a rip-off, but it is very commonly mispriced, and the mispricing runs one way. The assignment rate has to carry costs that an ordinary employer absorbs, so an umbrella rate and a payroll rate are different currencies with an exchange rate of about 1.16. Almost every complaint about umbrella pay traces back to somebody comparing the two as though they were the same.

If you are given a choice and the rate is identical, take agency PAYE. It is simpler, it is roughly £4,400 a year better on a £22 an hour job, and you avoid a weekly margin. If you are only offered umbrella, that is common and often not negotiable, so negotiate the number instead: work out the payroll rate you want, multiply by 1.16, and ask for that.

There is nothing to buy here and no product that improves your position. The whole of the value in this article is one multiplication and two documents you are already entitled to. If an umbrella company is promising you anything more than that, read section 4 again.

Frequently asked

Is it legal for the umbrella to take employer National Insurance out of my rate? Yes, provided the assignment rate was quoted to you as an assignment rate and the deductions are set out properly, which is what the Key Information Document is for. What is not allowed is taking employer National Insurance or employer pension contributions out of your gross pay. HMRC is explicit that deductions from gross pay should always be employee contributions. If your payslip shows employer National Insurance below the gross pay line, query it.

Can I just use my own limited company instead? Sometimes, but not if the client has assessed the role as inside IR35, which is usually why you were pushed to an umbrella in the first place. Where the off-payroll rules apply, a personal service company gives you the admin of a company with none of the tax benefit. Our guide to sole trader versus limited company covers the structures, though it is aimed at people trading on their own account rather than agency workers.

Can I choose my own umbrella company? Sometimes. Agencies usually have a preferred supplier list, and since April 2026 they have a stronger reason to insist on it, because they now carry the PAYE risk. It is still worth asking, and if you can choose, compare the weekly margin, which is the only line that genuinely differs.

What if my assignment rate is close to the minimum wage? Be careful. The umbrella must pay you at least the National Living Wage, £12.71 an hour for 2026/27, for every hour worked. Once employer National Insurance, the levy and a margin come out of a low assignment rate, there may not be enough left. If the numbers look impossible, they probably are, and that is worth raising with the agency before you start rather than after.
