The Cheapest Job on Your House, and the Easiest One to Ruin

Repointing is priced per square metre, which makes it look expensive on a whole elevation and remarkably cheap once you divide by the fifty years it should last. The catch is the mortar. Use the wrong one on an old wall and you will not be repointing again, you will be replacing bricks.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Repointing typically costs £40 to £90 per square metre depending on access, joint type and how hard the old mortar is to remove.
  • Good work lasts 40 to 60 years, which puts the whole-life cost at roughly £1 to £1.80 per square metre per year. That is the number worth quoting.
  • The mortar must be softer than the brick. Cement mortar on a soft handmade brick forces moisture to evaporate through the brick face instead of the joint, and the face spalls off.
  • Access is usually the biggest single line. Scaffolding a full elevation can equal the pointing itself, which is why doing one gable at a time is false economy.
  • Failed pointing is a damp cause, not a cosmetic issue. Open joints on a driven-rain elevation let water into the wall, and the repair bill is then plaster as well as mortar.

1. How to tell whether it actually needs doing

Pointing that looks tired is not the same as pointing that has failed, and the difference is several thousand pounds.

Take a key or a screwdriver and press it into a joint. If it scratches the surface and stops, the mortar is sound. If it goes in twenty or thirty millimetres with modest pressure, or comes out as sand, that joint has gone.

Then look for the three patterns that matter.

Recessed or eroded joints on one elevation only, usually the one facing the prevailing wind and rain. In most of Britain that is the south-west. Erosion concentrated on a single face is normal and tells you the job can often be confined to that elevation.

Cracked joints following a line. A crack that runs through both brick and mortar in a continuous line is movement, and repointing it will not fix anything. That is a structural question first.

Damp on the inside face that correlates with driving rain rather than with season. Open joints on an exposed elevation are one of the most common causes of penetrating damp in solid wall houses, and the internal symptom appears within a day or two of heavy wind-driven rain rather than building slowly through the winter.

If none of those apply and the joints are merely discoloured, leave it. Repointing sound mortar is a cost with no benefit, and every repoint slightly damages the brick arrises around the joint.

2. The mortar question, which decides everything

This is the section that justifies the article, because getting it wrong is genuinely ruinous and it is a mistake made every week.

A masonry wall handles water by letting it evaporate. In a traditional solid wall, the mortar joint is the sacrificial element: it is deliberately softer and more permeable than the brick, so moisture leaves through the joint, and when the joint erodes you replace the joint.

Modern ordinary Portland cement mortar is harder and far less permeable than a soft handmade Victorian brick. Point an old wall in it and the water that gets in cannot leave through the joint. It leaves through the brick face instead. In winter it freezes there, and the face of the brick spalls off in flakes.

The wall then looks worse every year, and the remedy is no longer pointing. It is cutting out and replacing thousands of brick faces, or rendering over the damage, and both cost multiples of the original job.

The right answer on a pre-1919 solid wall is generally a lime mortar, either a natural hydraulic lime or a lime putty mix, matched to the exposure and the brick. On a twentieth century cavity wall built in cement mortar with a hard machine-made brick, a cement-based mix is appropriate, because it matches what is already there.

So the single question to ask any quote is: what mix are you using, and why that one for this wall? An answer that names a lime type and a ratio is a good sign. An answer of "standard four to one" on a Victorian house is a reason to keep looking.

3. What moves the price per square metre

Raking out. Removing old cement pointing from a soft brick wall by hand, without damaging the arrises, is slow and skilled, and it is the biggest labour variable. Angle grinders are fast, cheap and widen every joint permanently, which is why the cheapest quote is often the one that does the most harm.

Access. Ground floor from a tower is cheap. A full two-storey gable needs scaffold, and scaffold on a single elevation commonly runs into four figures before a trowel is lifted.

Joint finish. A flush or bucket-handle finish is quick. A struck weather joint, a penny-struck joint or tuck pointing on a fine Georgian facade is slow and specialist, and priced accordingly.

Lime. Lime mortar needs protection from frost and from drying too fast, so the working season is narrower and the work is often hessian-covered and damped. That is real additional labour rather than a surcharge.

Worked example: the Sowerby household, Keighley

1890s stone-fronted terrace, south-west facing gable of roughly 38 m², joints eroded 20mm deep across most of the face, damp appearing on the inside of the gable bedroom after heavy rain from the west.

Quote one: £1,520, cement mortar, grinder raked, scaffold included. That is £40 per m².

Quote two: £3,190, natural hydraulic lime, hand raked, scaffold included, hessian protection. That is £84 per m².

Over a 50-year life, quote two is £1.68 per m² per year. Quote one, if it lasted the full fifty, would be £0.80. But on soft stone in a cement mortar it would not, and the failure mode is spalled stone rather than eroded joints.

The difference is £1,670, which over fifty years is £33 a year. Replacing spalled stone on that gable had been quoted separately, for a small area of earlier cement damage, at £180 per square metre.

They took quote two. The internal damp stopped after the first winter, and the redecoration they had been repeating every other year stopped with it.

5. How to spot a bad repoint after it has been done

Repointing is paid for on completion and inspected, if at all, from thirty feet away. Four signs tell you within a week whether you got what you paid for.

Smeared joints. Mortar spread onto the face of the brick either side of the joint, so the joint looks wider than the brick course allows. This happens when the mortar is too wet and the work too fast, and it is permanent unless the brick faces are cleaned before it sets.

Proud pointing. Mortar standing out from the face of the brick rather than finished flush or slightly recessed. It sheds water onto the brick below instead of away from the wall, and it looks wrong for the whole life of the work.

Widened joints. Compare a repointed area with an untouched one. If the new joints are visibly thicker, the raking out was done with a grinder and the brick arrises have been cut away. Nothing recovers that.

Shrinkage cracks along the edges. A fine crack running between the new mortar and the brick on both sides means poor adhesion, usually because the joint was not dampened before the mortar went in. Water runs straight down those cracks into the wall, which is precisely the problem the job was meant to fix.

Photograph a section of wall before work starts, at a known distance, and photograph the same section afterwards. That comparison settles any later disagreement faster than any amount of argument about workmanship.

6. The honest verdict

Test the joints with a key before you do anything. Discoloured pointing that does not scratch out does not need replacing, and repointing sound mortar is money spent to make a wall slightly worse.

If the house predates 1919, insist on lime and ask what mix and why. The extra is a pound or two per square metre a year over the life of the work, and the alternative is spalled brick faces that no amount of later pointing repairs.

Do a whole elevation at once if scaffold is needed. Paying for access twice to save half the pointing is the most reliable way to spend more for less.

Frequently asked

How often does a house need repointing? Properly done, every forty to sixty years, and often only on the most exposed elevation. A house being repointed every fifteen years is telling you the mortar specification is wrong.

Can I repoint myself? A small area at ground level, yes, with care and the right mix. Raking out without damaging brick arrises is the skilled part, and the results of learning on your own front elevation are permanent.

Does repointing stop damp? It stops penetrating damp caused by open joints, which is a common cause on exposed solid walls. It does nothing for condensation or for damp coming up from the ground, so establish which you have first.

Is a waterproof sealant a cheaper alternative? No, and on a solid wall it is often actively harmful. Sealing the outside face traps moisture already in the wall and prevents it evaporating, which is the same failure mode as cement pointing on soft brick.
