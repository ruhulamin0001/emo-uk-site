An Insulated Garage Door Keeps a Cold Room Slightly Less Cold

A replacement garage door runs from about 450 pounds for a manual up and over to 2,400 for an electric sectional. The insulation is sold as an energy saving and, on an unheated garage, it is worth roughly seven pounds a year. What genuinely costs money is the wording in your insurance policy.

The short version

  • Fitted price bands: manual up and over 450 to 900 pounds, manual sectional 900 to 1,400, electric roller 1,100 to 1,900, electric sectional 1,400 to 2,400.
  • Cost per year over life: up and over about 45 pounds, electric sectional about 96. Electric costs roughly twice as much a year and buys you not getting out of the car.
  • Insulation on an unheated garage is worth about 7 pounds a year on the arithmetic below. It is a comfort and noise purchase, not an energy one.
  • If the garage is integral and heated as a room, the sum changes, and that is the only case where an insulated door pays for itself.
  • Read the policy before choosing the lock. Many policies require the garage to be locked and define it, and a door with no key is not the same as a locked door.

1. The four door types, and what each is for

Up and over. The single panel door that swings out and up on a canopy or on retractable gear. Cheapest by a distance, mechanically simple, and it needs about a metre of clear driveway in front because the bottom edge swings out as it opens. If you park close, it is unusable.

Sectional. Horizontal panels that lift straight up and run back along the ceiling. Nothing swings outwards, so you can park right up to it, and the seal around the frame is far better than an up and over. It is the door most people would choose if price were not a factor.

Roller. A curtain of slats that coils into a box above the opening. Takes almost no headroom inside, which is why it suits garages with a low ceiling or with storage in the roof. Usually electric, because rolling one by hand is tiresome.

Side hinged. Two doors on hinges, like very large gates. Out of fashion and genuinely useful if you mostly use the garage as a store and want to open one leaf to get the mower out without opening the whole thing.

The first question is not which type but how you actually use the garage. A household that opens the door twice a day for cars has a different requirement from one that opens it twice a week for bikes, and the second household should not be paying for electric operation.

2. The insulation claim, worked out

Insulated doors are sold hard, and the pitch usually implies a heating saving. Here is the arithmetic, stated openly so you can check it.

A typical single garage door is about 5 square metres. An old uninsulated steel up and over has a doorset U value around 6.0 watts per square metre per degree. A modern insulated sectional is around 1.3. The difference is 4.7, which across 5 square metres is 23.5 watts per degree.

Now the crucial part. A detached, unheated garage is already close to outside temperature. There is no heat on the inside for the door to keep in. The door is separating cold from cold, and the saving on your house heating bill is zero.

An integral garage under a bedroom is slightly different, because heat leaks from the house into the garage and then out through the door. But the resistance in that path is dominated by the insulated wall and floor between the garage and the house, not by the door, and the improvement is small enough that it is not honestly measurable.

The one case where it counts is a garage heated as a workshop or a gym. Suppose you heat it electrically to 16 degrees for about 1,200 degree hours a year, which is a few hours a week through the winter. The saving is 23.5 watts per degree times 1,200, which is about 28 kWh, and at the Ofgem capped electricity rate of 26.32p per kWh for 1 October to 31 December 2026 that is about 7 pounds a year.

Seven pounds. That does not mean an insulated door is a bad buy. It is quieter, it does not boom in the wind, it is stiffer and it feels far better. Those are genuine reasons. An energy saving is not one.

Electric operation roughly doubles the cost per year against a manual up and over. It buys not getting out of the car in the rain, and for some households, particularly anyone with limited mobility or who reverses onto a busy road, that is worth far more than 50 pounds a year. It just is not a saving and should not be presented as one.

The last row deserves more attention than it gets. Most failed garage doors have failed at the springs, the cables or the gear, and those are replaceable parts. A torsion spring replacement is commonly 120 to 220 pounds and gives you several more years. Springs are under extreme tension and are genuinely dangerous to change without the right tools, so this is a job for someone with the bar and the experience, not a Saturday afternoon.

4. The insurance wording, which is the expensive detail

This is where a garage door decision can cost far more than the door.

Most home contents policies cover items in a garage at a lower limit than items in the house, often a few thousand pounds, and many make that cover conditional on the garage being locked. The question is what your insurer means by locked.

Some electric doors have no key at all. They are held shut by the motor and the drive, which is genuinely difficult to defeat, but an assessor reading a policy that says the garage must be secured by a key operated lock may take a different view. Ask the insurer directly, in writing, before you buy, and keep the answer.

Three things usually satisfy an insurer. A door carrying a recognised security certification, with Secured by Design being the one most often named. A key operated lock, either built in or an additional floor bolt or hasp. And an integral garage door meeting the requirement that applies to any door giving access to the house, which is usually stricter than the standard for a detached garage.

The other point specific to integral garages: a door between the garage and the house is a fire door requirement in most arrangements, and replacing the main garage door does not change that. If yours is a plain internal door, that is a separate and much cheaper thing to put right.

Finally, if you are storing anything of real value, a bike collection or tools, check the single item limit rather than the total. That is where garage claims most often fall short.

5. Worked example: the Whitfield garage in Scarborough

Derek Whitfield has a 1970s detached house in Scarborough with an integral single garage. The up and over door is original, the bottom edge has rusted through and the spring on one side has gone.

Three quotes. Repair the spring and weld a new bottom rail for 290 pounds. A manual sectional at 1,180 pounds. An electric sectional at 1,890 pounds.

He uses the garage for a car he takes out most days, and the driveway is short, so the up and over swing is already awkward. That rules out repairing it in the long run, though the repair would buy about five years.

On the insulation question he asks whether it will cut his heating bill. The garage is unheated and he does not intend to heat it, so on the arithmetic above the answer is nothing at all. The salesman quoting the electric sectional had described the insulation as paying for itself, which is not true and Derek said so.

He takes the manual sectional at 1,180 pounds, which is 59 pounds a year over twenty years against 95 for the electric. He is 62 and mobile and does not mind getting out of the car.

The call he made before ordering was to his insurer. His policy required the garage to be locked, and the sectional he had chosen came with a slam latch only. Adding a key operated handle cost 65 pounds at the point of order. Retrofitting one afterwards would have cost about 190, and discovering the problem after a claim would have cost considerably more than that.

Frequently asked

Do I need planning permission? Replacing a garage door like for like does not need permission. Widening the opening, or converting the garage into a room, is a different question and can need both planning and building control, particularly if it removes a parking space where the local authority has a policy on that.

Is an electric door a security risk? Modern units use rolling code remotes, which are not realistically cloneable. The older fixed code remotes from before about 2010 are. If your existing opener is that old, replacing the opener alone is worth doing.

What happens in a power cut? Every electric door has a manual release, usually a cord inside. Make sure you know where yours is and that it can be reached if you are outside with no other way in, which is the scenario people discover the hard way.

Are insulated doors quieter? Yes, and noticeably so, both in operation and in wind. If the garage is under a bedroom and somebody leaves for work at six, that is the strongest practical argument for insulation, and it is a better one than the seven pounds.

The honest verdict

Buy a manual sectional unless you have a specific reason to want electric. At about 58 pounds a year over its life it is the best value on this page, it needs no driveway clearance, and it seals far better than the up and over it replaces.

Do not pay extra for insulation on an unheated garage, because it saves nothing. Pay for it if the garage is a heated workshop, or if you want the door to be quieter, and be clear with yourself which of those you are buying. Then ring your insurer about the lock before you place the order.
