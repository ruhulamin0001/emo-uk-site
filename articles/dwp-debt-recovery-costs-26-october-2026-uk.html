From 26 October, the Cost of Chasing Your DWP Debt Becomes Part of Your DWP Debt

One line in a commencement order made in June sets a date that has had almost no coverage. On 26 October 2026, the Department for Work and Pensions gains the power to recover what it spent chasing you, using the same methods it uses to recover the debt. Here is what the provision says, and the important thing it does not say.

The short version

  • The date is 26 October 2026, set by regulation 2(2) of SI 2026/601. Nothing else in that instrument commences that day.
  • What commences is section 99, which inserts a new section 80G into the Social Security Administration Act 1992.
  • Section 80G makes recovery costs recoverable by the same methods as the debt. Not a new way to take money. A rule about what can be taken.
  • The driving disqualification power is not new in October. It came into force on 24 June 2026 and is a separate provision.
  • No tariff has been published. There is no figure in the Act, none in the regulations, and no published schedule of what recovery costs will be.
  • The lever is still the same. An affordable arrangement agreed before costs start accruing is the cheapest version of this for everyone.

1. What actually happens on 26 October

The Public Authorities (Fraud, Error and Recovery) Act 2025 has been switched on in pieces. The piece that matters this autumn was set by The Public Authorities (Fraud, Error and Recovery) Act 2025 (Commencement No. 3) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/601, made at 2.06pm on 8 June 2026.

That instrument does two separate things, and they are routinely reported as one:

Regulation 2(1). Sections 94 to 97, 100 and 104, together with Schedules 5 and 6, came into force on 24 June 2026. That block includes section 95 and Schedule 5, recovery from bank accounts, and section 96 and Schedule 6, disqualification from driving.

Regulation 2(2). Section 99, recovery of costs, comes into force on 26 October 2026. It is the only provision commencing on that date.

So the powers people associate with October arrived in June. What arrives in October is narrower, quieter, and has had far less attention: the cost of the recovery process itself.

2. What section 80G says, in plain terms

Section 99 does two things. It deletes subsection (3) of section 71ZE of the Social Security Administration Act 1992, which deals with court action, and it inserts a new section 80G into that Act.

The explanatory note to SI 2026/601 describes new section 80G as stating that any costs incurred by the Secretary of State in recovering an amount under sections 71 to 80 of, or new Schedule 3ZA or 3ZB to, the Social Security Administration Act 1992 may be recovered as though they were recoverable under the same methods as the debt itself.

Unpick that and it means three things.

  • Costs attach to the debt. Money the department spends pursuing you can be added to what you owe.
  • They travel by the same routes. Whatever mechanism is being used against the debt, deduction from benefit, deduction from earnings, a direct deduction order against a bank account under Schedule 3ZA, or the court route under Schedule 3ZB, can carry the costs too.
  • It covers the new powers as well as the old. The reference to Schedules 3ZA and 3ZB is deliberate. Costs of running a direct deduction order, or of going to court for a disqualification, are inside scope.

3. Four things this does not do

This is the part most coverage skips, and on a rule about people in debt it matters more than the headline.

  1. It does not create a new way of taking money from you. Section 80G is about what is recoverable, not about how. Every collection mechanism that exists on 27 October existed on 25 October.
  2. It does not set an amount. There is no figure in section 99, none in SI 2026/601, and no published tariff of recovery costs. Anyone quoting you a number for this today is guessing. That is a genuine gap, and it is fair to say so plainly rather than fill it.
  3. It does not start the driving ban clock. Section 96 and Schedule 6 commenced on 24 June 2026. A disqualification still needs a court, still needs at least £1,000 outstanding, and still begins as a suspended order.
  4. It does not make silence cheaper. Costs only exist because a process ran, and escalation is what runs it.

4. Worked example: Marcus in Doncaster

Marcus, Doncaster. He stopped claiming in 2023, works for himself now, and has an overpayment of £1,800 outstanding from a tax credits era award he never fully understood. He has ignored three letters.

Before 26 October. If the department escalates, whatever that escalation costs sits with the department. Marcus owes £1,800.

After 26 October. The same escalation can produce costs that are recoverable by the same methods as the £1,800 itself. His exposure is £1,800 plus an amount nobody can currently quantify.

What changes his position. Not paying £1,800 in one go, which he does not have. Contacting the department and agreeing an affordable amount, which stops the escalation that generates costs in the first place. Separately, if he thinks the overpayment is wrong, that is a dispute, and a dispute is a different track that does not resolve itself by ignoring post.

5. What is worth doing before the date

For most people this changes nothing. The cases that need escalating at all are those with an outstanding departmental debt who are no longer claiming and not in PAYE employment. If that is you, four things are worth doing, in this order.

  • Open the post. Every safeguard in this legislation is triggered by engagement, and none of them by silence.
  • Ask for an affordable arrangement, not a figure you cannot sustain. An arrangement you break is what moves a case along, and movement is what creates cost.
  • Challenge it if it is wrong. Overpayment decisions can be disputed. That is separate from the repayment conversation and you can do both.
  • Get free advice. Citizens Advice, StepChange and National Debtline cost nothing. Do not pay a company to talk to the department for you.

The honest verdict

This is a real change on a real date, and it is smaller than it sounds. Section 99 does not hand the department a new weapon. It changes the accounting: from 26 October 2026 the cost of pursuing a benefit debt can be pursued alongside it, through the same channels.

The honest problem is that the size of it is unknown. No tariff has been published, so the practical answer to "how much will this add" is that nobody outside the department can tell you yet. What is knowable is the direction. Costs accrue when a case escalates, and escalation is the one part of this a person with a debt can still influence, by picking up the phone before October rather than after.

Frequently asked

Does this mean the DWP can take my driving licence from 26 October? No. That power is in section 96 and Schedule 6, and it came into force on 24 June 2026. It requires a court, at least £1,000 still outstanding, and a suspended order before any immediate order. Section 99 is only about recovery costs.

How much will the recovery costs be? Nobody has published a figure. There is no amount in the Act, none in SI 2026/601, and no published schedule. Treat any specific number you see today as speculation.

Can costs be added to a debt I am already repaying by agreement? Section 80G makes costs incurred in recovering an amount recoverable by the same methods as that amount. If no recovery action is being taken because an arrangement is being kept to, there is no recovery process generating costs. This is general information and not legal advice, so if you have an arrangement and receive anything that looks like an additional charge, take it to free debt advice.
