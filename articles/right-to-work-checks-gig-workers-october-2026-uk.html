From 1 October the app has to check your right to work, not just your bank details

Almost every write-up of this rule change is addressed to employers. Here is the same change from the other side, because if you pick up shifts through an agency, a platform or a subcontract, the paperwork lands on you first.

Ruhul Amin Published 12 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked 30 September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • 1 October 2026 is the date, set by SI 2026/683, which brings section 48 of the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 into force.
  • Three new groups are covered: worker's contracts, individual subcontractors, and people matched with customers by an online matching service.
  • The penalty falls on the business: a starting point of £45,000 per worker for a first breach and £60,000 for a repeat within three years, under the draft code of practice that comes into force the same day.
  • Penalties only bite on new engagements: that draft code says a civil penalty in the three new categories may only be imposed where the engagement commenced on or after 1 October 2026.
  • It does not change your immigration status and does not make you an employee of the platform. It changes who has to check, not what you may do.
  • What you will be asked for: a passport if you are a British or Irish citizen, or a share code if you are not.

1. What section 48 actually does

The Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 (Commencement No. 4) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/683 (C. 57), made on 24 June 2026 and signed by Alex Norris, Minister of State at the Home Office, bring section 48 of that Act into force on 1 October 2026.

Section 48 amends the Immigration, Asylum and Nationality Act 2006, inserting a new section 14A. In sections 15 to 24 of the 2006 Act, which are the illegal working provisions that produce referral notices and civil penalties, a reference to employing someone now also covers:

  • engaging an individual under a worker's contract, meaning a contract other than a contract of service or apprenticeship under which the individual does the work personally, and where the engager is not simply a client or customer of a business the individual runs;
  • engaging an individual subcontractor, where the business has contracted with a third party to provide the work and the individual has not;
  • an online matching service passing a service provider's details to potential clients or customers.

The checking duty that applied to employees now applies far more widely.

2. What counts as an online matching service

This definition decides whether an app is caught, and it has three limbs, all of which must be met. In the course of a business, the service must:

  1. keep a register of service providers for the purpose of matching them with potential clients or customers;
  2. provide an online service through which potential clients or customers can submit enquiries to be matched with a suitable provider; and
  3. charge a fee or commission in return for making such matches.

A "service provider" is a person providing, or seeking to provide, work or services for remuneration.

The fee or commission limb does real work. A free noticeboard that takes nothing from either side does not obviously meet limb three. A platform that takes a cut of every job does. If your work comes through an app that keeps a percentage, assume you are inside the rules from 1 October.

What is actually at stake is worth having in one place. These are the consequences GOV.UK lists for employing someone without the right to work, and from 1 October 2026 they reach the engagements described above.

Put that against a real business. Rashid runs a two van courier round in Luton and uses three regular subcontract drivers, each invoicing him as self employed. Until now he has checked nothing, because none of the three is his employee and the law did not ask him to. From 1 October he is engaging individual subcontractors, which is exactly what section 48 brings into scope. If all three turned out not to have the right to work, his exposure is 3 × £60,000, which is £180,000, against a business that does not turn over that in a year. The check that removes the exposure costs him nothing and takes a few minutes per person on the Home Office online service. There is no version of this arithmetic in which skipping it is the cheaper option.

The honest caveat is that £60,000 is a maximum and not a flat fee, and the Home Office sets the actual amount case by case. But the statutory excuse only exists where the check was done in the prescribed way, so the useful move is not to argue about the number afterwards, it is to hold the evidence beforehand.

3. Who can be fined, and from when

The money side of this change is not in section 48 at all. It sits in a code of practice, and on the day before commencement that code is still published on GOV.UK as a draft.

The Illegal working penalties: codes of practice for employers page was last updated on 30 June 2026. Two editions sit on it. The edition in force is dated 13 February 2024 and says nothing about any of this. Beside it is a draft, dated 30 June 2026 and 37 pages long, which calls itself the seventh version of the code, is issued under section 19 of the Immigration, Asylum and Nationality Act 2006, and states that it will come into force on 1 October 2026.

Two things in that draft change the picture for anyone already doing this work, and neither is in the commencement regulations.

First, the penalty is two numbers, not one. The draft code sets the starting point for a first breach at £45,000 per worker, and £60,000 per worker where a breach is found within the previous three years. The £60,000 that every headline quotes is the repeat-breach figure, not the opening one.

Second, and this is the part almost nobody is reporting. For the three new categories, the draft code says a civil penalty may only be imposed where the employment commenced on or after 1 October 2026. It says that applies both to liability under section 15 of the 2006 Act and to the extended liability that section 48 inserts as a new section 15A, which can reach a business further up a contracting chain. On the face of the draft code, an engagement that was already running before 1 October does not expose the business to a civil penalty under the new categories.

Read that carefully, because it cuts one way only. It is a limit on fines, not permission to work without status, and it does not stop a platform or an agency asking you anyway. Most will ask everybody, because working out who is in scope costs more than checking. But if you are told you must be checked today or lose work you have been doing for a year, the document that decides the money says something narrower than that.

It is also a draft, and a draft can change before it is issued. Until it is, the code in force is the 2024 one. What does not change either way is the thing that makes this painless from your side: one share code, generated once, sent to everyone who asks. If being asked by three different engagers in a month is the nudge you needed towards one properly sponsored job instead, our sister site sponsorjobuk.com sets out how a sponsored job is actually found, step by step.

4. What this does not do

This matters as much as what it does, because the scare version of the story is already circulating.

  • It does not change your immigration status, or the hours and work types your permission allows.
  • It does not make you an employee of the platform or the agency. Employment status is decided elsewhere, on the usual tests.
  • It does not put the fine on you. GOV.UK is clear the civil penalty of up to £60,000 for each illegal worker goes to the business that failed to check. The separate criminal offence, carrying up to five years in prison and an unlimited fine, applies to someone who knew or had reasonable cause to believe a person did not have the right to work.
  • It does not touch genuine business to business work. The worker's contract limb excludes the case where the engager is a client or customer of a business you carry on.

Marek, Slough. Two or three warehouse shifts a week through an agency, plus weekend delivery work through an app that takes a commission on each drop. Until now only the agency had to check him. From 1 October the app is caught too, because it keeps a register of drivers, matches them through its own software, and charges commission. He will be asked twice for the same thing. He generates one share code and sends it to both, and it costs him ten minutes. The failure mode is not the check, it is being asked on a Friday night for a document he has not looked at since 2023.

The verdict

This is an administrative change with a hard date and a wide reach, reported almost entirely as a compliance headache for businesses. For the person doing the work it is one sentence: from 1 October 2026, more of the places you get work from are legally obliged to check your right to work, so have the document or the share code to hand. Students get caught here more than anyone, because their permission carries an hours limit as well as an expiry date, and the student visa maintenance rules changing on 30 November 2026 sit alongside it.

If your status is settled, this is a ten minute job. If it is not, get free advice from a named source before 1 October rather than at the point of being asked.

Frequently asked

What exactly will I be asked for? British and Irish citizens can use a British passport, or an Irish passport or passport card, and GOV.UK confirms these can be current or expired. Everyone else uses a share code, which you can generate if you hold a biometric residence permit or a UK Visas and Immigration account.

Will a company I already work for ask me again? Probably, and it is worth expecting it. The commencement regulations contain no transitional carve out, but the draft code of practice does one: it says a civil penalty in the three new categories may only be imposed where the engagement commenced on or after 1 October 2026. That limits the fine, not the asking. Most platforms and agencies will check everyone, because sorting out who is in scope costs more than checking. Being asked is not an accusation.

I am self employed and invoice my own clients. Am I caught? The worker's contract limb does not apply where the engager is a client or customer of a business you genuinely carry on. Section 48 is aimed at platform and subcontract arrangements, not ordinary client work.
