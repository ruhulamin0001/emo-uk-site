Every Guide To Starting A Bookkeeping Business Skips The Bit Where It Is A Criminal Offence

Bookkeeping is sold as one of the safest home-based side hustles in Britain: low startup cost, work from a laptop, steady demand. What almost none of that content mentions is that taking a paying client without anti-money laundering supervision is, in HMRC's own words, a criminal offence. Here is what supervision costs and how to get it.

1. The rule the guides leave out

Search for how to start a bookkeeping business in the UK and you will find a fairly consistent answer: get some training, buy accounting software, sort out insurance, find your first client. It is presented as one of the lowest-barrier home businesses available.

Here is what HMRC actually says about businesses covered by the Money Laundering Regulations, in the plainest words on the page:

"A business must not trade without registering with HMRC under the regulations. Trading while not registered is a criminal offence. It may result in a penalty or prosecution."

That is not a footnote about large firms. Bookkeeping falls inside the regulations as an accountancy service provider, which HMRC lists among the sectors it supervises where the business is not already supervised by a professional body.

So the sequence most content implies, find a client and then sort the paperwork out, is the wrong way round. Supervision comes before the first paying client, not after the first invoice.

There is one piece of genuinely useful nuance that softens this, and almost nobody reports it either. HMRC says that only money service businesses and trust or company service providers must wait for their registration to be confirmed before trading. All other businesses, including accountancy service providers, can trade while their application is being processed. So the wait is not a barrier. Not applying at all is.

2. Whether this actually applies to you

The regulations bite on activities carried out by way of business. That phrase is doing the work, and HMRC acknowledges it is not always obvious, offering a contact route for people who are unsure after reading the guidance.

Two situations are clear enough.

If you are charging clients to keep their books, you are almost certainly in scope. It does not matter that you work alone, from a spare room, for three small businesses, and earn a few thousand pounds a year. Nothing in the regulations has a hobby threshold.

If you are doing the books for your own business, you are not providing a service to anybody else and this does not apply.

Two more points worth knowing. Certain charities and public sector bodies are outside the regulations because their services are not carried out by way of business, for example UK registered charities providing these services free or for a nominal charge. And you are carrying out business in the UK if your registered or head office is here and the day-to-day management happens here, regardless of where your clients are.

You can check whether another business is registered using HMRC's Supervised Business Register, which is also a reasonable thing for your own future clients to do to you.

3. What supervision costs, in HMRC's own figures

HMRC's published fees, updated on 22 December 2025 to reflect changes from 1 December 2025:

FeeAmountWhen
Application fee£300One-off, non-refundable, on first registration
Premises registration fee£400Per premises, on registration
Approval process fee£40Per person tested, for accountancy service providers
Annual declaration fee£400Per premises, every year

The fit and proper test fee of £500 applies to money service businesses and trust or company service providers only, so a bookkeeper does not pay it. What a bookkeeper does pay is the £40 approval check, which looks for relevant criminal convictions among beneficial owners, officers or managers.

Two details that catch people out.

Your home is premises. HMRC's list of premises to register includes a "home address or contact address (if you do not have a business address)", alongside offices, shops, call centres and virtual offices. Working from the kitchen table does not avoid the £400.

The premises fee is not refundable if you deregister. HMRC will not refund the fee for a premises you remove, and if your registration is cancelled, including for non-payment of annual fees, you pay the £300 application fee again if you come back.

There is a small business reduction if your turnover is under £5,000: you pay the full £300 and £400 up front and receive a £500 refund once the application is accepted, which HMRC aims to process within 30 days of having the information it needs. You have to email HMRC separately with "Turnover Reduction" in the subject line after paying, so it is not automatic.

One honest flag. The same GOV.UK page says in the application fee section that the fee "will be waived for businesses eligible for the small business reduction", and in the small business section that you pay it and get a £500 refund. Those two statements do not obviously agree. If you are near the £5,000 turnover line, ring HMRC and get the answer for your case rather than relying on either sentence, including ours.

4. The choice that changes the number: HMRC or a professional body

This is the decision that most affects your costs, and it comes first.

You do not need to register with HMRC if you are already supervised by a professional body that acts as a supervisory authority. HMRC publishes the list, and several are directly relevant to bookkeepers:

  • Institute of Certified Bookkeepers
  • Institute of Accountants and Bookkeepers
  • Association of Accounting Technicians
  • Association of Chartered Certified Accountants
  • Institute of Financial Accountants
  • Association of Taxation Technicians and the Chartered Institute of Taxation

Membership of one of those bodies typically bundles supervision with a practising licence, professional standards, technical support and often insurance arrangements. Whether it is cheaper than HMRC depends entirely on that body's current fees, which are set by them and change, so we are not going to publish a comparison table that would be stale within months. What we can tell you is the shape of the decision: compare the body's total annual cost of membership plus practising licence against HMRC's £440 a year, and remember the body is buying you more than a registration number.

The one thing you must not do is assume that holding a bookkeeping qualification means you are supervised. A qualification is not supervision. Supervision is a live registration with either HMRC or a listed body, and if you hold neither, you are trading unsupervised.

5. Worked example: what year one really costs

Worked example: Aisling in Belfast

Aisling is a part-time bookkeeper starting out from home with three small clients. She has no business premises and no employees. She decides to register directly with HMRC rather than join a professional body.

Year one, HMRC fees only:

  • Application fee: £300
  • Premises fee for her home address: £400
  • Approval process fee for herself: £40

Total: £740, before she has earned anything, and none of it is optional.

Year two onwards: the £400 annual declaration fee plus the £40 approval fee, so £440 a year. Over five years that is £2,500 in supervision fees alone. All of these are our own additions of HMRC's published figures.

What that means for her pricing. On three small clients, £740 in year one is a meaningful share of her first year's income, and it lands before the first invoice. It is also entirely predictable, which makes it the easy part of the business plan once you know about it. The people this rule hurts are the ones who find out in month seven.

The alternative she should price first. If a professional body's membership plus practising licence costs less than £440 a year, she gets supervision and a body of standards and support for less than HMRC alone. If it costs more, she is paying the difference for those extras. Either way it is a comparison she can do in an afternoon, and it is the first thing to do rather than the last.

6. The honest verdict

Bookkeeping is still a reasonable UK side hustle. The work is real, the demand is real, and the barrier to entry is genuinely lower than most professional services. This article is not an argument against it.

It is an argument against starting it the way the internet tells you to. Anti-money laundering supervision is not admin you catch up on later. HMRC describes trading without it as a criminal offence, and the fee structure means year one costs £740 in supervision before software, insurance or training.

So do it in this order. Decide between HMRC and a professional body, and price both. Register. Then find your first client. The good news, and it is genuinely good news, is that as an accountancy service provider you can trade while your application is processed, so the only thing standing between you and a compliant start is applying.

If you have already been trading unsupervised, the answer is not to keep quiet. Get registered, and if you are unsure about your position, take proper advice before you contact anyone.

Frequently asked

Does this apply if I only do data entry and not tax returns? Bookkeeping services generally fall within accountancy services for these purposes, and HMRC's test is whether you carry out the activity by way of business rather than which specific tasks you perform. If you are genuinely unsure where your work sits, HMRC has a contact route for money laundering questions and it is worth using before you take a client.

I already have a qualification. Am I covered? No. A qualification is not supervision. You are only covered if you hold a live registration with HMRC or are supervised by one of the listed professional bodies, which usually means being a member in practice with a current practising licence rather than simply holding their certificate.

What if I have more than one work location? Each premises carries its own GBP 400 fee. Adding premises in the first six months of your registration year costs the full fee; adding in the second six months costs half. Removing a premises does not get you a refund.

Can I be prosecuted for a genuine mistake? HMRC's guidance says trading unregistered may result in a penalty or prosecution, which leaves it to HMRC's judgement rather than making prosecution automatic. That is not a reason to rely on leniency. It is a reason to register before you trade, which costs nothing extra since you can trade while the application is processed.
