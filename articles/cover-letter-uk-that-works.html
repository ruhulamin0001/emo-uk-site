Half of UK Adverts Ask for a Cover Letter. Only a Third of Applicants Send One

Cover letters are widely described as dead. The survey data says something more useful and more exploitable: fewer people write them than are asked for them, and the people who make the final decision read them more often than the recruiters who screen first.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • 40 to 60% of UK roles still ask for a cover letter, down from around 80% before 2018. Only 30 to 40% of UK candidates send one. That gap is the whole opportunity.
  • 94% of hiring managers say a cover letter influences the interview decision, with 45% always reading them and 38% usually.
  • 39% of recruiters skip them, but only 23% of final decision makers do. The person screening may not read it. The person hiring probably will.
  • When a listing says "optional", 72% of hiring managers still expect one. Optional means optional for them, not for you.
  • 81% of recruiters have rejected someone purely on the cover letter. A bad one is worse than none, which is the honest caveat.

1. The gap in the numbers

Two figures, side by side, and they do not match.

  • 40 to 60% of UK roles ask for a cover letter. That is down from around 80% before 2018, so the "they are dying out" claim is not wrong.
  • Only 30 to 40% of UK candidates actually send one.

So in a large share of applications, the employer asked and the applicant did not deliver. When several hundred people apply for a role, anything that separates you from the majority without requiring more experience is worth twenty minutes.

2. Who actually reads it

The confusion in cover letter advice comes from lumping two different jobs together.

WhoWhat they doHow often they skip the cover letter
Recruiter or screenerFirst pass, high volume, often matching against a checklist39% do not read it
Hiring manager, the final decision makerChooses who to interview and who to hireOnly 23% do not read it

In surveys of hiring managers, 94% say cover letters influence their interview decisions, 45% always read them and 38% usually do.

The practical reading: your cover letter is unlikely to get you through the first automated or checklist screen, and that is not its job. Its job is to work on the person who is choosing between six shortlisted CVs that all look similar.

Your CV says you are qualified. Six other shortlisted people are also qualified. The cover letter is the only place you get to say why you, specifically, for this job, specifically.

3. The four paragraph structure

One page. Four paragraphs. Never longer.

Paragraph one: the specific opening. Name the role, and give one concrete reason you are applying to this employer. Not "I was excited to see your advert". Something that proves you looked: their new site, their product, a change in their sector.

Paragraph two: your strongest matching evidence. One achievement, with a number, that maps onto the main requirement in the advert. Not a summary of your CV. One thing, in detail.

Paragraph three: the second requirement, and the gap. Cover the other thing they emphasised. If you are missing something they asked for, address it here in one sentence rather than hoping they will not notice. Silence on an obvious gap reads worse than a plain explanation.

Paragraph four: the close. Short. What you would bring in the first months, and that you would welcome the chance to discuss it. No pleading, no "I would be grateful for the opportunity".

Formatting. Same font and header as your CV. Address a named person if you can find one, "Dear Hiring Manager" if you cannot, never "To Whom It May Concern". Paste it into the application box as well as attaching it, because attachments sometimes do not open.

4. Before and after, the same applicant

Tom, applying for a facilities coordinator role at a Leeds manufacturer.

Before, paragraph one: "I am writing to apply for the position advertised on your website. I am a hard working and motivated individual with excellent communication skills and I believe I would be a great asset to your team."

What is wrong with it. It does not name the role. It contains nothing that could not be sent to any employer in Britain. And it uses three phrases that appear on almost every application, which means it distinguishes him from nobody.

After, paragraph one: "I am applying for the Facilities Coordinator role. I saw that you brought your maintenance in house last year, which is the same move I ran at my current site, and the reason the job interests me."

After, paragraph two: "At my current employer I manage planned maintenance across a 40,000 square foot site and a team of four contractors. Moving from reactive callouts to a planned schedule cut unplanned downtime from about 14 hours a month to under 4, and reduced our contractor spend by roughly £11,000 a year."

After, paragraph three, addressing the gap: "Your advert asks for experience with a CAFM system and I have used Excel and a supplier portal rather than a dedicated platform. I learned our supplier portal in a fortnight and would expect the same here, and I am happy to be tested on it."

Why the second version works. Nothing about Tom changed. The before version asked to be believed. The after version gave two numbers, proved he had researched them, and dealt with his weakest point openly instead of leaving the reader to find it.

5. What to do when it says optional

When a listing calls the cover letter optional, 72% of hiring managers still expect one. Treat "optional" as "this is how we find out who is serious".

The one time to skip it: when there is genuinely no field for it and no email address, and the application is a structured form only. In that case put your effort into the free text answers, which are read for the same reason.

6. Using AI without sounding like everyone else

Generative tools are now used widely in job applications, and recruiters read hundreds of the outputs a week. The register is recognisable, and a letter that reads as machine written signals that you did not care enough to write it.

The version that works: write your own bullet points first, with your real numbers, then use a tool to tighten the sentences. What you must not outsource is the specific detail, because that is the only part carrying any weight, and a model that does not know your job cannot invent it.

A simple test before you send: could this letter be sent to a different company with only the name changed? If yes, it is not doing anything, and you would be better off sending nothing.

7. The mistakes that cause rejection

Worth taking seriously, because 81% of recruiters say they have rejected a candidate purely on the cover letter. This is the reason a weak one is worse than none.

  • The wrong company name. The single most common fatal error, caused by editing a previous letter. Read the first and last lines aloud before sending.
  • Repeating your CV in prose. They have the CV. This is your only chance to say something that is not on it.
  • Making it about what you want. "This role would help me develop" is about you. "You need someone who can do X, here is when I did X" is about them.
  • Length. Over one page and a recruiter with 300 applications will not start it.
  • Explaining a gap defensively. One plain sentence. Long justifications draw attention to the thing you want them to move past.
  • No name, no sign off, no contact details. Basic, and it still happens.

The honest verdict

Cover letters are declining in how often they are requested, and that is real. What has not declined is how much they matter when the person deciding actually reads one, and 94% of hiring managers say they influence the interview decision.

The arithmetic favours writing one. Between 40 and 60% of UK roles ask, only 30 to 40% of candidates comply, and 72% of hiring managers expect one even when the advert says optional. Twenty minutes puts you in the smaller, more considered pile.

But write a real one. A generic letter is worse than no letter, because 81% of recruiters have rejected somebody on the strength of one. If you cannot say something specific about that employer and back it with one number of your own, the letter is not ready to send.

Frequently asked

How long should a UK cover letter be? One page, four paragraphs, roughly 250 to 350 words. Shorter is fine if it is specific. Longer is not.

Should I attach it or paste it into the email? Both where possible. Paste it into the body so it is read without a download, and attach a PDF that matches your CV formatting.

What if I cannot find a name to address it to? Check LinkedIn for the hiring manager or the department head, then the company's team page. If neither works, "Dear Hiring Manager" is perfectly acceptable in the UK.

Do I need one for an agency application? A shorter version, yes. The recruiter uses it to pitch you to their client, so give them the specific line you want repeated.
