The Energy Saving Trust puts the cost of leaving devices on standby at about £45 a year in Great Britain. That is a real number you can act on for about £24. It is also a number that gets wasted by people who put a smart plug on the wrong appliance.

1. What the £45 actually is

The Energy Saving Trust's appliance guidance, last updated on 3 August 2026, says that switching devices off standby can help you save £45 a year in Great Britain and £55 in Northern Ireland. That is the whole-house figure for everything left drawing power while it is doing nothing.

It is worth being precise about what standby means here, because two different things get called the same thing. There is genuine standby, where a device sits waiting for a remote control or a wake signal. And there is the slower drain of chargers, set-top boxes, printers and speakers that are simply never turned off at the wall. The £45 covers both.

The same page puts consumer electronics, meaning televisions, laptops and games consoles together, at around 6% of a typical household energy bill. So this is a real saving, but it is a small slice. Anyone selling you a smart plug on the promise of transforming your bill is overselling it.

2. Turning £45 into watts, so you can check your own house

Ofgem's price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 sets electricity at 26.32p a kWh with a 54.83p daily standing charge. There is no VAT on electricity from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, so that 26.32p is the whole rate.

Divide £45 by 26.32p and you get 171 kWh a year. Spread that over 8,760 hours and you get 19.5 watts of continuous draw. That is the sentence that makes the number checkable: if your house is average, something in it is quietly pulling about 19 watts, day and night, forever.

Nineteen watts is roughly a modern television on standby plus a games console in rest mode plus a set-top box plus a handful of chargers. If that sounds like your living room, the £45 applies to you. If you already unplug things, it does not, and no smart plug will find money that is not there.

You can price any individual device the same way. Watts, divided by 1,000, times 8,760 hours, times 26.32p. A device drawing 2 watts costs £4.61 a year. A device drawing 10 watts costs £23.06 a year.

4. The four things that must never go on a smart plug

The fridge and the freezer. A smart plug that loses its Wi-Fi connection, gets knocked off a schedule or fails closed will cost you a freezer full of food. Some smart plugs also have a lower continuous current rating than a compressor's start-up surge. The saving is zero anyway, because a fridge has no standby state.

The boiler. Cutting power to a boiler at the wall interrupts frost protection and, on some systems, forces a reset sequence. If you want the boiler to run less, that is what a programmer and a room thermostat are for. Our page on heating controls and their payback covers the honest version of that.

The broadband router. Switching a router off at night does not save a meaningful amount, and on some connections it triggers the line to retrain at a lower speed. If your landline runs over broadband, and from January 2027 most will, cutting the router cuts your phone.

Anything medical. This should not need saying, but it does.

5. The payback, done properly

Smart plugs are widely sold in the £10 to £15 range and in multipacks that bring the unit price down. We are not going to print a price here, because prices move daily and a number typed into a page today is wrong by the weekend. Assume £12 each and check the current price before you buy.

Two plugs, one on the television stack and one on the games console, is £24. Between them those two groups sit at the top of the table, which on the middle of the ranges above is about £22 a year recovered.

£24 divided by £22 a year is 1.1 years, or about 13 months. One winter and a bit. That is a genuinely good payback for a £24 spend, and it repeats every year afterwards.

What it is not is the full £45. To get the rest you would need to switch off things with clocks, timers and update schedules that most households will not tolerate. Anyone quoting the whole £45 as a smart plug saving is quoting a number that assumes behaviour nobody sustains.

The alternative is free: a switched extension lead you already own, turned off at the wall with your hand. It saves exactly the same electricity. The smart plug buys you the schedule, so that it happens on the nights you forget.

6. Worked example: Priya in Reading

Priya has a television, a soundbar and a set-top box on one extension lead behind the unit, and a games console on a separate socket. She measures nothing, so she uses the middle of the table: 10 W for the television group and 8 W for the console.

10 W plus 8 W is 18 W. Over 8,760 hours that is 157.7 kWh a year, which at 26.32p is £41.50 a year. She is close to the national figure, which is what you would expect from a fairly normal living room.

She schedules both plugs off from midnight to 7am, seven days a week. That is 49 hours a week, or 29% of the week. 29% of £41.50 is £12.04 a year. Her £24 of plugs pays back in two years, not one.

She then extends the schedule to cover the working day as well, midnight to 7am and 9am to 5pm, which is 105 hours a week or 62.5% of the week. That takes the saving to £25.94 a year and the payback to 11 months.

This is the honest shape of it. The plug does not save you money. The schedule does. A smart plug on a permanent always-on schedule is a £12 ornament.

7. Measuring your own house, which beats every table on this page

Everything above uses typical draws, because we cannot see your living room. A plug-in energy monitor, or a smart plug with energy monitoring built in, replaces all of that guesswork with your own numbers in an afternoon.

The method is short. Put the monitor between the socket and the extension lead that feeds your television stack. Put every device on that stack into its normal standby state. Leave it an hour and read the wattage.

Then use the same sum from section 2: watts, divided by 1,000, times 8,760, times 26.32p. A reading of 12 W is £27.67 a year. A reading of 3 W is £6.92 a year and you should not bother with a smart plug for it.

Three readings are worth taking, because they answer different questions.

Standby with everything nominally off. This is the number the £45 refers to and the number a smart plug can recover.

Draw during normal use. Useful for a sanity check against the energy label, and for spotting a device that is far worse than its rating suggests.

Draw overnight on a device you thought was off. This is where people find the surprise: a games console downloading updates at three in the morning, a printer waking hourly to check for jobs, a television running a background service. Those are not standby in the technical sense and they are often the largest single item.

One warning about the readings. Cheap monitors are inaccurate at very low wattages, and some cannot resolve anything below about 3 W at all. If a device reads 0 W, that may be true or it may be below the meter's floor. It matters little either way, because anything under 3 W is under £7 a year and not worth a plug.

The reason this is worth doing once, rather than reading a table forever, is that it settles the argument in your house permanently. You will know which two sockets are worth a plug and which nine are not, and you will not spend £60 on plugs to recover £15.

8. The honest verdict

Buy two smart plugs, put one on the television stack and one on the games console, and set a schedule that actually switches them off overnight and while the house is empty. On the arithmetic in section 6 that is about £24 for £12 to £26 a year, paid back somewhere between eleven months and two years depending on how long your schedule runs. That is a good return on a small spend and it repeats every year.

Do not buy a plug for every socket in the house. Chargers, clocks and low-draw devices do not carry their own plug's cost, and the fridge, freezer, boiler and router must never go on one at all. And if you already switch things off at the wall by hand, you are getting the same saving for nothing; the plug only buys you the nights you would have forgotten.

Frequently asked

Does a smart plug itself use electricity? Yes, typically 0.5 to 1 W, which is £1.15 to £2.31 a year at 26.32p. That is already netted off in the arithmetic above if you are recovering £20 or more. If you were only ever going to save £3 a year on one device, the plug cancels most of it out.

Is it cheaper to leave the television on standby or switch it off? Switch it off. There is no warm-up penalty on a modern television and no component that suffers from being powered down. The old advice about leaving things on because switching them costs more applies to nothing sold this century.

What about turning the lights off? The Energy Saving Trust puts that at around £7 a year in Great Britain and £9 in Northern Ireland, just from switching lights off as you leave a room. It costs nothing to do.

Will a smart plug work in a power cut? It will come back on when power returns, but whether it returns to on or off depends on the model. If you have scheduled something important, check that behaviour before you rely on it.
