The Bank Of England Decides On 17 September. Three Members Already Wanted A Rise. What To Do With Savings And A Mortgage Now.

Two weeks from today the Monetary Policy Committee announces its next Bank Rate decision. The last vote was not the comfortable hold the headline suggested: six members voted to stay at 3.75% and three voted to go to 4%. Inflation has risen to 2.9%, and the August figure is published the morning before the decision. Here is what the numbers say, and what a saver or a borrower can actually do with them.

1. Where things stand

The Bank of England's own page states the facts plainly: Bank Rate 3.75%, next decision due 17 September 2026, current inflation 2.9% against a 2% target.

The July decision is the one to read. At the meeting ending 29 July, the Monetary Policy Committee voted by a majority of six to three to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%. Three members voted to increase it by 0.25 percentage points to 4%. The summary published on 30 July gives the reason: crude and refined energy prices have stayed volatile and higher than before the conflict in the Middle East, and the committee is watching whether that energy shock is feeding into wider prices and wages.

So the September question is not "will they cut". It is whether two more members join the three. A hold at 3.75% remains the base case in most market commentary, but a rise to 4% is a live possibility rather than a tail risk, and that is a very different backdrop from the spring.

2. Savings: what a hold or a hike does to £10,000

Moneyfacts published its inflation analysis on 19 August 2026. The Moneyfacts Average New Savings Rate was 3.63% AER. The top-paying easy access account was 5.00%, the top easy access ISA 4.61%, and 1,850 accounts beat the 2.9% inflation rate, roughly four in five. Those are Moneyfacts' figures, dated 18 and 19 August, and top rates move weekly.

The arithmetic on £10,000 held for a year:

RateInterest on £10,000Real gain after 2.9% inflation
Top easy access, 5.00%£500about £210
Average new rate, 3.63%£363about £73
A poor account at 2.00%£200about minus £73

Now the decision itself. If Bank Rate rises by 0.25 points and your easy access account passes it on in full, which not all do, that is £25 more a year on £10,000. Useful, not life-changing. The gap between a poor account and a good one is £300 a year on the same money, which is twelve times the size of the decision.

Fixed-rate bonds work differently. Providers price them off what the market expects Bank Rate to be over the term, not off today's rate. The three dissenting votes in July were public on 30 July, and the market has been pricing a possible rise since. A hike on 17 September would confirm what fixes already assume; a surprise hold could see them drift down.

Tax matters too. Interest above your Personal Savings Allowance is taxable, and at 5% a basic-rate taxpayer with £20,000 in an ordinary account is already at the £1,000 allowance. We covered the mechanics in the savings interest tax guide linked below.

3. Mortgages: trackers move, fixes already have

A tracker or standard variable rate mortgage follows Bank Rate directly, usually from the next payment. A fixed rate does not change until the deal ends, and new fixes are priced off swap rates, which react to expectations rather than announcements.

On a £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years, a 0.25 point move is worth about £29 a month, on our arithmetic:

RateMonthly payment
4.25%£1,083
4.50%£1,112
4.75%£1,140
5.00%£1,169

Those are illustrative rates, not quotes. The point is the shape: each quarter point is roughly £28 to £29 a month on that loan, and each is small next to the difference between the rate you are on and the rate you could be on.

If your fix ends within six months, most lenders let you lock a new rate now and switch to a cheaper one if rates fall before completion. That is the one genuinely useful move available before 17 September, because it costs nothing to hold the option.

4. A worked example in Reading

Tom in Reading has £10,000 in an easy access account paying 2.0% and a £200,000 tracker at 4.50%.

If the Bank holds: nothing changes. Tom's savings still earn £200 a year and lose value against 2.9% inflation. Moving to a 5.00% account, if he qualifies for it, earns £500. The £300 gain is available on 4 September, no decision required.

If the Bank raises to 4%: his tracker goes to 4.75% and his payment rises from £1,112 to £1,140, so £28.57 a month, £343 a year. His 2.0% account might go to 2.25%, worth £25. The hike costs him £318 net a year. Switching the savings account alone would nearly cover it.

The lesson is not about predicting the MPC. Tom's biggest number is the one he controls.

5. What to watch on 16 and 17 September

The ONS publishes August's inflation figure at 7am on Wednesday 16 September. If it is above 2.9%, the case for a fifth vote to hike strengthens overnight. The MPC announcement follows at noon on Thursday 17 September, with the vote split published alongside it. A 5 to 4 hold would tell you as much about November as a 6 to 3 hold told you about September.

The other date is 21 October, when September's inflation figure arrives. That number sets benefit uprating and feeds the state pension triple lock, which we cover separately.

6. The honest verdict

Do not wait for the decision to sort your savings. The difference between a poor account and a good one is around £300 a year on £10,000. The decision is worth £25.

If your fix ends by March, lock a rate now and keep the right to switch. That is the only free option on the table.

If you are on a tracker, budget for £29 a month per £200,000 and hope you do not need it. There is nothing to buy here, and nobody can tell you the vote.

Frequently asked

When is the next Bank of England interest rate decision? Thursday 17 September 2026, announced at noon. The Bank's website lists it as the next due date.

What was the last vote? At the meeting ending 29 July 2026 the MPC voted 6 to 3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%. Three members voted for a rise to 4%.

Will my savings rate go up if Bank Rate rises? Variable and easy access rates usually rise, but providers decide how much to pass on and when. Fixed-rate accounts already price expectations, so they may not move at all on the day.

Should I fix my mortgage before 17 September? That is a personal decision and we are not advisers. The factual point is that most lenders let you secure a rate up to six months before your deal ends and switch if a cheaper one appears, which removes most of the timing risk.
