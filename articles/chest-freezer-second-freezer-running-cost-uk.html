A Second Freezer Costs £43 To £62 A Year Before You Put Anything In It. Most Households Never Save That Back.

The case for a second freezer is always the same: buy in bulk, cook in batches, waste less. All three are real, and all three have to clear a bar that the freezer's own energy label sets before they save a penny. We took two current Bosch label figures, priced them at the October 2026 cap, added the purchase, and worked out what a household has to save on food for the freezer to be a saving rather than a cost. Then we added the garage.

1. The label figures and what they cost to run

Every freezer sold in the UK carries an energy label with an annual consumption in kilowatt hours, measured under a standard test at a standard room temperature. It is the only number you need. We took two current freestanding Bosch models from the manufacturer's own product pages, read on 4 September 2026, because Bosch publishes the figures in a form that can be checked.

Two things to notice. The big freezer costs only 43 per cent more to run than the small one while holding nearly three times as much, so if you are buying a second freezer at all, a bigger one is cheaper per litre. And a label figure is measured at 25 degrees ambient with the door shut; a freezer in a warm kitchen that is opened often will use more, and one in a cool room less.

Chest freezers are not in the table because Bosch does not publish UK chest freezer figures in the same checkable form. A chest freezer of similar volume generally uses less than an upright, because cold air is denser than warm and stays in the box when the lid is lifted, and because chest freezers are usually manual defrost. Treat the tall freezer's £62 as a ceiling for a 250 litre chest and the small one's £43 as about right for a 100 litre chest, then check the label on the one you are looking at.

The Energy Saving Trust's appliance blog, updated 3 August 2026, puts fridges and freezers at around 13 per cent of a typical household's energy bill and gives a typical lifespan of 17 years. A second freezer is therefore a decision you make once and pay for two hundred times.

Running cost is not the whole cost. The freezer has to be bought, and its price spread over its life is part of every year's bill. The break-even column is the article. A second freezer is a saving only if it lets you save more than £1 to £2 a week on food that you would not otherwise have saved, every week, for its whole life. For a household that already meal-plans and shops carefully, that margin does not exist. For a household that throws away a bag of food a week, it does, and the freezer is not the thing saving it; the habit is.

The free inherited freezer is the trap in the last row. It costs nothing to acquire and more than the new one to own, because a 2011 freezer can easily use double the label figure of a 2026 one. If you are given an old freezer, measure it with a plug-in monitor for a week before you commit to keeping it.

3. What batch cooking and bulk buying actually have to save

The freezer enables three things, and each has an honest value.

Bulk buying. A larger pack of meat, fish or frozen vegetables is usually cheaper per kilo than a smaller one, sometimes by 20 to 30 per cent. A household spending £30 a week on freezable food and getting 20 per cent off it saves £6 a week, which clears the bar comfortably. A household spending £10 a week on freezable food saves £2, which just about clears it. Work out your own freezable spend; it is usually less than people think, because bread, milk, fruit and most vegetables are not going in.

Batch cooking. Cooking four portions of a stew uses very little more energy than cooking two, and the Energy Saving Trust's cooking comparison says an electric oven is best used full. The energy saving from batching is real but small, a few pence a meal; the bigger saving is the takeaway or ready meal that the frozen portion replaces on a tired Thursday. If the freezer stops one £15 takeaway a month, that is £3.50 a week and the freezer has paid. If it does not change what you eat on Thursday, it has not.

Waste. Food thrown away is money thrown away, and a freezer that catches the half loaf, the leftover curry and the bananas going brown is doing something a fridge cannot. But it only counts if the food comes back out and is eaten. A freezer that fills with good intentions and is defrosted every two years into the bin is a £60 a year cupboard.

The test is a month's honesty. Before buying, keep a note for four weeks of what you would have bought in bulk, batched, or saved from the bin if you had the space. Price it. If the total is under £5, the freezer will not pay; if it is over £10, it will.

4. The garage, the old freezer, and the empty freezer

The garage. Most freezers are rated for a climate class printed on the label: SN for 10 to 32 degrees, N for 16 to 32, ST for 16 to 38, T for 16 to 43. A freezer rated N in an unheated garage that drops to 5 degrees in January can stop its compressor running for long spells, because the thermostat on a fridge-freezer thinks the fridge is cold enough, and let the freezer compartment warm; a freezer-only unit copes better but is still outside its rating. A utility room that reaches 35 degrees behind a tumble dryer pushes the compressor to run constantly and can add a third to the label figure. Read the climate class before choosing the room, and if the garage is the only option, buy a freezer explicitly rated for it.

The old freezer. A freezer past fifteen years old is not just inefficient; it is also the one most likely to fail in August with £200 of food inside. The Energy Saving Trust's 17 year lifespan is a median, not a promise. If you are running an old one as your second, the honest sum includes a slice of that food loss.

The empty freezer. A freezer's energy use does not fall much when it is empty; the compressor is cooling the air and the walls, not the food, and an empty freezer loses more cold every time the door opens because there is nothing to hold it. If the second freezer is half empty most of the year, its cost per litre of food stored doubles. Fill the gaps with bottles of water if it has to stay on, or switch it off and defrost it between the big shops.

Defrosting and seals. A manual-defrost freezer with a centimetre of ice on the walls is working harder than it needs to; defrost it when the ice is thick. A door seal that no longer grips a piece of paper is letting warm air in continuously and costs pounds a year; seals are cheap and usually a push fit.

5. Worked example: the Hussain family in Oldham

Family of five, fridge freezer in the kitchen already full, considering a second freezer for the garage, which drops to about 4 degrees in winter

Mrs Hussain cooks from scratch and buys meat from a wholesale butcher, where a 5 kg pack is about a quarter cheaper per kilo than the supermarket. They currently buy weekly because there is no room to freeze more.

The saving available. About £35 a week on meat and freezable staples, 25 per cent off in bulk: £8.75 a week, £455 a year, if every bulk pack is used. Plus the occasional saved takeaway. This is a household that clears the bar easily.

The freezer. A 242 litre tall freezer at an assumed £350: running £62 a year on the label, purchase £29 a year over twelve years, true cost £91 a year. Net saving about £360 a year; payback on the purchase about ten months.

The garage. The freezer they liked is class N, rated down to 16 degrees. At 4 degrees in January it is outside its rating and the retailer will not honour the warranty for garage use. They choose instead a model the manufacturer rates for outbuildings, which costs a little more, and accept that its label figure will be beaten in winter and exceeded in a hot August.

Decision. Buy it, the garage-rated one, and keep the four-week honesty note going for the first three months to check the bulk packs are actually being used. For this family the freezer is one of the better purchases in this series. For the couple next door who eat out twice a week and buy two chicken breasts at a time, the same freezer would be £91 a year for nothing.

6. The honest verdict

A second freezer costs £43 to £62 a year to run on current label figures at the October 2026 cap, and £55 to £100 a year once the purchase is spread over its life. Buy one only if you can name, in pounds per week, the bulk discount, the replaced takeaways or the rescued food that will clear that bar, and only if the answer is comfortably over £2 a week.

If you buy, buy the bigger one, because it is cheaper per litre; check the climate class against the room; and do not take the free fifteen-year-old freezer from a relative without metering it first, because it can cost double. For a household that already wastes little and shops carefully, the honest answer is not to buy one at all.

Frequently asked

How much does a chest freezer cost to run per year in the UK? Check the kWh figure on its label and multiply by 26.32p, the October 2026 cap rate. Bosch's 242 litre upright is rated at 234 kWh, about £62 a year; its 83 litre under-counter model at 164 kWh, about £43. A chest freezer of similar volume is usually a little below the upright figure.

Is a second freezer worth it? Only if the bulk buying, batch cooking or avoided waste it enables saves more than its true cost, which is £55 to £100 a year once the purchase is spread over twelve years. That is £1 to £2 a week, every week. Households buying meat in bulk clear it easily; households that already waste little usually do not.

Can I put a freezer in the garage? Only if its climate class allows it. Class N freezers are rated for 16 to 32 degrees and can misbehave below that; class SN goes down to 10. A garage that drops to 4 degrees in winter needs a model the manufacturer specifically rates for outbuildings, or the warranty may not apply.

Does an empty freezer use less electricity? Not much. The compressor cools the air and the cabinet, not the food, and an empty freezer loses more cold each time the door opens. If it is going to stay half empty, fill the space with bottles of water or switch it off between big shops.
