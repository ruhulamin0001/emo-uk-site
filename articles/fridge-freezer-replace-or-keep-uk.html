Should You Replace A Working Fridge Freezer To Save Electricity? Measure It First. The Payback Is Usually Longer Than The Fridge.

The fridge freezer runs 24 hours a day for about 17 years, so it is the one appliance where an efficiency gap really compounds. That makes it the favourite example of people selling new ones. We took the annual consumption from a current spec sheet, priced it at the Ofgem October 2026 rate, and worked out how much your old one has to be using before a £450 replacement pays back before it dies. The answer is: more than most of them do.

1. What a new one actually costs to run

Every fridge freezer sold in the UK carries an energy label with an annual consumption figure in kWh, measured under a standard test. That figure is the one to use, because it is the only running-cost number that comes from a test rather than a marketing department.

We took a current mid-range model as the reference: a Hotpoint 317-litre freestanding 50/50 frost-free fridge freezer, class E on the current label, with a stated annual consumption of 258 kWh. At the Ofgem cap rate of 26.32p per kWh for 1 October to 31 December 2026 that is £67.91 a year, or about 19p a day. Hotpoint gives ten years on parts and one year on labour.

For context, the Energy Saving Trust's appliance guide, updated 3 August 2026, gives two figures that between them make the most important point in this article: an F-rated 424-litre fridge freezer costs about £80 a year to run in Great Britain, and a G-rated 265-litre one costs about £70. The letter on the label went down a grade and the running cost went down too, because the volume fell by a third. Capacity is at least as important as class. A family buying the biggest A-rated American-style unit they can find will very likely spend more on electricity than they did with the old under-counter pair.

2. What your old one might cost, and why you cannot know without measuring

There is no reliable published figure for "an old fridge freezer", and we are not going to make one up. The label figure on a 2008 model told you what it used when it was new, in a test room at 25 degrees. What it uses now depends on the door seals, the dust on the condenser coils, whether the freezer has a centimetre of ice in it, how warm your kitchen is, and whether the thermostat still works. Two fridges of the same age and model can differ by a factor of two.

So the only honest method is to measure. A plug-in energy monitor sits between the plug and the socket and counts kWh. Leave it on for seven days, because a fridge cycles and a single afternoon tells you nothing, multiply the reading by 52, and you have your annual figure. The monitor costs a few pounds; the fridge you might otherwise buy costs hundreds.

To give the payback table something to work with, we use three illustrative old-fridge figures, and we label them as ours: 350 kWh for a well-kept older unit, 500 kWh for a tired one with worn seals, and 600 kWh for a faulty one running much of the day. If your monitor says something else, use that instead.

3. The payback table: purchase price against the gap

Payback is the purchase price divided by the annual saving, where the saving is the gap between what the old fridge uses and what the new one is rated at. We assume a purchase price of £450, which is the sort of money the reference model and its competitors cost; we are not quoting a live price because it will have changed by the time you read this. The break-even, if you want a rule, is roughly this: at £450 and a 17-year life, the new fridge needs to save about £26.50 a year just to return its purchase price by the day it dies, which is a gap of about 100 kWh. Anything less than that and you have paid £450 to lose money slowly. To pay back in a reasonable five years you need a gap of about 340 kWh, meaning the old unit uses around 600 kWh, which is a fridge with a problem, not a fridge that is merely old.

There is a further cost the table leaves out: the old fridge freezer still works, and it has a resale or reuse value of at least something, while the new one has a delivery charge and a disposal charge unless the retailer includes recycling. Both push the payback longer.

4. When it does die: size, rating and where you put it

When the old one finally fails, the running-cost decision is mostly made in the first ten seconds of shopping, when you choose the size. The EST's advice is to buy the smallest model that meets your needs with the highest rating you can afford, in that order. A 265-litre G costs less to run than a 424-litre F; a 317-litre E at 258 kWh sits between them.

Three things then matter more than the brand. First, frost-free models save you defrosting but use fans and heaters to do it, which is part of why the label figures are not lower than they are. Second, where it sits: a fridge beside the oven or in direct sun runs harder, and a freezer in an unheated garage in winter can behave badly unless the model is rated for it, as the reference Hotpoint is down to minus 15 degrees. Third, the warranty. Ten years on parts, as Hotpoint offers, matters on a machine you expect to keep for seventeen.

Do not pay for an extended warranty on a fridge freezer on running-cost grounds; it changes nothing about the electricity. And do not be talked into a bigger unit on the promise that it is "A-rated so it is cheaper". Read the kWh figure on the label, divide by 100, multiply by 26.32, and that is your annual pounds. It takes ten seconds and it is the only number the shop cannot spin.

5. Worked example: Ellen in Doncaster

Fourteen-year-old fridge freezer, still cold, kitchen gets the afternoon sun

Ellen has a fridge freezer bought in 2012. It works, the seals look tired, and the freezer needs defrosting twice a year. A retailer's website tells her a new model would "save up to £100 a year". She buys a plug-in monitor instead and leaves it on for a week.

The reading: 8.9 kWh in seven days. That is 463 kWh a year, about £122 at 26.32p. Against the reference model's 258 kWh, the gap is 205 kWh, a saving of £54 a year. On a £450 replacement that is a payback of 8.3 years, inside the 17-year life but only just past the halfway point.

What she does first. She defrosts the freezer, vacuums the dust off the coils at the back, and moves the unit a foot along the wall out of the afternoon sun. Then she measures again: 7.4 kWh in seven days, 385 kWh a year, £101. The gap to a new one is now 127 kWh, £33 a year, and the payback stretches to 13.6 years. An afternoon's cleaning saved her £21 a year and made the new fridge a much worse idea.

Her decision. Keep it. Put the £450 in a savings account earmarked for the day it dies, and when that day comes buy a 250 to 300-litre model with the lowest kWh figure on the label rather than the biggest one that fits the gap. If the monitor had said 12 kWh a week, 624 kWh a year, the payback would have been five years and the answer would have been to replace it now.

6. The honest verdict

Do not replace a working fridge freezer to save electricity unless a monitor has told you it is using roughly twice what a new one is rated at. Below that, the payback is longer than the new fridge's life, and you are spending £450 to save £20 or 30 a year. Clean the coils, defrost it, and keep it.

When it fails, buy small and well-rated, in that order, and read the kWh on the label rather than the letter. The only fridge freezer that pays to replace early is a faulty one, and the £10 monitor is how you find out whether yours is.

Frequently asked

How much does a fridge freezer cost to run per year in the UK? A current 317-litre model rated at 258 kWh costs about £68 a year at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p. The Energy Saving Trust quotes £70 to £80 for typical F and G-rated models. Old or faulty units can be double that.

Is it worth replacing a 15-year-old fridge freezer? Only if it is using around 500 kWh a year or more, which you can check with a plug-in monitor over a week. At 350 kWh the payback on a £450 replacement is about 19 years, longer than the new one will last.

Does a bigger A-rated fridge use less than a smaller G-rated one? Often not. The EST's own example has a 424-litre F-rated model costing more to run than a 265-litre G-rated one. Compare the kWh figure on the label, not the letter.

How long do fridge freezers last? About 17 years typically, according to the Energy Saving Trust, which is why a 19-year payback is a loss.
