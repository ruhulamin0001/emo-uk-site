Your Driveway Can Earn £1,000 Tax Free. The Platform Telling You That Is Measuring It Wrong

JustPark's own help centre tells space owners to add up the payments that landed in their bank account. HMRC's own guidance says the £1,000 test is measured on the money before any fees come off. Those are two different numbers, and since January the platform has been sending HMRC the bigger one.

The short version

  • The allowance is £1,000 a year, and it is the property allowance, not Rent a Room. The £7,500 Rent a Room threshold covers furnished accommodation in your home. A driveway is not accommodation, so you get £1,000, per GOV.UK.
  • HMRC measures it gross. Your platform tells you to measure it net. HMRC defines gross income as the total "before any allowances or expenses are taken off". JustPark's help centre says the allowance "is from the earnings that you have received directly into your bank, after any fees are deducted". Both pages read 29 August 2026. They cannot both be right, and HMRC writes the rules.
  • There is no small-seller exemption for a driveway. The 30-transactions-or-€2,000 let-off applies to goods only. JustPark's own help page says that because parking is "rental of immovable property", it "has to report anyone who has received at least one booking since 2024".
  • Two thresholds nobody quotes: gross above £1,000 means you must contact HMRC. Gross above £2,500 means you must register for Self Assessment. That second line works out at roughly £209 a month of bookings.
  • If you already have a buy-to-let, do not touch the £1,000 allowance without advice. GOV.UK: claim the property allowance in one property business and you "may not claim actual expenses in respect of the other business".
  • The sums are small and we are not going to pretend otherwise. On the worked example below, a host who crosses the line owes £4 in tax. The paperwork is the problem, not the bill.

1. The number that counts is not the number in your bank

Every guide to renting out a driveway says the same comforting thing: the first £1,000 a year is tax free. That is true. It is the property allowance, worth up to £1,000 of gross property income a year since 6 April 2017.

The problem is how you are told to count to £1,000.

JustPark's help centre article "How much tax do I pay for earnings from my space?", read on 29 August 2026, tells space owners this:

"The £1,000 allowance is from the earnings that you have received directly into your bank, after any fees are deducted. The best way to view this would be to view a full list of all incoming payments to your bank from JustPark and filter this by the tax year dates."

Now here is HMRC, on the page JustPark links to from that same article:

"Gross income means the total amount you would put on your tax return before any allowances or expenses are taken off."

A platform commission is an expense. So HMRC's £1,000 test runs on what the driver paid you, and JustPark's instruction runs on what survived the commission and reached your current account. On a 3% fee those numbers are about 3% apart, which sounds like nothing until your bookings land between £1,000 and £1,031 for the year. In that band you look compliant on your bank statement and you are not. Whatever you earn, the figure that decides your obligations is always higher than the figure you actually received, and nobody is showing you the higher one prominently.

The same JustPark article also calls this "the property trading income allowance". There is no such thing. GOV.UK has two separate £1,000 allowances, and confirms that "if you have both types of income, you'll get a £1,000 allowance for each". Driveway rent is property income. Your eBay flipping, if you do any, is trading income with its own separate £1,000. Merging the names invites people to assume one pot.

2. Why there is no small-seller exemption for a driveway

Since 1 January 2024, UK online platforms have had to collect information about the people earning through them and send it to both HMRC and the individual by the following 31 January. Most people have heard about the exemption: platforms need not report you if you make under €2,000 (roughly £1,700) and complete fewer than 30 transactions in a year. Vast numbers of casual sellers assume they are safely under it.

That exemption is for goods. It does not cover services, vehicle rental, or the rental of immovable property. This is not our reading. It is JustPark's, stated on their own HMRC reporting page:

"Because JustPark falls under the rental of immovable property category, this reporting exception does not apply to our platform or our space owners. This means we have to report anyone who has received at least one booking since 2024."

One booking. Not thirty. Not €2,000. One.

So JustPark now requires every host to submit their full legal name, date of birth, residential address and National Insurance number, then pass a photo ID check run by Mitek on JustPark's behalf. You get seven days from the Mitek request before it expires. Miss it and JustPark says it may withhold payouts, restrict withdrawals and suspend your hosting.

To be fair to the platforms, none of this changes what tax you owe. The Low Incomes Tax Reform Group is blunt about that: these rules "do not create new tax obligations for individuals". What changed is that HMRC now sees your gross figure and your National Insurance number, whether you earned £40 or £4,000.

3. What it actually pays, and what the platforms do not publish

The honest position: there is no independently audited figure for what a UK driveway earns. What exists is marketing. JustPark's landing page promises you can "earn up to £1,000 a year tax free, with no earnings cap" and claims more than 65,000 space owners. "Up to" is doing all the work there, and the £1,000 is not a JustPark achievement, it is a statutory allowance that applies whatever platform you use.

Fees are where the two big platforms genuinely differ, and only one of them tells you the number.

YourParkingSpace publishes its structure plainly: a 20% uplift on top of the price you set, paid by the driver, plus a 3% processing fee deducted from your earnings monthly. Set £100 and the driver is quoted £120 while you are credited £97. That 20% is not costless to you even though you never pay it, because it is 20% on the price a driver compares against the council car park down the road.

JustPark does not publish a percentage. Its FAQ answers "How much does JustPark charge?" with "We take a small service fee from each booking" and no figure. A 3% standard commission is widely reported in the personal finance press and matches what hosts describe, but we could not find it stated on any JustPark page read on 29 August 2026, so treat it as reported rather than published. That difference in transparency is itself a data point.

Location does the rest. A driveway near a commuter station, a hospital, a stadium or an airport is a real asset. One on a quiet street with free kerbside parking is worth close to nothing, and no listing optimisation changes that. Both platforms quote you a location-specific figure before you commit, which costs nothing and is the only number worth trusting.

4. Worked example: the Hussain driveway in St Albans

Tanvir Hussain has a second parking space on his driveway in St Albans, about eight minutes' walk from the station. He lets it as a monthly commuter space at £85 a month and it is taken all year.

What he sees:

  • Gross bookings: £85 × 12 = £1,020
  • Commission at 3%: £30.60
  • Paid into his bank: £989.40

Following JustPark's own instruction, Tanvir filters his bank statements, totals £989.40, sees that it is under £1,000 and does nothing further. Reasonable. Also wrong.

His gross property income is £1,020. GOV.UK is specific about what that triggers: gross property income "over £1,000 up to £2,500" means you must contact HMRC. He has a notification obligation he does not know he has, and JustPark has already sent HMRC the £1,020.

What he actually owes. He takes partial relief: £1,020 minus the £1,000 allowance leaves £20 taxable. At the basic rate that is £4.00. At the higher rate, £8.00.

Four pounds. We are not going to dress that up as a crisis, because it is not one. The point is the mismatch, not the money.

Now scale it up. Suppose the space is better placed and goes for £150 a month, so £1,800 gross and £54 of commission. Tanvir now has a genuine choice:

  • Claim the £1,000 allowance: £1,800 minus £1,000 leaves £800 taxable. Tax at 20% is £160.
  • Deduct actual expenses instead: his only real cost is the £54 commission, so £1,746 is taxable. Tax at 20% is £349.20.

The allowance wins by £189.20, and it will almost always win for a driveway, because a driveway has essentially no running costs. You cannot do both. GOV.UK is explicit that if you use the allowance you cannot deduct any other expenses or allowances.

He is still under £2,500, so he contacts HMRC rather than registering for Self Assessment. Divide that £2,500 ceiling by twelve and you get the threshold nobody publishes: a space earning more than about £209 a month gross puts you into Self Assessment. Below roughly £84 a month you are inside the allowance entirely and have nothing to report at all.

5. Three things that stop this before tax ever matters

Permission. JustPark's own FAQ puts the condition plainly: "If you own the space or have permission from your landlord, you're free to list it." A tenancy may forbid subletting any part of the property. A lease very often restricts what a parking space can be used for and by whom, and a managing agent can and will enforce it. Read the document before you list, not after a neighbour complains.

Planning. There is no national permitted-development right that says "you may run a commercial parking operation from your front garden". Occasional, small-scale letting is not generally treated as a material change of use, but that is a judgement your local planning authority makes on the facts, and councils have taken enforcement action where a driveway started operating as a car park. Letting one space part-time, you are almost certainly fine. Letting several spaces all day every day near a stadium, ring the council's planning team first. A free phone call is cheaper than an enforcement notice.

Insurance. Your buildings and contents policy is written for your household, not for a stranger's vehicle sitting on your land for ten hours a day. JustPark says it "provides protection for space owners" and verifies drivers with licence plate checks, which is worth having, but it is not your own insurer agreeing to the arrangement. Tell your insurer in writing and keep the reply.

The honest verdict

If your driveway is genuinely near something people need to park next to, this is one of the better small income streams in the UK. No skill, no stock, no hours, and unlike almost every "passive income" pitch online, the asset already exists and you already paid for it.

If your driveway is on an ordinary street with free parking outside, there is nothing here. You will get a handful of bookings a year and the honest advice is not to bother. No listing tricks fix a location problem.

On the tax, keep it in proportion. The worked example owes £4. What deserves ten minutes is not the bill, it is that HMRC now holds a figure for your driveway larger than the one you saw in your bank, supplied by the platform, with your National Insurance number attached, triggered by one booking. Keep your own record of gross bookings rather than bank credits, and if the gross crosses £1,000, tell HMRC. That is the whole job.

There is nothing to buy here.

Frequently asked

Can I use the £7,500 Rent a Room allowance for my driveway? No. GOV.UK describes Rent a Room as letting "furnished accommodation in your home" as a resident landlord. A parking space is not accommodation. Driveway income sits under the £1,000 property allowance instead. If you have a lodger as well, note that GOV.UK also says you cannot use the property allowance on income from letting a room under the Rent a Room Scheme, so the two do not stack on the same income.

I have a buy-to-let. Does the £1,000 allowance still help? Be careful, and take advice. GOV.UK states that "if you have two businesses and claim the property allowance in one business you may not claim actual expenses in respect of the other business". Claiming £1,000 of relief on your driveway could cost you the ability to deduct real expenses against your rental property, which for anyone with a mortgage would be a very expensive £1,000. You also cannot use the property allowance at all if you claim the finance-cost tax reducer on a residential let.

Will JustPark reporting me increase my tax bill? No, and the platforms are right about that. The reporting rules changed what HMRC can see, not what you owe. If you were already declaring correctly, nothing changes. If you were not, the chance of HMRC noticing has gone up considerably.

Do I get reported if I only let my space out twice? Yes. Because parking is rental of immovable property rather than a sale of goods, the small-seller exemption does not apply, and JustPark states it must report anyone with at least one booking since 2024. Being reported is not the same as owing tax. If your gross for the year is under £1,000, you owe nothing and have nothing to tell HMRC.
