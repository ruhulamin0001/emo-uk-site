Washing At 30 Instead Of 40 Saves About 8p A Wash, Or £17.60 A Year, For Free

This is the only saving on this site that costs nothing at all, needs no purchase, and can be made this afternoon. It is also modest, and the honest version comes with two exceptions that matter more than the money.

The short version

  • Roughly 90 per cent of the electricity in a wash cycle heats the water. The motor, the pump and the electronics are almost a rounding error.
  • So the temperature setting is the cycle's cost. Dropping the target from 40 to 30 cuts the heating work by about a third.
  • A 0.90 kWh 40 degree cycle becomes about 0.58 kWh at 30. At 26.32p that is 8.42p a wash saved.
  • Over 220 cycles a year, the UK labelling basis, that is £17.60 a year. Over a machine's ten year life, £176.
  • Two exceptions. Towels, bedding and anything from an illness need 60 degrees. And a machine washed only cold builds a bacterial film, so run one 60 degree maintenance cycle a month with the drum empty.

1. Why the temperature dial is the only expensive control

A washing machine does four things that use electricity: it fills, it heats, it turns the drum, and it spins. Three of those are cheap.

The drum motor on a modern machine draws a few hundred watts intermittently across a cycle lasting an hour or two. The pump runs for a few minutes. The electronics draw a handful of watts. Together they account for a small fraction of the total.

Heating the water is almost all of it. Raising roughly ten litres of water from a mains temperature of around 12 degrees up to 40 takes about 0.33 kWh of pure heat, and the element does that with a 2kW to 3kW draw. Raising the same water to 30 takes about 0.21 kWh. The difference is the whole saving.

This is why the eco cycle is long. It compensates for a lower temperature with more time and more mechanical agitation, both of which are cheap, in order to avoid heat, which is expensive. A two and a half hour eco wash at 40 uses less electricity than a one hour quick wash at 40, which is counter-intuitive until you know what the meter is actually paying for.

It is also why the arithmetic in our page on repairing or replacing a washing machine concludes that a new A-rated machine only saves about £26 a year. The machine is not where the money is. The dial is.

3. The two cases where 30 degrees is the wrong answer

One: hygiene loads. Bedding, towels, underwear, cloths, and anything at all from someone who has been ill. Public health guidance is consistent that 60 degrees is the temperature at which laundry is reliably disinfected, and a 30 degree wash does not get there. This is not a place to save 8p. If someone in the house has a wound, a skin infection, or works in healthcare or food, wash their things at 60 and do not think about it again.

Two: the machine itself. A machine that only ever runs cold develops a biofilm in the drum, the door seal and the detergent drawer. The symptoms are a musty smell, black spotting on the rubber gasket, and washing that comes out smelling worse than it went in. This is now common enough that manufacturers put a drum clean cycle on the panel.

The fix is trivial. Run one empty cycle at 60 degrees or above once a month, with either a machine cleaner or a cup of soda crystals, and wipe the door seal dry after use. At 36.85p a time that is £4.42 a year, which leaves you well ahead on the £18 you saved and removes the whole problem.

There is a third, milder case. Heavily soiled work clothes, oily kitchen textiles and cloth nappies wash better warm, and a load that has to go round twice at 30 has cost you more than doing it once at 40. Judge the load, not the rule.

4. The other free changes, ranked by what they are worth

Once the dial is down, the remaining laundry savings are all behavioural and none of them cost anything.

Fill the drum properly. A cycle costs almost the same whether the drum is half full or full, because the water volume and heating work barely change. Two half loads a week instead of one full one is roughly £16 a year thrown away. The old rule still works: you should be able to fit a flat hand between the top of the load and the drum.

Use the eco programme and accept that it is long. It is longer precisely because it is cheaper. Set it going when you leave the house.

Use the right amount of detergent. Overdosing does not clean better, it leaves residue that feeds the biofilm and it wastes money on the detergent itself. Dose for your water hardness, which is on your water company's website, not for the biggest number on the packet.

Spin faster before drying. This is the big one and almost nobody thinks of it. A higher spin speed costs a few pence more and removes considerably more water, which means far less work for whatever dries the clothes. If you use a tumble dryer or a heated airer, the drying stage costs several times what the wash does, so spending 2p on a faster spin to save 20p of drying is the best trade in the laundry room.

Dry outside when you can. Free, and it also keeps several litres of water a load out of the indoor air, which is the main cause of the condensation problem covered in our page on dehumidifier running costs.

5. Worked example: Marisol in Swansea

Marisol runs five washes a week, so 260 a year, all at 40 degrees on the standard cotton programme because that is what the dial was set to when the machine arrived.

Her machine's plate figure for a 40 degree cotton cycle is 0.95 kWh. That is 24.99p a wash, or £64.97 a year.

She switches the everyday loads to 30 degrees eco, at about 0.61 kWh, which is 16.06p. She keeps two loads a week at 60 for towels and bedding, at 1.42 kWh, which is 37.37p.

New pattern: 156 washes at 30 degrees, so £25.05. Plus 104 washes at 60 degrees, so £38.86. Total £63.91.

That is a saving of only £1.06 a year, and the reason is that she was under-washing her towels before and is now doing it properly. The honest accounting is that she has bought better hygiene for a pound rather than saved eighteen.

So she looks at the other levers. She had been running three-quarter loads; consolidating to four fuller washes a week plus the two hot ones takes her to 208 cycles and £51.14. She turns the spin up from 1,000 to 1,400rpm, adding about 0.6p a wash but cutting her heated airer time by roughly a fifth, worth about £9 over the winter.

Total: about £23 a year, none of it spent on anything. The temperature dial was the smallest of the three changes, which is the useful thing to learn.

6. The honest verdict

Set the everyday wash to 30 degrees on the eco programme. It saves about 8p a wash and £17.60 to £18.50 a year, it costs nothing, and modern detergents are designed for it.

Keep towels, bedding and anything from an illness at 60. The 8p is not worth having that argument with yourself.

Run one empty 60 degree cycle a month. At £4.42 a year it prevents the smell that cold washing causes, and it is the difference between this being a saving and being a false economy.

And if you want a bigger number than £18, look at the drying rather than the washing. That is where the money in this room actually is.

Frequently asked

Do clothes actually get clean at 30 degrees? For normal everyday soiling, yes. Biological detergents have been reformulated around lower temperatures for years and their enzymes work well at 30 and reasonably at 20. What lower temperatures do not do is disinfect, which is a different job from cleaning.

Is the quick wash cheaper because it is shorter? No, usually the opposite. A quick wash compensates for having less time by using more water and reaching temperature faster, so it often draws more electricity than a longer eco cycle at the same temperature. Length is not cost.

What is the mains water temperature and does it matter? In the UK it varies roughly between 8 degrees in winter and 18 in summer. It matters because it sets how far the element has to lift the water, so the same cycle genuinely costs more in January than in July. The figures here assume 12 degrees.

Does washing at 30 damage the machine? Not directly, but it allows the biofilm described above to establish, which then damages the door seal and can eventually block the drain. The monthly hot maintenance cycle is the whole answer and it takes no effort.
