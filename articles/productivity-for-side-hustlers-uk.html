Getting a Side Hustle Done Around a Full Time Job

21 July 2026 · 5 min read · Checked July 2026

The short version
  • A full time job plus a side hustle is not a time management problem. It is a capacity problem, and most advice pretends otherwise.
  • The realistic budget is 6 to 10 hours a week, not the 20 the internet suggests. Plan for the number you can actually sustain in February, not the one you feel capable of in January.
  • Check your employment contract first. Many UK contracts contain exclusivity or conflict of interest clauses. Since 2015 exclusivity clauses are unenforceable in zero hours contracts, but not in ordinary ones.
  • Two properly protected blocks a week beats seven scattered evenings. Consistency beats intensity, and it is the only version that survives a bad month.
  • If it is costing you sleep, it is costing you your day job and your health. That is a bad trade, and no amount of side income fixes it.

1. Check this before you start

Do this before you spend a penny or an hour.

  • Read your employment contract. Look for exclusivity, conflict of interest, and intellectual property clauses. Some require you to declare outside work, some prohibit competing work, and some claim ownership of anything you create during your employment.
  • The IP clause is the one that catches people. If you are a developer and your contract assigns work created in the course of employment, building software in your evenings can get complicated. Do not build in the same domain as your employer without checking.
  • Never use work equipment, work email or work time. That is the fastest route to a disciplinary, and it is entirely avoidable.
  • Exclusivity clauses in zero hours contracts have been unenforceable since 2015. In ordinary contracts they can be enforceable.

Most employers are fine with unrelated outside work. The problem is almost never the side hustle, it is not having checked.

2. The honest time arithmetic

Here is a week. 168 hours.

Eating, cooking, washing up10
Washing, dressing, household admin7

Remaining46

That 46 hours is where family, friends, exercise, rest and everything that makes life worth living has to fit. Take a partner, children, or a parent who needs help, and it drops fast.

A realistic sustainable side hustle budget is 6 to 10 hours a week. You can do 20 for about six weeks. Then something breaks, usually your sleep or your relationships, and you stop entirely, which is why so many side hustles die at week seven.

Plan for eight hours a week and treat anything above it as a bonus. Eight hours a week is 416 hours a year, which is a great deal of progress if it actually happens every week.

3. Two blocks, protected

Scattered half hours do not work for most creative or building work, because the setup cost is real. Two protected blocks beat seven fragments.

The structure that works for most people:

  • One weekday evening block, 2 to 3 hours, same night every week. Same night matters more than which night, because it stops being a decision.
  • One weekend morning block, 3 to 4 hours, early. Before the day fills up.
  • Loose admin time, 30 to 60 minutes scattered, for the small things: replying, invoicing, listing, posting.

Protect them the way you would protect a shift. In the calendar, declined if something clashes, and told to the people you live with so it is not a surprise every week.

Batch by type, not by project. All the photography in one block. All the writing in another. Switching between kinds of work inside a two hour block wastes a large part of it.

Finish mid task. Stop when you know exactly what the next step is, and write it down. Starting is the expensive part, and a note saying "next: photograph the four blue items" removes it entirely.

4. Worked example: Marcus in Birmingham

Marcus works 9 to 5, has a partner and a four year old. He wanted to build a proofreading side business.

What failed first. He tried an hour every evening after his daughter went to bed. It lasted three weeks. By 9pm he was too tired to do accurate work, which is the entire product in proofreading, and he was making mistakes that took longer to fix than the work took.

What worked.

WhenLengthWhat
Tuesday 7.30 to 9.30pm2 hrsClient work only
Saturday 6.30 to 10am3.5 hrsClient work, then invoicing
Lunch breaks, 3 a week1.5 hrsEmail, quotes, admin only
Total7 hrs

Seven hours a week at an effective £28 an hour is about £196 a week, or £850 a month gross. It took him five months to get there.

The change that mattered was moving the hard work to Saturday morning, when he was rested, and keeping the weekday evening for a single defined task. He did less total time than his failed version and earned considerably more, because the work was accurate.

5. Energy is the real constraint, not time

This is the part that generic productivity advice gets wrong. You do not have eight interchangeable hours. You have a small number of good hours and a lot of tired ones.

Match the work to the state you are actually in.

Weekend morning, restedWriting, learning, anything needing accuracyNothing
Weekday evening, moderateDefined tasks with a clear finishOpen ended creative work, decisions
Late evening, tiredListing, packing, tidying files, light adminAnything a client will read
Lunch breakEmail, quotes, schedulingDeep work, you will not get into it

Protect your sleep. This is not a soft point. Cutting sleep to make time is borrowing from tomorrow at a terrible rate, and the cost lands on your day job, your driving, your mood and the people around you. If the only way the side hustle fits is by sleeping less, the side hustle does not fit yet. Make it smaller instead.

The same applies to exercise and to seeing people. A side project that costs you your health or your relationships has a negative return however much it pays.

6. Tools worth using, and the trap

The trap is obvious once you name it: setting up tools feels like progress and is not progress. A perfectly configured Notion workspace has earned nobody a single pound.

What is genuinely worth having, all free:

  • One list, in one place. Any app, or paper. What matters is that there is exactly one.
  • Calendar blocks for the two sessions, on repeat.
  • A separate bank account for the side income. Free with most app banks, and it makes tax time trivial.
  • A simple time record for the first two months, so you find out your actual hourly rate rather than the one you imagine.
  • Templates for anything you write more than twice: quotes, replies, listings.

Do not buy a course, a planner, or a productivity subscription before you have earned anything. Those are purchases that feel like commitment and function as delay.

7. What to do when it stalls

It will stall. Everyone's does. The question is what you do in that week.

  • Shrink the session, do not skip it. Twenty minutes keeps the habit alive. A skipped week usually becomes a skipped month.
  • Do the easiest task on the list. Momentum matters more than importance when you have stalled.
  • Check whether you are avoiding one specific thing. Usually a stall is one dreaded task, often a difficult message or a price conversation, blocking everything behind it. Name it and do it first.
  • Take one week off deliberately if you are genuinely exhausted. A planned break you return from beats a collapse you do not.

8. When to stop, and that being fine

Almost nothing written about side hustles says this, so it is worth saying plainly.

Stopping is a legitimate outcome. If after six honest months the money is not there, or you dread the sessions, or it is squeezing out things that matter more, stopping is a decision, not a failure. You have learned what the work is actually like, which is worth something.

Signals it is time to stop or change direction:

  • You are consistently sleeping less than seven hours to fit it in
  • Your day job performance has slipped, which risks the income you actually rely on
  • You have been at it six months with no revenue and no clear reason why
  • You dread the blocks rather than look forward to them
  • The people you live with have stopped mentioning it, which is usually not neutral

Changing the hustle is also an option. The skills mostly transfer, and the person who tried three things over two years is usually further ahead than the one who forced the first for two years.

The honest verdict

The realistic version is eight protected hours a week, in two blocks, for at least six months, on something you can do accurately when you are moderately tired.

That will not make you rich quickly and it is not what most content about this promises. What it will do is compound, because eight hours every week for a year beats thirty hours a week for two months and then nothing, which is what most people actually do.

The honest warning: the material benefit of a side hustle is usually a few hundred pounds a month. That is genuinely useful. It is not usually life changing, and it costs you the free time you currently have. Go in knowing the trade, and protect your sleep, your health and the people around you while you make it. If you find your mood or health slipping, that is worth taking seriously and talking to someone about.

Frequently asked

Do I have to tell my employer? Read your contract. Some require declaration, most do not. If it is unrelated to your employer's business and done entirely on your own time and equipment, it is usually fine, but the contract is the answer, not general advice.

Is it better to do a little every day or one big block? For most work, blocks. Daily habits work well for practice based things such as learning a skill, and badly for anything with a long setup.

How long before I know if it is working? Six months of genuine consistent effort. Less than that and you have not given it a fair test. More than twelve with no revenue and no explanation, and it is worth changing direction.

Do I need to register with HMRC straight away? Once your trading income passes the £1,000 trading allowance in a tax year, register for Self Assessment by 5 October following the end of that tax year. General information, not tax advice.
