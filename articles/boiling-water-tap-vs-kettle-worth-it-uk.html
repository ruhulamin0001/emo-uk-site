A Boiling Water Tap Cannot Pay For Itself. Its Standby Alone Costs More Than A Kettle's Entire Year. Buy It For The Kitchen, Or Do Not Buy It.

Boiling water taps are sold, in part, on energy: the claim that keeping a small insulated tank at 100 degrees costs less than repeatedly boiling a kettle. The manufacturer's own standby figure lets us test that claim exactly, and it fails, not narrowly but by a multiple. That does not make the tap a bad product. It makes it a kitchen product, and this article prices it as one, with the running cost, the filter, the installation and the one household where the sum comes closest.

The short version

  • Quooker states its tap's standby consumption at 10 W, on its own energy page. That is 0.24 kWh a day, 87.6 kWh a year, and £23 a year at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p, before a single cup is drawn.
  • Boiling a litre of water from 15 degrees takes 0.099 kWh of heat. A kettle at about 90 per cent efficiency draws 0.11 kWh, 2.9p; the tap's tank must add the same heat to replace what you draw, so the per-litre cost is similar. The standby is the difference.
  • A household making eight mugs of tea and coffee a day boils about 2 litres: £21 a year by kettle, £21 plus £23 standby by tap. The tap costs roughly double to run.
  • The only energy a tap saves is the water people overfill, which the Energy Saving Trust puts at £10 a year. That is less than half the standby.
  • Add the filter cartridge the tank needs, typically changed yearly, and a four-figure purchase, and the payback on a boiling water tap bought to save money is never, in any household we can construct.
  • What it actually buys: a clear worktop, no waiting, safer than a full kettle for some households, and a filtered chilled option on the combined models. Those are real. They are not savings.

1. The standby figure, and what it costs before you draw a cup

A boiling water tap is a small vacuum-insulated tank under the sink, held at or near 100 degrees all day, with a tap on top. The tank loses heat, however good the insulation, and an element replaces it. The rate at which it does so is the standby consumption, and Quooker, the market leader, publishes it on its own energy page: "the Quooker's standby power consumption is 10 W, about the same as a WiFi router." We read that on 4 September 2026. It is the manufacturer's figure, from a party with an interest in it being low, and it is the figure this article uses.

Ten watts, twenty-four hours a day, is 0.24 kWh a day and 87.6 kWh a year. At the Ofgem price cap unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p per kWh, that is £23.06 a year. It is spent whether you make one cup or fifty, on holiday, overnight, every day the tank is switched on.

A kettle's standby is zero. It draws nothing until the switch is pressed. So before either appliance has boiled a drop, the tap is £23 a year behind, and the question is whether it can make that up on the water it boils.

Other brands' standby figures vary and several do not publish one; a tank without vacuum insulation can lose several times as much. If a tap you are considering does not state its standby in watts, assume it is higher than Quooker's, because a lower figure would be on the box.

2. Boiling a litre: kettle against tank

Water needs 4.186 kilojoules per litre per degree. Taking a litre from a winter mains temperature of 15 degrees to 100 takes 356 kJ, which is 0.099 kWh of heat. That is fixed by physics and is the same for a kettle and a tank.

The kettle loses a little heat to its own body and to the air while boiling, and a typical figure for a modern kettle is about 90 per cent of the electricity ending up in the water, so a litre takes about 0.11 kWh, 2.9p at 26.32p. A mug is roughly 0.3 litres, so a mug's worth of water boiled exactly is about 0.9p.

The tank has to replace the litre you draw with a litre of cold mains water and heat it to 100 degrees, which is the same 0.099 kWh of heat, delivered by an element inside an insulated tank at close to 100 per cent. Call it 0.10 kWh, 2.6p. Per litre drawn, the tap is a shade cheaper than the kettle, by about 0.3p.

Now put the standby back. To claw back £23 a year at 0.3p a litre, the tap would need to boil 7,700 litres a year, 21 litres a day, about 70 mugs. No household does that. At a realistic two litres a day the tap saves 2p a day on boiling and spends 6.3p a day on standby, and loses by 4p a day, every day.

Quooker also publishes a figure for its COMBI tank, which supplies hot as well as boiling water: 511 kWh a year under the regulatory usage pattern. That is a different product doing a different job, replacing hot tap water as well as the kettle, and it is compared against a boiler's hot water rather than a kettle; its running cost, £134 a year at the cap rate, is for another article.

4. The overfilling argument, and what the tap is actually for

The overfilling argument. The energy case that is made for taps is that people boil a full kettle for one mug and the tap dispenses only what is drawn. That is true, and the Energy Saving Trust puts the saving from not overfilling the kettle at £10 a year in Great Britain. It is real money, and it is less than half the tap's standby. The cheaper way to capture it is to fill the kettle to the mug line, or to buy a kettle with a one-cup setting, which costs about a fortieth of a tap.

The installation. A boiling water tap needs a tank under the sink, a 13 amp socket in the cupboard, usually a filter, and sometimes a pressure reducer; installation is a plumber's job and adds a three-figure sum to a four-figure purchase. The tank takes cupboard space and needs access for the filter change. None of that is a reason not to buy it, but all of it belongs in the price.

What it actually buys. Instant boiling water, with no kettle on the worktop and no wait. For a household making many drinks across the day, or filling pans for pasta, that convenience is worth something, and people who own them tend to like them. Some households find a tap with a safety push-and-turn handle safer than a full kettle for children or for someone with a weak grip; others find a spout of boiling water at sink height the opposite. The combined models add filtered cold or sparkling water, which replaces a fridge filter jug or bottled water, and that is the one line where a real household saving can appear, if you currently buy bottled water.

Warranty and lifespan. Manufacturers typically give two years on the tap and tank, sometimes extendable; the element and the tank are the parts that fail, and out of warranty a replacement tank can be a significant share of a new tap. Build that into any comparison with a £30 kettle that is replaced every five years.

5. Worked example: Sophie in Cheltenham

Kitchen refit, two adults working from home, about ten hot drinks a day between them, a kettle that is usually overfilled

The kitchen designer has offered a boiling water tap in the quote "because it saves energy compared with a kettle". Sophie wants to know whether that is true before she pays for it.

The kettle, as they use it. Ten mugs is about 3 litres needed; they boil about 4.5 litres because the kettle is always overfilled. 4.5 litres at 0.11 kWh is 0.495 kWh a day, 13p a day, £47.50 a year.

The kettle, filled to the line. 3 litres, 0.33 kWh, 8.7p a day, £31.70 a year. Saving from the habit: £15.80 a year, close to the Energy Saving Trust's £10 figure and free.

The tap. 3 litres at 0.10 kWh is 0.30 kWh a day, £28.80 a year, plus standby £23.06, plus a filter at about £40: £91.90 a year. Against the kettle as they currently use it, the tap costs £44 a year more; against a kettle filled properly, £60 a year more.

The designer's claim. False in every version of the sum. The tap is the most expensive way in the kitchen to boil water.

Decision. Sophie still wants the clear worktop and the instant water, and the combined model would end the bottled sparkling water they buy, about £3 a week. She buys it, on that basis, and prices it as roughly £90 a year to run against £150 a year of bottled water avoided. That is the only household sum in which a boiling water tap ends up ahead, and it has nothing to do with the kettle.

6. The honest verdict

A boiling water tap does not save energy and cannot pay for itself. On the manufacturer's own 10 W standby figure it spends £23 a year at the October 2026 cap before boiling anything, its per-litre cost is only fractionally below a kettle's, and the overfilling saving it captures is worth £10 a year on the Energy Saving Trust's figure, which a kettle filled to the line captures for nothing. Anyone selling you one on the bills is wrong.

Buy one if you want the worktop, the speed and the filtered or sparkling water, and price it honestly at £70 to £110 a year to run plus the installation. If the tap replaces bottled water you currently buy, that is where the money comes back. If it replaces a kettle, it does not.

Frequently asked

How much does a boiling water tap cost to run per year? On Quooker's own 10 W standby figure, £23 a year at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p before any water is drawn, plus about 2.6p per litre boiled, plus a filter cartridge. For a couple making eight drinks a day that is roughly £80 a year all in; the same drinks from a kettle cost about £21.

Is a boiling water tap cheaper than a kettle? No. Per litre the tap is fractionally cheaper, by about 0.3p, but its standby costs £23 a year and a kettle's costs nothing. The tap would need to boil about 21 litres a day to break even on energy, which no household does.

Does a boiling water tap save energy by not overfilling? That is the saving the sales pitch relies on, and the Energy Saving Trust puts it at £10 a year. It is less than half the tap's standby cost, and a kettle filled to the mug line captures it for free.

What is a boiling water tap actually good for? A clear worktop, instant boiling water for drinks and pans, a safer dispensing action for some households, and on combined models filtered cold or sparkling water. If it replaces bottled water you buy now, that is the one place money comes back.
