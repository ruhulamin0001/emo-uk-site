Nine Things A Renter Can Fit Without Asking, And Six Of Them Come With You

Almost every energy saving guide starts with loft insulation and a smart thermostat, which are useless advice if you do not own the building. This is the version for people renting privately, where the test is not just what saves money but what you can take off the wall on the way out.

The short version

  • The renter's test has two parts. Can I fit it without permission, and can I unscrew it and take it when I leave?
  • The whole kit costs about £200 and the highest-value item in it is the cheapest one to reverse.
  • A £25 low flow shower head saves up to £206 a year on a metered gas-heated supply, so it pays back in about six weeks, and you keep the original head in a drawer to refit on moving day.
  • Swapping one 50W halogen for a 5W LED saves £12.97 a year at three hours a day. A £3 bulb pays back in under three months and the bulbs come with you.
  • Do not spend on anything screwed to the fabric of a building you do not own, and never on anything requiring an electrician, because you will leave it behind.

1. The two questions that filter every product

Question one: does fitting it alter the property? Anything that involves drilling into masonry, altering fixed wiring, touching the boiler or gas pipework, or changing the windows is out. Not because it would not save money but because it needs written consent, it may need a qualified person, and you will not be taking it with you.

Question two: what happens to it on moving day? This is the question that separates a renter's list from a homeowner's list, and almost nobody writing about energy saving applies it. A £300 item that stays behind after a fourteen month tenancy is not a £300 item, it is a £300 gift to your landlord.

Run every product past both and the list gets short, practical and surprisingly effective. What survives is a set of things that either plug in, screw into an existing fitting, hang from an existing rail, or sit on the floor.

Everything below is costed at the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: electricity 26.32p per kWh, gas 7.97p per kWh. Water figures use Severn Trent's published 2026/27 metered charges of £4.1758 per cubic metre for supply and used water combined.

Three of those have hard numbers and they are the three to do first.

The shower head is the outlier of the whole list. On a metered supply with a mixer shower off a gas boiler, dropping from about 12 litres a minute to about 7 saves roughly 28p every shower, which in a two person flat showering daily is £206 a year. The full arithmetic, including the two households where it saves nothing at all, is in our page on low flow shower head payback. Read the meter and shower type test there before buying, because if you are unmetered with an electric shower the answer is not to bother.

The LED swap is the most boring item here and it is nearly free money. A 50 watt halogen replaced by a 5 watt LED saves 45 watts. At three hours a day that is 49.3 kWh a year, or £12.97, from a bulb that costs about £3. A flat with six halogen downlighters left on in the kitchen is losing about £78 a year to lighting alone. Put the old bulbs in a labelled bag in the cupboard, refit them when you leave, and take your LEDs.

The smart plug depends entirely on what you plug into it, which is why the energy monitor is on the list. A device drawing a genuine 10 watts around the clock costs £23.06 a year, and set-top boxes, games consoles left in rest mode, and older printers are the usual culprits. A device drawing 0.5 watts costs £1.15 and is not worth a £8 plug. Measure first, then buy.

3. What to skip, and what is the landlord's job not yours

Skip anything that needs an electrician. A smart thermostat, an electric shower swap, new light fittings, a bathroom extractor fan. These are all good ideas and none of them are yours to install or to keep.

Skip secondary glazing panels cut to the window. Made-to-measure panels for one flat's windows are worthless in the next flat. Removable film is a different matter and is covered in our page on secondary glazing against window film for renters, which deals with exactly this trade-off.

Skip radiator reflector panels unless you know the wall. They work by reflecting radiant heat back off an uninsulated solid external wall, and behind an insulated cavity wall or an internal wall they do very little. Anyone quoting you a single national saving figure for these is guessing, and this page will not join them.

And know what is not your problem at all. A rented home in England and Wales must meet a minimum energy efficiency standard to be let, and damp and mould are the landlord's responsibility to investigate and address within set timescales under the current rules. If the flat is cold because there is no loft insulation, or wet because there is no working extractor, that is a repair request in writing, not a shopping list. Our page on PIV units against dehumidifiers sets out how to tell condensation from a building fault, which is the evidence you want before you write that letter.

One more thing worth knowing: if you pay the energy bill directly, the tariff is yours to change, and that is free. A landlord cannot require you to stay with a particular supplier when the account is in your name.

4. Worked example: Dmitri in Norwich

Dmitri rents a one-bedroom flat on a twelve month assured shorthold, pays his own energy and is on a water meter. The flat has a mixer shower off a combi and eight halogen downlighters, four in the kitchen and four in the bathroom.

He spends £15 on a plug-in monitor first and finds his television and set-top box together draw 14 watts permanently, which is 122.6 kWh a year, or £32.28.

He then buys, over two months:

£22 shower head. Showering alone once a day at seven minutes, the saving is about £103 a year. Payback 11 weeks.

£24 for eight LED bulbs. The kitchen four are on about four hours a day and the bathroom four about one hour. Saving is £69.20 plus £17.30, so £86.50 a year. Payback under four months.

£8 smart plug on the television and box, switched off overnight and at work, killing about 16 hours a day of that 14 watts. Saving £21.52. Payback under five months.

£40 heated throw for the evenings, letting him keep the thermostat lower.

Total spent: £109. Total saved in the first year: about £211, before counting the throw.

When his tenancy ends fourteen months later he unscrews the shower head and refits the original from the drawer, takes the eight LEDs out and puts the halogens back, unplugs the smart plug and the monitor, and folds the throw into a box. All of it leaves with him, and it all works again in the next flat on day one.

The mistake he avoided: a colleague in the same block spent £180 on made-to-measure secondary glazing panels, saved a comparable amount on heating, and left every one of them screwed to the window reveals when she moved.

5. The honest verdict

Buy the shower head, the LEDs and the energy monitor first. That is about £65 for something in the region of £190 a year in a metered one-bedroom flat, all of it reversible, all of it portable.

Apply the moving day test to everything else. If it does not unscrew, unclip, unplug or deflate, a renter should not be paying for it.

Do not buy anything for a problem that is a repair. Cold because there is no insulation and damp because there is no extractor are the landlord's obligations, and a dehumidifier bought to manage a building fault is money spent on a symptom.

And measure before you buy the smart plug. A 10 watt standby is worth £23 a year and a 0.5 watt standby is worth £1.15, and you cannot tell them apart by looking.

If you are buying

Buy the monitor before anything with a plug on it, because it turns guesswork into arithmetic for fifteen pounds. On bulbs, match the fitting and the colour temperature to what is already there, and keep the originals.

Frequently asked

Can my landlord object to me changing the shower head or the bulbs? In practice, no, provided you cause no damage and return the property as you found it. Both are consumable fittings that unscrew by hand. Keep the originals safe and refit them at the end, and there is nothing to discuss. Anything requiring a tool or a drill is a different conversation and should be in writing.

What if I am on a bills-included tenancy? Then none of the energy savings reach you, and the only items on this list worth your money are the ones that improve comfort, such as the throw. Check the wording carefully though, because many bills-included agreements set a monthly usage cap above which you are charged, in which case the savings are yours again.

Is it worth getting a water meter fitted in a rented flat? You can usually request one if you are the bill payer and the tenancy has enough time left to run, and many suppliers allow a change back within a set period. If the flat has more bedrooms than occupants, a meter usually saves money, and it also switches on the water half of the shower head saving.

What is the single biggest thing I can do that costs nothing? Heat the person rather than the room, and dry washing where the moisture can leave the building. Both are habits, both are free, and between them they beat most of the purchases on this page.
