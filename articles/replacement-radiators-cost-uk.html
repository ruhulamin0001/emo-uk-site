Your Radiators Are Not Too Small. Your Boiler Is Too Hot

Eight new radiators fitted is about 2,200 pounds and, bought purely for efficiency, pays back in roughly forty years. The change that actually delivers most of the gain is free, takes ten minutes, and is the reason anybody is quoting you for radiators in the first place.

The short version

  • A single radiator swapped like for like is 180 to 320 pounds fitted. A whole house of eight is 1,600 to 2,500 pounds, more if pipework has to move.
  • Radiator output is quoted at delta T 50 and almost nobody runs at delta T 50. At delta T 30, which is what a 55 degree flow gives you, output falls to roughly half.
  • Running a condensing boiler properly is worth about 6 per cent of the gas bill. On Ofgem typical consumption of 11,500 kWh at 7.97p that is about 55 pounds a year.
  • 2,200 pounds divided by 55 pounds a year is 40 years. Do not buy radiators as an energy saving measure. Buy them when one leaks or a room cannot get warm.
  • Turn the flow temperature down first, for nothing. If every room still heats, you have banked the saving and you do not need the radiators at all.

1. Delta T, the number the label hides

Every radiator sold in the UK carries an output figure in watts. That figure is measured at delta T 50, which means the average water temperature in the radiator is 50 degrees hotter than the room. With a 20 degree room, that requires a mean water temperature of 70, which in practice means a flow temperature around 75 and a return around 65.

That is a hot system. It is roughly how boilers were set up in 1995 and it is how a lot of them are still running today. It is also a temperature at which a condensing boiler cannot condense, because condensing requires the return water to come back cool enough for the flue gases to give up their latent heat, which is somewhere below about 54 degrees.

So the industry sells radiators rated at a temperature that stops your boiler doing the thing it was bought to do. Nobody is lying. It is a standard test condition. But it means the 1,500 watt radiator on the box is a 1,500 watt radiator only in conditions you should be trying to avoid.

Run the same radiator at a 55 degree flow, which gives roughly delta T 30, and the output drops to about 51 per cent of the label figure. That is the correction factor the trade uses and it is the single most useful number in this article. Delta T 40 sits at about 75 per cent.

2. Why low flow temperatures need bigger radiators

Put those two facts together and the whole radiator conversation makes sense.

If you turn your boiler's flow temperature down from 75 to 55, the boiler starts condensing and burns meaningfully less gas for the same heat delivered. But every radiator in the house now produces about half what it did. In a well insulated room that does not matter, because the room never needed the full output. In a cold north facing room with a large window, it does, and that room stops reaching temperature on the coldest days.

That is the moment somebody quotes you for radiators. And they are not wrong: a bigger radiator running at 55 degrees delivers the same heat as a smaller one at 75, while letting the boiler condense.

The trap is doing it in the wrong order and for the wrong reason. The saving comes from the lower flow temperature, not from the radiator. The radiator is what makes the lower flow temperature tolerable in the rooms where it otherwise would not be. So the radiator is a comfort purchase that enables a saving, not a saving in itself, and it should be priced accordingly.

Which means the right move is to turn the flow temperature down first and find out how many rooms actually have a problem. In a lot of houses the answer is one, or none.

Replacing two radiators shows a zero saving on its own because the saving belongs to the flow temperature change, which is free and which you can make with or without new radiators. Listing it honestly as zero is the only way to see what you are really buying, which is two rooms that get warm.

4. The order to do things in

Four steps, in this order, and do not skip to the end.

One, turn the flow temperature down. On most combis there is a radiator symbol on the display or a dial marked with one. Set it to 60 and live with it for a fortnight, then try 55. This is the free saving and it is the one almost nobody has taken.

Two, find out which rooms fail. A cheap thermometer in each room over a genuinely cold week tells you more than any survey. Most houses have one problem room, not eight.

Three, balance the system. Lockshield valves at the far end open, near ones nipped down, so every radiator reaches temperature at about the same rate. A plumber will do this in an hour or you can do it with two thermometers and patience. It fixes a surprising number of cold rooms for nothing.

Four, only then, replace what is left. Usually one or two radiators, sized for the room at delta T 30 rather than 50, which means a physically larger radiator or a type 22 where a type 11 was. That is 360 to 640 pounds rather than 2,200.

Anyone who quotes to replace every radiator in the house before step one has been done is selling you something you may not need.

5. Worked example: the Ashworth house in Chesham

Gary Ashworth has a 1988 four bedroom detached house in Chesham with eight radiators and a combi fitted in 2016. A heating firm quotes 2,380 pounds to replace all eight, describing it as making the system low temperature ready.

He does step one instead. Flow temperature down from 74 to 58, which takes about three minutes on the boiler front panel and costs nothing.

Over the following fortnight, six of the eight rooms are fine. The dining room takes an hour longer to warm up. The back bedroom, which is north facing with a large window and sits at the end of the pipe run, does not reach 18 degrees at all on the coldest night.

A plumber balances the system for 95 pounds. That fixes the dining room completely, because it had been starved by the two radiators nearest the boiler. The back bedroom is still short.

He replaces that one radiator with a type 22 sized at delta T 30, at 295 pounds fitted. Total spend 390 pounds against a 2,380 pound quote.

The gas saving is the same in both cases, roughly 55 pounds a year, because it came from the flow temperature. What the 390 pounds bought was a warm back bedroom. What the other 1,990 pounds would have bought was seven radiators that were already doing their job.

Frequently asked

Will a lower flow temperature take longer to heat the house? Yes, and that is the honest trade. A house that reached temperature in forty minutes at 75 degrees may take seventy at 55. The answer is to start the heating earlier rather than run it hotter, which is what a decent programmable thermostat is for.

Do aluminium radiators save money? They respond faster and hold less water, which helps in a room you heat intermittently. They do not change the arithmetic above, because the saving lives in the flow temperature. They are a comfort and response purchase and they cost more.

What about a heat pump later? This is the strongest argument for correctly sized radiators, because a heat pump runs at 45 to 50 degrees and the radiator sizing question becomes unavoidable. If a heat pump is genuinely in your plans, size the radiators for that now and treat it as the first step of that project, not as a gas saving.

Does changing radiators need building control? A like for like swap does not. Altering the system, moving pipework or changing the boiler brings Approved Document L into it, and a Gas Safe registered installer handles the notification.

The honest verdict

Do not buy radiators to save energy. Eight of them is about 2,200 pounds against roughly 55 pounds a year, which is a forty year payback, and the same 55 pounds is available today for nothing by turning the flow temperature down.

Turn it down, balance the system, then buy the one or two radiators that genuinely cannot cope. That is usually 300 to 600 pounds, and it is the whole job.
