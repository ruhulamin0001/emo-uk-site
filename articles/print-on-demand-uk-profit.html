Print on Demand UK: The Real Profit Per T-Shirt

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Checked August 2026

The short version
  • A £20 printed t-shirt typically leaves a UK seller £2 to £5 after base cost, marketplace fees and VAT. Not the £12 the guides imply.
  • The profit is not in t-shirts. It is in higher-margin, lower-competition products and in a design people search for by name.
  • You keep no inventory and no risk, which is the genuine advantage. You also keep almost no margin, which is the trade.
  • Use a UK or EU print partner. Printing overseas means slow delivery and possible import charges landing on your customer.
  • Copyright and trademark infringement is the fastest way to lose a shop, and "it was on a free design site" is not a defence.

The actual maths on a t-shirt

Print on demand is sold as a business with no costs. It has no upfront costs, which is a different thing entirely. Here is a typical UK sale.

LineAmountNote
Sale price£20.00Roughly the ceiling for an unbranded printed tee from an unknown shop.
Base cost of shirt and printing−£9.00Varies by garment quality and print method. Cheap blanks feel cheap and generate returns.
Shipping paid to the print partner−£4.00Often charged to you even when the customer paid postage.
Marketplace fees−£2.80Around 14% on Etsy including VAT on fees. Lower on your own shop, higher with ads.
Left before tax£4.20And that is a good day.

Indicative figures for a UK seller in August 2026. Base costs and shipping vary considerably between print partners and garment types, so run the numbers with your own supplier's live pricing.

Two things make it worse. If the customer paid separate postage, the marketplace fee applies to that too. And if the sale came through offsite advertising, take another 12% to 15% off, which on this example leaves you close to nothing.

Two things make it better. Multi-item orders share one shipping charge, so a two-shirt order is much more profitable than two one-shirt orders. And your own shop replaces a 14% marketplace fee with roughly 2% to 3% in payment processing.

Where the margin actually is

T-shirts are the most competitive and lowest-margin product in this business. They are also the first thing everyone tries.

ProductMarginCompetitionComment
T-shirtsLowExtremeEveryone starts here. Price ceiling is low because buyers compare instantly.
Hoodies and sweatshirtsModerateHighHigher price point carries the fees better. Autumn and winter seasonal.
MugsModerateHighCheap base, decent markup, but breakage in transit is a real cost.
Wall art and postersGoodModerateLow base cost, no sizing problems, no returns for fit, and buyers accept a wide price range.
Personalised itemsBestLowerA name, a date, a pet, a postcode. Personalisation removes price comparison entirely, and it is usually non-returnable.
Tote bags and apronsModerateModerateSteady, unglamorous, low return rate.
Notebooks and stationeryModerateModerateLow value per unit, so it depends on volume or bundling.
Digital downloadsHighestExtremeNo base cost at all, but the market is saturated and prices have collapsed. Worth having alongside physical products rather than instead of them.

The pattern is clear: the further you get from a generic garment, the better the economics. A personalised print for a specific occupation, hobby, town or family event competes with almost nobody, and the buyer is not shopping on price.

Start on a marketplace, because the hardest part of this business is not printing, it is being found. Then, once you know which designs sell, open your own shop and drive repeat buyers there. Doing it in that order means you build a direct channel around products you already know work.

Choosing a print partner from the UK

The single most important decision, and the one people give least thought to.

  • Print location. Insist on UK or EU fulfilment for UK customers. Overseas printing means one to three weeks of delivery, and post-Brexit it can mean your customer being asked for import VAT and a handling fee on their doorstep. That produces a refund request and a one-star review at the same time.
  • Order a sample of everything you sell. Not optional. You are putting your name on a product you have never touched, and print quality, garment weight and colour accuracy vary enormously.
  • Check who handles returns and reprints when the print is wrong. Good partners reprint free for their own errors. Confirm this in writing before you rely on it.
  • Compare all-in cost, not base cost. A partner with a low garment price and high shipping is usually the more expensive one.
  • Check production time separately from shipping time, and quote the sum of both to customers. Under-promising here is how you avoid most complaints.
  • Have a second partner set up before you need one. Printers go down, run out of stock in popular sizes, and get overwhelmed in November.

Copyright, trademarks and losing your shop

This is where shops die, and it happens fast.

  • Song lyrics, film quotes, book lines and character names are protected. Putting them on a shirt is infringement, whoever else is doing it.
  • Trademarks cover phrases too. Ordinary-sounding slogans are registered by companies for clothing specifically. Search the UK Intellectual Property Office register and the EU register before committing to a phrase.
  • Football clubs, universities, bands and brands enforce aggressively, and their legal teams monitor marketplaces automatically.
  • "It was free on a design site" is not a defence. Check the licence, and check specifically whether it permits commercial use and print on physical goods. Many free fonts are personal use only.
  • AI-generated designs are a live legal question. Ownership and copyright status are genuinely unsettled, and outputs can resemble protected work. Use them with care and never for anything you intend to build a brand on.

The consequence is not usually a lawsuit. It is a takedown, and after enough takedowns the marketplace closes your shop and keeps the pending balance. Original work is not only more ethical here, it is more durable.

Returns, quality and the reviews problem

You never see the product, which means you are trusting a third party with your reputation on every single order.

  • Sizing is the number one complaint. Publish the actual garment measurements, in centimetres, flat, not "fits like a regular tee".
  • Colour on screen is not colour on cotton. Photograph or mock up realistically, and say so.
  • Personalised goods are generally exempt from the standard UK distance-selling cancellation rights, which is another argument for personalisation. Non-personalised items are not, and you must handle returns properly.
  • Reply to every negative review calmly and factually. Future buyers read the reply more than the complaint.

What a realistic first year looks like

  • Months 1 to 3. You upload 30 designs, sell two, and one of them is to a relative. This is normal and it is the point at which most people conclude the business does not work.
  • Months 3 to 6. One or two designs start selling consistently. This tells you more than all your market research did. Make ten variations of what sold, not ten new ideas.
  • Months 6 to 12. A small consistent income, perhaps £50 to £300 a month for a shop that is being worked on properly, heavily weighted to the run-up to Christmas.
  • Seasonality is severe. A large share of the year's sales land between mid-October and mid-December. Upload Christmas designs in September, not November.

On tax: the first £1,000 of trading income in a tax year is covered by the trading allowance. Above that, register as self-employed with HMRC. Marketplaces now report seller data to tax authorities, so treat this as a certainty rather than a risk.

Questions people ask

Can I really start with no money? Almost. You need samples, which you should not skip, and either marketplace listing fees or a shop subscription. Call it a modest start-up cost rather than none.

Do I need to be a designer? No, but you need something people specifically want. Text-based and typographic designs sell well and need no drawing ability. What does not work is generic art with no audience in mind.

Is it saturated? The generic end is completely saturated. Specific niches with a real community and a phrase only they understand are not, and that is where every profitable print on demand shop lives.

Should I run ads? Not until a design has already sold organically. Advertising a product nobody has bought is paying to discover what you could have learned free.

The verdict

Print on demand is a real business with genuinely thin margins. Skip generic t-shirts, go straight to personalised and niche products, print in the UK or EU, order samples, and keep every design your own. Expect the first six months to teach you rather than pay you, and judge the whole thing on what happens in your second Christmas.
