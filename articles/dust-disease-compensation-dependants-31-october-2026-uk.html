If the Payment Lands After You Die, Your Family Gets £7,000 Less. That Is Being Fixed

There is a government compensation scheme for people who developed asbestos and dust related diseases at work and cannot claim from the employer, usually because the employer no longer exists. It pays less to a widow than it would have paid to her husband, for the same disease and the same working life. Regulations laid on 1 September 2026 would end that.

1. What these schemes are, and who they are for

Most people have never heard of this, and the reason is structural. It only becomes relevant decades after the exposure that caused it.

Workers who develop asbestos related and dust related diseases such as mesothelioma and pneumoconiosis because of their work can claim a one-off payment from the government if they have not already secured compensation from their employer.

Why that gap exists is the whole point. As DWP puts it, these diseases take years to develop, "meaning the employer responsible has usually gone out of business by the time someone is diagnosed". You cannot sue a company that dissolved in 1994. For a great many people this scheme is not the backup option, it is the only option.

Two schemes do the work:

  • the Pneumoconiosis etc. (Workers' Compensation) Act 1979 scheme
  • the 2008 Diffuse Mesothelioma Scheme

Both provide lump-sum compensation to people who contracted a dust related disease through their work, or to their dependants, where they have been unable to claim damages from an employer.

2. The gap, and exactly how big it is

Under the current system, the payment is passed to the family at a reduced rate if it is paid after the sufferer dies.

Average award to a sufferer: £17,700.

Average award to a dependant: £10,700.

The shortfall: £7,000.

Same disease, same working life, same scheme. The variable that decides which figure applies is whether the claim was concluded before or after the person died, in a category of illness where the time from diagnosis to death is frequently very short.

The government's own description of the effect is direct: families are "penalised twice, first by their loss, then by an unfair system". Campaigners including the Asbestos Victims Support Group Forum UK, and parliamentarians including Baroness Blower, have raised it for over a decade.

On volume, in 2025 2,730 sufferers received an award and 300 awards were paid to dependants at the lower rate. So the reduced rate hits a minority of cases, but it hits them at the worst possible moment.

3. What the regulations change, and when

Two changes, laid before Parliament on 1 September 2026.

First, dependants get the same rate as sufferers, in full. The reduced dependant rate goes.

Second, the calculation is simplified. The changes remove outdated top-up payments and introduce a single, clear award structure for everyone. They also clear out rules that referred to Pneumoconiosis Medical Boards, bodies which stopped operating years ago and whose continued presence in the rules caused confusion. A clear "relevant date" for award calculations is being introduced.

On timing, be precise. The changes "will come into force on 31 October 2026 subject to Parliamentary approval". The draft regulations now go before both Houses for debate. That is a real condition, not a formality to be waved through in a summary. Until both Houses have approved them, 31 October 2026 is an intended date rather than a settled one.

DWP expects the regulations to increase payments to dependants by between £2 million and £6 million annually across all dependants.

The changes follow an independent legal review of the schemes, which recommended updates to reduce legal and reputational risk, including the dependant payment change and the clear relevant date.

4. Worked example: the Trevithick family in Redruth

John Trevithick worked in construction in Cornwall from the mid 1970s. He is diagnosed with mesothelioma in 2026. The firm that employed him during the exposure years was wound up long ago, so there is nobody to bring a civil claim against. The 2008 scheme is his only route.

If his claim concludes while he is alive, the award goes to him. On the published averages that is around £17,700.

If he dies before it concludes, under the current rules the award passes to his wife Eileen at the dependant rate. On the published averages, around £10,700.

The difference is £7,000, and under the current rules it turns on timing that nobody in the family controls, in a disease where survival after diagnosis is often measured in months.

Under the new regulations, if they are approved and come into force on 31 October 2026, Eileen receives the same rate John would have received. The £7,000 gap closes.

The caveat that has to be said: these are published averages, not entitlements. Actual awards under both schemes vary with the age at diagnosis and the disability assessed, and younger claimants generally receive more. Do not treat £17,700 as a figure you can budget against. Treat it as the size of the unfairness being fixed.

5. What this does not do

Being clear about the limits matters more than usual here, because the people reading this are often in the worst weeks of their lives and cannot afford a wasted hope.

It does not create a new right to compensation. The eligibility rules for both schemes are unchanged. If you did not qualify before, this does not make you qualify.

It does not help you claim against an employer. These schemes exist precisely for cases where that route is closed. If your former employer or its insurer still exists, a civil claim is a different and usually larger route, and that is a question for a solicitor.

It is not yet law. The draft regulations are before both Houses. 31 October 2026 is subject to approval.

It does not automatically cover Northern Ireland. The devolved schemes there are administered by the Department for Communities, which is "expected to introduce corresponding legislation". Expected is the word on the page, and it is not the same as done.

It does not backdate. Nothing in the announcement suggests awards already paid at the dependant rate will be topped up.

The honest verdict

This is a decade-old unfairness with no defenders, being fixed. There is no other honest way to describe it. A scheme that pays a widow £7,000 less than it would have paid her husband, because the illness that killed him moved faster than the paperwork, was never going to be argued for on the merits.

The practical value of knowing about it is narrower than the headline suggests. It creates no new entitlement and it does not backdate. If a family member died of a dust related disease and the award was already paid at the dependant rate, this does not reach them.

Where it does matter is now. If a claim is live, or about to be made, the difference between an award concluded under the old rules and one concluded after 31 October 2026 could be around £7,000 for a dependant. That is worth raising with whoever is handling the claim, and it is worth raising this month rather than in November. Frequently asked

Who counts as a dependant? That is defined in the schemes themselves rather than in the announcement, and it is not something to guess at. The 1979 Act and the 2008 scheme each set out who can claim after a death. Ask the DWP scheme team or a support group before assuming.

My relative's claim was paid years ago at the lower rate. Will we get the difference? Nothing in the announcement suggests the change is backdated. It describes regulations coming into force on 31 October 2026, subject to Parliamentary approval.

Does this apply in Northern Ireland? Not automatically. Northern Ireland runs devolved schemes administered by the Department for Communities, which the announcement says is expected to introduce corresponding legislation. Expected, not done.

Is it definitely happening on 31 October? It is subject to Parliamentary approval. The draft regulations go before both Houses for debate. Until that completes, treat the date as intended rather than fixed.
