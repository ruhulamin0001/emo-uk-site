Two People Give the Same £100. One of Them Gets £25 Back

Gift Aid is the rare arrangement where the charity gains and you do too, and the second half goes unclaimed by most of the people entitled to it. Here is what it is worth at each tax rate, how it compares with Payroll Giving, and the rule that can turn a donation into a tax bill.

The short version

  • Gift Aid adds 25p for every £1 you give, at no cost to you, provided you have paid enough tax and have signed a declaration.
  • A higher rate taxpayer can reclaim more, and usually does not. On a £100 donation that is £25 back, and on an additional rate donation £31.25.
  • Gift Aid and Payroll Giving deliver exactly the same value per pound of your net cost. Both get £1.67 to the charity for every £1 it costs a higher rate taxpayer. That surprises almost everyone.
  • The differences are National Insurance, admin and backdating, not the headline maths. Payroll Giving still costs you NI. Gift Aid can cover donations from the last four years.
  • The trap is the four times rule. Your donations must not exceed four times the tax you paid that year, or HMRC can come to you for the shortfall.

1. How Gift Aid actually works

When you sign a Gift Aid declaration, the charity can reclaim the basic rate tax you are treated as having already paid on the donation. GOV.UK puts the effect simply: the charity or community amateur sports club can claim an extra 25p for every £1 you give, and it does not cost you anything extra.

The arithmetic behind the 25p is worth understanding, because the rest of this article depends on it. A £100 donation is treated as the net remainder of a gross gift of £125, from which 20% basic rate tax of £25 has already been deducted. The charity reclaims that £25.

You need to do two things for this to work. Sign a declaration with each charity you support, and have paid enough tax to cover what they reclaim.

2. The part higher rate taxpayers miss

If you pay tax above the basic rate, the charity reclaiming 20% does not settle the account. You paid 40% or 45% on that income, and the difference belongs to you.

You claim it through your Self Assessment return, or by asking HMRC to amend your tax code. GOV.UK gives the worked example directly: donate £100, the charity claims Gift Aid to make it £125, and as a 40% taxpayer you can personally claim back £25.00, being £125 multiplied by 20%.

The general formula is the gross donation multiplied by the gap between your rate and the basic rate. For an additional rate taxpayer that is £125 times 25%, which is £31.25.

Every higher rate taxpayer who gives to charity without claiming is making a second donation, to HMRC, that nobody asked them to make.

The last column is identical for Gift Aid and Payroll Giving at every tax rate. That is not a coincidence. Both mechanisms relieve income tax at your marginal rate and nothing else, so the efficiency per pound of your own money is the same. The only route that loses money is the last one, where nobody claims anything.

So what actually distinguishes them?

  • National Insurance. GOV.UK is explicit that with Payroll Giving you still pay National Insurance on the amount you donate. Neither route relieves NI, so this is a point of similarity rather than difference, and worth stating because it is widely misunderstood.
  • Who does the work. Payroll Giving is automatic once set up, with relief given at source and nothing to reclaim. Gift Aid at the higher rate requires you to claim, every year.
  • Backdating. A Gift Aid declaration can cover donations made in the last four years. Payroll Giving starts when it starts.
  • Cash flow for the charity. Payroll Giving pays the charity a regular, predictable amount. Gift Aid reclaims arrive later.

4. The four times rule, and why it matters

Your donations qualify for Gift Aid only as long as they are not more than four times what you have paid in tax in that tax year, counting income tax and capital gains tax.

So a person who paid £600 of tax in a year can Gift Aid up to £2,400 of donations. Beyond that, the charity is reclaiming tax you never paid, and HMRC may ask you for the difference.

This catches two groups in particular: people who retire or stop working partway through a year while their standing orders continue, and people whose income falls below the personal allowance. If your tax position changes, you must tell the charities you support.

5. How to claim if you do not file a return

Most higher rate taxpayers who give to charity are not in Self Assessment, and assume the relief is therefore out of reach. It is not.

You contact HMRC and tell them how much you donated. GOV.UK sets the thresholds for how:

  • £5,000 or less can be claimed by phone.
  • More than £5,000 must be claimed in writing.
  • £10,000 or more also requires the date of the donation and who you gave it to.

HMRC will normally adjust your tax code so you pay less tax going forward.

Worked example: Meera in Leicester

Meera earns £62,000, so she is a higher rate taxpayer. She gives £50 a month to two charities by direct debit, with Gift Aid declarations in place. That is £1,200 a year.

What the charities get. £1,200 plus 25% is £1,500.

What she can reclaim. £1,500 multiplied by 20% is £300 a year.

What she has been reclaiming. Nothing. She does not file a Self Assessment return and assumed the charity claiming the tax was the end of it.

Backdating. A declaration can cover donations made in the last four years, and she has been giving at this level throughout. Four previous years at £300 plus the current year comes to roughly £1,500.

How she claims it. The total is over £5,000 of donations across those years, so she writes to HMRC rather than phoning, with the dates and the charities. Her tax code is adjusted and the earlier years are settled.

The check she did first. Her tax paid each year comfortably exceeds a quarter of £1,200, so the four times rule is nowhere near being breached. Had she taken a career break in one of those years, that year would have needed a second look.

6. Frequently asked

Does claiming higher rate relief take money from the charity? No. The charity keeps its 25%. The relief you claim is tax you personally overpaid.

What if I give to a charity abroad? Gift Aid applies to UK charities and community amateur sports clubs with an HMRC charity reference number. Ask if you are unsure.

Can I Gift Aid a raffle ticket or an auction lot? Generally no. Payments where you receive something in return do not qualify, and the rules on benefits are detailed. Straight donations are the safe case.

Do both partners need declarations? Declarations are personal. A joint donation from a couple should be split, and each partner claims relief on their own share at their own rate.

7. The honest verdict

Sign a Gift Aid declaration for every UK charity you give to, every time. It costs you nothing and adds 25% to the gift.

If you pay tax above the basic rate, claim the difference. On £1,200 a year of giving that is £300 back, and four years of backdating turns a first claim into roughly £1,500. Phone HMRC if the donations total £5,000 or less, write if they are more.

Do not agonise over Gift Aid against Payroll Giving. Per pound of your own money they deliver exactly the same amount to the charity. Choose Payroll Giving if you would rather it happened automatically, and Gift Aid if you want the four year backdating and the freedom to give to whoever you like.
