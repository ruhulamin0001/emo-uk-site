One of These Insurances Is Compulsory and It Is Not the One You Think

Public liability is the policy every side hustle guide tells you to buy, and no law in the UK requires it. The one that is compulsory, with a daily fine attached, is the one hardly anyone mentions. Here is which is which, priced per activity, with the break-even measured in jobs.

The short version

  • Public liability insurance is voluntary. Employers’ liability is not. The Health and Safety Executive is explicit: public liability is generally voluntary, while employers’ liability is compulsory under the Employers’ Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969.
  • If you employ anyone, the legal minimum is £5 million of employers’ liability cover, and you can be fined up to £2,500 for each day you trade without it, plus £1,000 for not displaying the certificate.
  • A solo side hustle with no employees needs neither by law. What forces the purchase is somebody else’s rule: a council, a market operator, a venue, a letting agent or a client.
  • Price it in jobs, not in pounds. A £90 premium against £18 profit a walk is five walks a year. That is the only number that matters when deciding.
  • For a home tutor teaching online, the honest answer is usually do not buy it. Nobody requires it and there is no public to be liable to.

1. What the law actually requires

The Health and Safety Executive’s guide to the Employers’ Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969 draws the line clearly. Employers’ liability covers claims by your employees. Public liability covers claims by members of the public or other businesses. In its own words, public liability is generally voluntary while employers’ liability is compulsory.

If you do employ someone under a contract of service, the requirements are specific:

  • You must be insured for at least £5 million. Most insurers in practice offer at least £10 million.
  • The insurer must be authorised. HSE directs employers to check the regulator’s register.
  • You must display the certificate where employees can read it, and electronic display has been allowed since 1 October 2008.
  • You can be fined up to £2,500 for any day you are without suitable insurance, and up to £1,000 for failing to display or produce the certificate.

Two exemptions matter for small operators. Family businesses where every employee is a close relative are exempt, unless the business is incorporated as a limited company. And a company that employs only its owner, where that owner holds 50% or more of the issued share capital, is also exempt. A one-person limited company with a sole director shareholder therefore generally does not need employers’ liability cover at all.

There is no law telling a solo side hustler to buy public liability insurance. There is very often a market operator, a council or a venue telling them to, and that is a different kind of compulsory.

2. Who actually forces you to buy public liability

Because the law is silent, the requirement arrives from somewhere else. In practice it is one of five sources.

  1. Council licensing. Street trading consents and market pitches commonly require evidence of public liability cover, often at £5 million, before a pitch is allocated.
  2. Private market and event operators. Craft fairs, food markets and school fetes usually ask for a certificate at booking.
  3. Venues. Church halls, community centres and sports halls hiring space to a tutor, class or club almost always require it.
  4. Clients and letting agents. Anyone letting you into a property to work, from a cleaner to a gardener to a valeter, may require it.
  5. Platforms. Some booking platforms include cover, and some require you to hold your own. Read which, because assuming the first when the second applies is expensive.

If none of those five apply to you, nobody is requiring you to buy this.

The last two rows are the interesting ones. The online tutor has no public to be liable to and nobody asking for a certificate, so the honest answer is that the premium buys nothing. And the moment any of these hustles takes on a single employee, the conversation changes from a calculation to a legal requirement with a daily fine attached.

4. The line between helper and employee

This is where small operators get caught. HSE is clear that what matters is the real nature of the relationship, not what you call it or what their tax status is. You may need employers’ liability cover for someone where you deduct tax and National Insurance from their pay, you control where and when and how they work, you supply their materials and equipment, and they cannot send a substitute.

You probably do not need it where they work for others too, supply their own equipment, are clearly in business on their own account, and can send a substitute.

The practical test for a side hustle: if your cousin helps on the stall for a share of the takings every Saturday, using your gazebo and your stock, doing what you tell them, that starts to look like employment whatever you both call it. Unless the family exemption applies and you are not incorporated, that is a £5 million policy you are missing.

Worked example: Owen in Bridgend

Owen walks dogs at weekends. He walks four dogs a day, two days a week, roughly 44 weeks a year, at £18 profit a walk. That is 352 walks and about £6,336 a year of profit.

Does the law require insurance? No. He has no employees, so employers’ liability does not apply, and public liability is voluntary.

Does anyone else? Yes. Three of his eleven clients asked for proof of cover before handing over a key, and his local authority requires it for the licence he needs to walk more than four dogs at once.

The break-even. A £110 policy costs him £110 divided by £18, which is 6.1 walks a year. Against 352 walks, that is 1.7% of his annual profit.

What it protects. Not the dogs, which needs a separate care, custody and control extension that standard public liability often excludes. It protects him against a claim from a third party, for example if a dog he is handling causes a cyclist to come off. That is the realistic scenario, and it is the one he cannot absorb himself.

His decision. Buy it, and specifically check the care, custody and control wording, because the version that excludes injury to the animals in his charge is not the cover he thinks he is buying.

5. Frequently asked

Is public liability insurance a legal requirement in the UK? No. HSE states it is generally voluntary. Employers’ liability is the compulsory one, and only where you have employees.

What happens if I employ someone and have no cover? HSE inspectors can ask to see your certificate. The fine is up to £2,500 for each day you are uninsured, and up to £1,000 for not displaying the certificate.

Does the £1,000 trading allowance affect this? Only in the sense of scale. If your hustle earns under £1,000 a year you may have no tax to report, and at that size an annual premium is a large share of the income. That is a strong argument for not taking on work that requires cover until the income supports it.

Is it tax deductible? Insurance taken out wholly and exclusively for the trade is normally an allowable business expense, which reduces the real cost by your marginal rate.

6. The honest verdict

If a council, a market, a venue or a client is asking for a certificate, buy public liability cover. The break-even in the table above is between one and nine jobs a year, which is trivial against being allowed to trade. Check the limit they specify and buy at that limit, not below it.

If nobody is asking and your work happens online or in your own home, do not buy it. You are insuring against a public that is not there. Revisit the decision the first time somebody asks for a certificate, not before.

And if you take on even one helper who looks like an employee, stop reading about public liability and get £5 million of employers’ liability cover. That one is not a judgement call.
