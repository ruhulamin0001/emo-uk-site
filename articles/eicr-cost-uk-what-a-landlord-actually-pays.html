Your Landlord Electrical Report Costs £180. The Findings Are Where the Money Goes

An EICR is a fixed-price inspection with an open-ended bill attached. The inspection is cheap and predictable. The remedial work it uncovers is neither, and the regulations give you 28 days to do it. Here is the five-year cost spread properly, and the two codes that decide everything.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector and Social Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020 require an inspection at least every five years by a qualified and competent person.
  • A C1 or a C2 makes the report unsatisfactory, and remedial work must be done within 28 days of the report, or sooner if the report specifies a shorter period.
  • A C3 is a recommendation and is not mandatory. A report carrying only C3 items is satisfactory. Paying to clear C3s because an electrician suggested it is optional spending, not compliance.
  • Spread over five years, a clean £180 report is £36 a year. A report with a £900 remedial bill is £216 a year, which is still not the disaster it feels like on the day.
  • The single biggest cost driver is age of installation, not size of property. A one-bed flat with a rewirable fuse box will cost more to bring into line than a four-bed house wired in 2015.

1. What the regulations actually require

The governing instrument is SI 2020/312, originally the Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020 and since amended to extend to the social rented sector. It came into force on 1 June 2020, applied to new tenancies from 1 July 2020 and to all existing tenancies from 1 April 2021.

The duties are short and specific.

  • Ensure the fixed electrical installation is inspected and tested at least every five years by a qualified and competent person.
  • Obtain a report, and supply it to existing tenants within 28 days of the inspection.
  • Supply it to a new tenant before they occupy, and to a prospective tenant within 28 days of a written request.
  • Where the report requires remedial or further investigative work, complete it within 28 days, or a shorter period if the report specifies one.
  • Obtain written confirmation that the work is done, and supply that to the tenant and the local authority within 28 days.

Note what is not in that list. There is no requirement to bring an older installation up to the current edition of BS 7671. An installation wired to an earlier standard can be perfectly safe and pass. The test is safety, not currency, and any electrician telling you otherwise is selling something.

Scotland runs a parallel regime under the Housing (Scotland) Act 2006 with a five-yearly cycle, and Wales has its own requirements under the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016. The English SI is the one described above.

2. C1, C2, C3 and FI, and which ones cost you money

Every observation on the report carries a code, and the codes are where your money is decided.

C1, danger present. Risk of injury, immediate action required. Exposed live parts, that sort of thing. The electrician will usually make it safe on the spot. Report is unsatisfactory.

C2, potentially dangerous. Urgent remedial action required. No earthing at a socket, missing bonding to the gas or water supply, a damaged cable inside a wall. Report is unsatisfactory.

FI, further investigation required. The inspector found something they cannot resolve without more work. Report is unsatisfactory, and the investigation itself has a cost before you know what the repair costs.

C3, improvement recommended. Not dangerous. No RCD protection on a circuit that predates the requirement, no labelling on the board. Report remains satisfactory. You are under no legal obligation to act.

That last point is worth a paragraph on its own, because it is where landlords lose the most money unnecessarily. A quote that bundles every C1, C2 and C3 into one figure lets the discretionary work hide inside the mandatory work. Ask for the quote itemised by code. Pay for the C1s, the C2s and the FIs. Decide about the C3s separately, on their merits, in your own time.

3. What moves the price of the inspection itself

An EICR is priced mostly by circuit count, not by floor area, because the labour is in testing each circuit individually. A one-bed flat might have six circuits, a four-bed house sixteen.

Four things push it up. Access, where sockets are behind furniture or the consumer unit is boarded in. Age, because an old installation throws up more to investigate. Tenant coordination, since the power goes off for part of the day and someone has to be there. And distance, on a rural property.

One thing brings it down reliably: booking the EICR and the gas safety check together with a firm that does both, and doing a whole portfolio on the same visit if you have one.

4. Who counts as qualified and competent, and how to check

The regulations require a qualified and competent person and then, unhelpfully, do not define the phrase in a list of qualifications. Government guidance fills the gap, and the practical test has three parts.

Membership of a competent person scheme. NICEIC, NAPIT and ELECSA are the names you will see. Registration is checkable online in under a minute, and you should check it rather than accepting a logo on a van or a website. Logos are easy to copy; a scheme's own register is not.

Qualification to inspect and test, specifically. Installing and inspecting are different competencies. The relevant qualification is the current inspection and testing award, formerly 2391 and now usually 2391-52 or the equivalent, and an electrician who installs beautifully may not hold it.

Insurance. Ask for the certificate, not the assurance. If a report is wrong and a tenant is hurt, the question of who carries the liability matters enormously.

There is one more check worth ten minutes. Ask, before booking, roughly what proportion of the properties they inspect come back unsatisfactory. An inspector who says nearly all of them, or nearly none of them, is telling you something about their coding habits either way. You want the one who talks about the property rather than the percentage.

Finally, keep every report. The regulations require you to give the previous report to the next inspector, and a five-year paper trail showing a property being maintained rather than patched is worth having if a local authority ever asks.

Worked example: the Bramley household, Stockport

A two-bed terrace let since 2014, last inspected in 2021. The 2026 report came back unsatisfactory with one C2 for missing main protective bonding to the incoming gas supply, one C2 for a damaged socket back box in the kitchen, and four C3s covering an unlabelled board and the absence of RCD protection on the lighting circuit.

Inspection: £195. The electrician's first quote to clear everything on the report was £1,340, which included replacing the consumer unit to add RCD protection.

Itemised by code, the two C2s came to £385: bonding conductor run and clamped, socket back box replaced and tested. The £955 balance was the board, a C3 item, legally optional.

Five-year cost of compliance: £195 plus £385, or £580. That is £116 a year.

They chose to do the consumer unit anyway, twelve months later and out of a different budget, at a price they had time to compare. Same work, no 28-day clock, and a quote obtained without an unsatisfactory report sitting on the table.

6. The honest verdict

Budget the inspection as a fixed five-yearly cost and treat the remedial bill as the variable. If the property is older than about 1990 and has not been rewired, assume four figures and be pleasantly surprised rather than the reverse.

Always ask for the remedial quote broken down by observation code. C1, C2 and FI are the law and get done inside 28 days. C3 is a suggestion, and a suggestion you can take a year to price properly.

Do not book the cheapest inspection you can find. An inspector who codes conservatively to generate work costs you far more than the £60 you saved on the visit, and one who codes too leniently leaves you holding a property that is not actually safe.

Frequently asked

How often does an EICR have to be done? At least every five years for a private rented property in England, or more often if the report itself specifies a shorter interval. The inspector can set a shorter date and that date binds you.

What happens if I miss the 28-day remedial deadline? The local housing authority can serve a remedial notice, carry out the work itself and recover the cost, and impose a financial penalty of up to £30,000 for a breach.

Does a new build need one straight away? A new installation comes with an Electrical Installation Certificate rather than an EICR. The first EICR is then due within five years of that certificate.

Can the tenant refuse access? A tenant cannot unreasonably refuse, and the regulations recognise that a landlord who has taken all reasonable steps and kept evidence of them is not in breach. Keep every letter, message and attempted appointment.
