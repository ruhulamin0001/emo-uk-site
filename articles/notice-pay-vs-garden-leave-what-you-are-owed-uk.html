Your employer cannot just pay you off instead of your notice, unless you let them

Notice, garden leave and pay in lieu are three different things that pay differently and carry different rights. The rule that surprises people most is that pay in lieu of notice needs either a contract term or your agreement.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Statutory minimum notice from your employer is at least one week if employed between one month and 2 years, one week for each year between 2 and 12 years, and 12 weeks if employed 12 years or more.
  • Your contract can give more, never less. The statutory figures are a floor.
  • Pay in lieu of notice needs your contract to allow it, or your agreement. If neither applies, you can work your notice.
  • Garden leave pays the same as working your notice: the same pay and the same contractual benefits.
  • You keep bonuses and commission during notice unless your contract says otherwise.

1. The statutory minimums, in full

There is a legal floor on how much notice your employer must give you, and it is set by length of service. GOV.UK states the statutory notice periods as:

  • at least one week's notice if employed between one month and 2 years
  • one week's notice for each year if employed between 2 and 12 years
  • 12 weeks' notice if employed for 12 years or more

Two points that matter. The 12 week figure is a cap on the statutory minimum, not a cap on your notice: someone with 25 years of service is entitled to 12 weeks under statute, and possibly far more under their contract.

And the direction only runs one way. Your employer may give you more than the statutory minimum, but they cannot give you less. If your contract says less than the statutory figure, the statutory figure wins.

Going the other way, if you are the one resigning, you must give at least a week's notice if you have been in the job more than a month, and your contract may require more.

2. Three different things that people mix up

Working your notice. You keep turning up, you keep being paid normally, and your employment ends at the end of the notice period.

Garden leave. Your employer asks you not to come into work, or to work from home or another location, during your notice period. GOV.UK is explicit about the effect on your money: you get the same pay and contractual benefits. You are still employed. Your employment still ends at the end of the notice period. That last point matters, because it means your continuous service keeps running.

Payment in lieu of notice, usually shortened to PILON. Your employment ends immediately and you receive a payment instead of the notice period. Your employment has ended on day one, not at the end of the notice period.

The difference between garden leave and PILON looks cosmetic and is not. Under garden leave you remain an employee, which affects continuous service, benefits that run with employment, and restrictions in your contract. Under PILON you are out.

3. The consent rule almost nobody knows

This is the sentence worth remembering from the whole page.

You can only get payment in lieu if it is in your contract or you agree to it. If you do not agree to it, you can work out your notice period.

That is GOV.UK's own wording, and it reverses the assumption most people bring to the conversation. A great many employees believe that if an employer says "we will pay you for your notice, clear your desk today", they simply have to accept it.

They do not, unless the contract contains a PILON clause. Many contracts do contain one, particularly in larger organisations, so the first thing to do is read your contract rather than assume either way.

Where this genuinely changes outcomes is when the notice period spans something with a date attached: a bonus payment date, a share vesting date, a pension milestone, or simply the qualifying length of service for something. Whether you are employed on that date can be worth a great deal more than the notice pay itself.

4. What your notice pay is calculated on

For redundancy, GOV.UK states that your notice pay is based on the average you earned per week over the 12 weeks before your notice period starts.

That reference period is worth knowing if your earnings vary. Twelve weeks of unusually low hours immediately before notice will pull the average down, and twelve weeks including overtime or commission will pull it up.

On the components of pay, you are entitled to bonuses and commission during your notice period unless your contract states otherwise. You will usually get your normal pay rate during notice, with specific exceptions set out on GOV.UK for periods when you are off sick, on holiday, on maternity, paternity, adoption or shared parental leave, or willing to work while your employer has asked you not to because there is not enough work.

5. A worked example: eight years of service

Harriet has worked for a distribution firm in Wakefield for eight years and four months. She is being made redundant. Her contract is silent on notice, so the statutory minimum applies, and it contains no PILON clause.

Her service falls in the 2 to 12 year band, so her statutory notice is one week per full year of service. Eight full years means eight weeks' notice. The extra four months does not add a ninth week, because the entitlement counts complete years.

Her employer offers to end her employment immediately with a payment covering those eight weeks. Because there is no PILON clause in her contract, that offer requires her agreement. She can decline it and work her eight weeks instead.

Whether she should decline depends on facts we do not have and she does. If a bonus or a service related entitlement falls inside those eight weeks, staying employed through it may be worth more than leaving early. If she has another job to go to, leaving immediately with the money may be worth more than the eight weeks.

We are deliberately not putting a pounds figure on her notice pay. It would be calculated on her average weekly earnings over the 12 weeks before notice starts, and we do not know her hours, overtime or commission. Publishing a number would make this example look more useful and actually make it less true.

Frequently asked

What is the minimum notice my employer must give me? At least one week if you have been employed between one month and 2 years, one week for each year between 2 and 12 years, and 12 weeks if you have been employed 12 years or more. Your contract can give more but not less.

Can my employer force me to accept pay in lieu of notice? Only if pay in lieu of notice is in your contract. GOV.UK states that you can only get payment in lieu if it is in your contract or you agree to it, and if you do not agree you can work out your notice period.

Does garden leave pay less than working my notice? No. GOV.UK states you get the same pay and contractual benefits on garden leave. You also remain employed, which is not true of pay in lieu of notice.

Do I still get commission during my notice period? You are entitled to bonuses and commission during your notice period unless your contract states otherwise.
