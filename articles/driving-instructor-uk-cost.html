Three Attempts, Two Years, And A Two-Year Lockout: The Driving Instructor Route Priced Honestly

Becoming an approved driving instructor is sold as a flexible route into self-employment, and the training adverts lead with earnings. The part that decides whether it works for you is not the fee schedule but the structure: three attempts at each of the hard tests, a two-year clock, and a two-year wait if you run out of either.

1. The route, in the order it actually happens

To teach people to drive for money in Great Britain you have to be on the DVSA register of approved driving instructors. Getting there is a fixed sequence, and DVSA sets it out as a step by step process:

  1. Check you are eligible and that the role suits you.
  2. Apply to become a driving instructor, which includes a criminal record check.
  3. Get a trainer. Training is not compulsory, but the part 3 pass standard makes going it alone a very hard route.
  4. ADI part 1, the theory test.
  5. ADI part 2, the driving ability test. This tests your own driving, not your teaching, against the national standard for driving cars.
  6. Get practice teaching, which is where the trainee licence in section 4 comes in.
  7. ADI part 3, the instructional ability test. This is a test of your ability to teach pupils.
  8. Register and get your ADI badge.

Two things about that list matter more than the order. Parts 2 and 3 test completely different skills, so being a very good driver tells you almost nothing about whether you will pass part 3. And the process is different in Northern Ireland, where fees and rules are set separately, so everything here covers Great Britain.

2. Every DVSA fee, and what is missing from that list

These are DVSA's published fees. They are the only figures in this article we did not calculate.

FeeCost
ADI part 1 test£81
ADI part 2 test£111
ADI part 3 test£111
First ADI certificate, the "badge"£300
Trainee driving instructor licence£140
Renewal or re-registration, every 4 years£300
Replacement badge£3

A clean run costs £603 in DVSA fees: £81 plus £111 plus £111 plus £300. That is our own addition.

DVSA also states plainly that you pay the full amount again if you do not pass a test and want another attempt. There is no reduced retake fee.

Now the part that is missing from that table, and it is the larger number. Training is not a DVSA fee. DVSA says so directly: fees for driving instructor training vary and are set by training organisations. We are not going to publish a training price range, because it is provider-set, varies enormously by hours and format, and any figure we printed would be a guess dressed as research. Get written quotes from at least two providers, and make sure the quote states the number of hours, not just a headline price.

Other fees exist once you are qualified and are optional: the Pass Plus starter pack is £37 with refills at £29, the DVSA car special test is £144, fleet driver trainer registration is £120, and ORDIT qualifying assessment is £151.20 if you later want to train instructors yourself.

3. The three attempts and the two year clock

This is the section that should decide whether you commit, and it is the one the training adverts do not lead with. DVSA's ADI register guide sets out the limits:

TestWhat it testsAttempts allowed
ADI part 1TheoryUnlimited
ADI part 2Driving ability3 attempts
ADI part 3Instructional ability3 attempts

Two separate constraints then sit on top.

The clock. You must have booked the ADI part 3 test within two years of passing the part 1 test. If you do not qualify by then, you restart the process.

The lockout. If you use up all your attempts, you restart, and you have to wait until two years after you passed part 1 before you can start again.

Sit with what that combination means. Somebody who passes part 1, takes their time arranging training, then fails part 3 three times has spent the fees, spent the training money, and cannot legally begin the process again until the two years from part 1 have run out. There is no appeal to effort here. It is a hard stop.

This is why the honest advice about part 3 is not "practise more", it is do not book it until your trainer thinks you would pass today. An attempt used up early, to see what the test is like, is a third of your allowance spent on reconnaissance.

4. The trainee licence, and why it exists

After you pass part 2 you can apply for a trainee driving instructor licence, which costs £140. DVSA's register guide describes it as allowing you to get six months of experience providing on-road instruction before your part 3 test.

It is worth understanding what problem this solves. Part 3 is a test of teaching, assessed with a real pupil in a real lesson. You cannot get good at that by reading, and you cannot legally charge for lessons before you qualify. The trainee licence is the bridge: it lets you teach, for money, under supervision arrangements, while you build the skill the final test measures.

So the £140 is not really a cost. It is the difference between arriving at part 3 having taught for six months and arriving having practised on your trainer. Given that you only get three attempts, that is a very cheap way to protect them.

What it is not is a shortcut to earning. A trainee licence comes with conditions about supervision and the training you must have completed, so treat it as a structured apprenticeship rather than an early badge.

5. Worked example: three cost scenarios

Worked example: Yusuf in Leicester, three ways it could go

Yusuf is 34, has held a full licence for twelve years, and wants to teach part time before going full time. All figures below are DVSA fees only, added up by us. None of them include training, which is the larger and more variable number.

Scenario A, a clean run. Passes all three first time. £81 plus £111 plus £111 plus a £300 badge = £603. With a trainee licence to build experience before part 3, £743.

Scenario B, one retake each at parts 2 and 3. £81 plus two part 2 attempts at £111 plus two part 3 attempts at £111 plus the £300 badge = £825. Unremarkable, and still a working outcome.

Scenario C, the one to plan against. Passes part 1 at £81, uses all three part 2 attempts at £333, and all three part 3 attempts at £333. He has spent £747 in DVSA fees and is not an instructor. He must restart, and he cannot begin again until two years after he passed part 1. Add the training he paid for and the number is a great deal worse.

The point of scenario C is not that it is likely. It is that it is the reason to be slow about booking part 3 and to take the trainee licence route. The cost of an extra month of preparation is small. The cost of a third failed attempt is two years.

Once he is in, registration lasts four years and renewal is £300, which works out at £75 a year to stay on the register.

6. The honest verdict

The DVSA side of this is cheap and completely predictable: £603 for a clean run, £75 a year to stay registered afterwards. If someone has told you the qualification is expensive, they were talking about training, which is set by private providers and is where nearly all of the money goes.

What is not cheap is the risk structure. Three attempts at part 2 and three at part 3, a two-year clock from passing part 1, and a two-year lockout if you exhaust either. That is unusually strict for a UK vocational qualification and it changes how you should approach it.

So the practical advice is short and slightly boring. Get quotes from two training providers in writing, with hours specified. Use the £140 trainee licence to teach for six real months before part 3 rather than after it. And do not book part 3 to find out what it is like, because you only have three of those and they do not come back.

Frequently asked

Do I need training, or can I just book the tests? Training is not legally required, but DVSA's own step by step route includes getting a trainer, and part 3 assesses teaching skill that most people do not already have. Given you get three attempts, going in untrained is an expensive way to find out what the test involves.

Is it different in Northern Ireland? Yes. DVSA points to nidirect for the Northern Ireland process, and both the fees and the rules are set separately. Everything in this article covers Great Britain.

What happens after four years? Registration lasts four years and renewal or re-registration costs GBP 300. There are also standards checks during your registration, so staying on the register is about continuing to meet the standard rather than simply paying.

Can I teach in an automatic car? Yes, and DVSA's eligibility page specifically covers what rules apply if you have an automatic or a manual licence, because it affects what you are allowed to teach. Check that page before you pay for any training, since it can change the plan entirely.
