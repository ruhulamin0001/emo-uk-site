Your pothole claim is decided by a filing cabinet, not by your tyre

Councils reject most of these, and they are usually entitled to. The reason sits in one section of the Highways Act 1980, and once you have read it the whole process makes a different kind of sense. It also tells you what to ask for first.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Section 58 of the Highways Act 1980 is the whole game. It is a defence for the authority to prove it took such care as in all the circumstances was reasonably required.
  • The court must have regard to five specific matters, and the fourth is whether the authority knew or could reasonably have been expected to know of the danger.
  • Hiring a contractor is not a defence on its own. The section says so directly, unless the authority also proves it gave proper instructions and that they were carried out.
  • The section binds the Crown, and extends to England and Wales.
  • It does not remove contributory negligence, which is stated on the face of the section.
  • The inspection record is the document to ask for, because it is the evidence the defence is built from.

1. What section 58 actually says

The Highways Act 1980 puts a duty on a highway authority to maintain a highway maintainable at the public expense. If it fails and you suffer damage, you can sue. Section 58 is the answer the authority gets to give back, and it is titled, in the Act's own words, a special defence in action against a highway authority for damages for non repair of highway.

Subsection (1) says that in such an action it is a defence, without prejudice to any other defence or the application of the law relating to contributory negligence, to prove that the authority had taken such care as in all the circumstances was reasonably required to secure that the part of the highway to which the action relates was not dangerous for traffic.

Read that twice, because two things are hiding in it. First, the test is about care taken, not about the state of the road. A road can be genuinely dangerous and the authority can still win, if it can show it did what was reasonably required. Second, contributory negligence survives, so your own driving is still in scope.

The version above is the text as it stands on legislation.gov.uk, up to date with all changes known to be in force on or before 10 September 2026. Subsection (4) was repealed on 1 January 1993 by the New Roads and Street Works Act 1991. Subsection (3) says the section binds the Crown. The section extends to England and Wales.

2. The five matters a court must weigh

Subsection (2) tells the court what to have particular regard to. There are five, and they are not vague.

  • (a) the character of the highway, and the traffic which was reasonably to be expected to use it. A rural lane and an urban A road are not held to the same standard.
  • (b) the standard of maintenance appropriate for a highway of that character used by that traffic.
  • (c) the state of repair in which a reasonable person would have expected to find the highway.
  • (d) whether the authority knew, or could reasonably have been expected to know, that the condition of that part of the highway was likely to cause danger to users.
  • (e) where the authority could not reasonably have been expected to repair it before the cause of action arose, what warning notices of its condition had been displayed.

Limb (d) is where nearly everything turns. Authorities run inspection regimes with set frequencies by road category, and they log defects and repair them to a timetable. If the last inspection found nothing, and nobody reported this defect, the authority has a straightforward answer to (d).

Then there is the sentence at the end of subsection (2) that almost nobody quotes, and it cuts the other way. It is not relevant for the authority to prove it arranged for a competent person to carry out or supervise the maintenance, unless it also proves that it gave that person proper instructions with regard to the maintenance and that he carried out the instructions. Outsourcing the work does not outsource the defence. The authority has to stand behind the instructions and the delivery.

The five matters as a document checklist

Set out as a table, the five limbs stop being a legal recitation and become a list of things to ask for. The middle column is the sentence the authority's insurer writes back; the right-hand column is the piece of paper that either supports it or does not.

(c) the state of repair a reasonable person would expect to findWhether this defect was beyond ordinary wear"Every road carries some surface damage"Your own dated photograph with a ruler or a coin in the frame for scale
(d) whether the authority knew, or could reasonably have been expected to knowIn practice, the whole case"The last inspection was clear and no report had been received"The inspection records for that section for the twelve months before, and the log of public reports
(e) warning notices, where repair before the cause of action was not reasonableOnly reached if the authority knew and could not repair in time"Signage was in place"Photographs of the site exactly as you found it

Two of those five are answered by a document you do not have and can get. The inspection policy is usually published on the authority's own website. The inspection records and the report log are not, and a Freedom of Information request is the route to them. Under section 10(1) of the Freedom of Information Act 2000 a public authority must respond promptly and in any event by the twentieth working day after receipt, so a request sent the week the damage happens is answered inside a month, well before any court deadline bites. Ask for one thing only: the inspection records and defect reports for the named section of road for the twelve months up to the date of the damage.

The honest part: if those records come back showing an inspection eleven days before, nothing found, and no public report on the log, the s.58 defence is made out and there is no claim worth bringing. That is the answer in most cases, and finding it out for the price of an email is a better outcome than finding it out after paying a court fee.

3. A worked example, and the document to ask for

Marcus in Stoke on Trent hits a pothole on a B road at night. The garage invoice comes to £430 for a tyre and a buckled alloy. He submits a claim to the county council and gets a rejection letter citing section 58.

Nothing about his £430 is in dispute, and that is the point people find hardest. The council is not saying the pothole was not there or that the damage did not happen. It is saying it took reasonable care.

So the useful move is not to send the invoice again with a stronger covering letter. It is to ask for the evidence the defence is made of. Write to the council and ask for the inspection frequency assigned to that road, the date of the last inspection before the incident, what that inspection recorded at that location, any reports from the public about that defect, and the intervention level and repair timetable that applied to it.

Say the last scheduled inspection was 11 days before, logged as no defect, and a member of the public reported the hole 3 days before. Now limb (d) is live, because the authority arguably knew, and the question becomes whether three days was a reasonable time to act given the road's category. Say instead the last inspection was 11 days before with nothing recorded and no public report at all. Limb (d) is answered and the claim is very likely finished.

The figures above are a worked illustration of how the test is applied, not data about any real council. What is not an illustration is the list of things to ask for, because that list is drawn straight from the words of subsection (2).

The verdict

For most single pothole claims, the honest answer is that this will not get you your money, and section 58 is the reason. A council with a documented inspection regime that it actually followed has a defence written into primary legislation, and no amount of photographs of the hole changes that.

Where it is worth pursuing is the narrow case where the authority already knew. A previous report, a repair that failed, a defect logged and left past its own timetable, or a contractor instruction that was never followed up. Those attack limb (d) or the final sentence of subsection (2), and those are the arguments the section leaves open.

Before you spend an evening on it, work out which of those you can actually evidence. If the answer is none of them, your time is better spent on the insurance excess maths than on the claim form.

Frequently asked

Does section 58 apply in Scotland or Northern Ireland? No. The section extends to England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own highways legislation.

Can the council blame its contractor? Not by itself. Subsection (2) says proving it arranged for a competent person is not relevant unless the authority also proves it gave proper instructions and that they were carried out.

Is it worth claiming if the damage is under my insurance excess? That is usually the only reason to bother, since claiming on insurance may cost you your no claims discount. Weigh the excess and the discount against a claim that section 58 makes hard to win.

Does taking a photograph of the pothole help? It proves the defect and the location, which you need, but it does not touch the defence. The defence is about the authority's system of inspection and repair, not about the size of the hole.
