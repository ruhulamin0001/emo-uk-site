You Are Budgeting for the Bath. The Bill Is Mostly People

Almost everyone plans a bathroom by choosing a suite and some tiles, then discovers the quote is three times what those cost. Labour is commonly 40 to 60 per cent of a bathroom refit, because a small room needs four trades taking turns in it. And the single most expensive decision in the whole project is one people make for looks: whether the toilet stays where it is.

The short version

  • A full refit of an ordinary UK bathroom is about £4,000 to £9,000. Small en suites can come in below, large or wet room projects well above.
  • Labour is commonly 40 to 60 per cent of it. Four trades, one small room, and they cannot all work at once.
  • Keeping the layout is the biggest saving available. Moving the soil pipe is what separates the bottom of the range from the top, and the decision is usually made for aesthetic reasons.
  • It is notifiable work. GOV.UK lists installing a bathroom that will involve plumbing, and changing electrics near a bath or shower, as work that may need building regulations approval.
  • The suite is the part you can flex and it changes the total least. The difference between a budget and a mid range suite is often a few hundred pounds against a five figure job.
  • Cost per year is the honest frame. A £6,000 bathroom over a 15 year life is £400 a year. A £4,000 one that you dislike and redo in seven is £571.

1. Where the money actually goes

A bathroom is the most labour dense room in a house. It is small, it involves plumbing, electrics, tiling, plastering and sometimes joinery, and none of those trades can be in there at the same time as another. That sequencing is why a three by two metre room takes a fortnight and why the labour share is so high.

The published breakdowns from UK bathroom fitters and builders vary in how they categorise things, but they converge on the same headline: labour is roughly 40 to 60 per cent of the total on a mid specification job that keeps the existing layout. Some breakdowns split it further into around a third for the suite and fittings, a quarter for labour, a fifth for tiles and materials, and a fifth for the plumbing and electrical work, which puts labour higher still once you count the trades separately.

Those figures come from firms that fit bathrooms, so they are the people who set the labour price. That is worth knowing, though it cuts both ways: an industry with an interest in looking cheap would understate its own labour share, not overstate it.

The practical consequence is the thing to take away. If labour is half the bill and it is fixed by the room rather than by your choices, then the half you control is the materials, and the materials are the half that changes the total least.

Downgrading the suite saves hundreds. Keeping the layout saves thousands. Most people do the first and not the second.

2. The soil pipe decision

The toilet connects to a soil pipe, which is a large diameter waste that has to run at a fall to reach the stack. Baths and basins have small, flexible wastes that can be moved fairly freely. The soil pipe is the one that does not move easily.

Relocating a toilet means one of three things, and all three are expensive:

  1. Running new soil pipework under the floor. On a suspended timber floor this means cutting joists, which needs doing properly and sometimes needs structural support. On a concrete floor it can mean breaking up the slab.
  2. Boxing a new run at high level or through a bulkhead. Cheaper, uglier, and it eats the room you were trying to gain.
  3. A macerator. Which introduces a mechanical item that can fail, is noisy, and restricts what can be flushed. Fine in a converted cupboard, a compromise in a main bathroom.

The cost of moving the toilet, once you include the floor work and the making good, is commonly £1,000 to £2,500 on its own. And in most refits the reason it is being moved is that the new layout looks better in the showroom plan.

That is a legitimate thing to want. It is just worth pricing it as a separate line and deciding on it deliberately, rather than letting it arrive inside a single total where it is invisible.

The two cheapest rows per year are the two that most people skip past on the way to the showroom. A cosmetic refresh at £75 to £225 a year and a bath swap at £54 to £133 a year both beat every full refit in the table, often by a factor of three.

They also do not solve the same problem. If your bathroom is tired, they solve it completely. If it leaks, or the layout genuinely does not work for your household, they do not solve it at all. The point of the table is that you should know which problem you actually have before you spend £8,000 on it.

4. Building regulations, and the certificate

GOV.UK lists, among work that may need building regulations approval, installing a bathroom that will involve plumbing and changing electrics near a bath or shower. Both of those describe an ordinary refit.

As with any notifiable work, there are two routes: your tradesperson is registered with a competent person scheme and self certifies, or you apply to building control yourself before the work starts. GOV.UK states plainly that you do not need to get approval yourself if you use someone registered with a competent person scheme.

The consequence of doing neither is the same as for a rewire, and the same sentence applies: without approval you will not have the certificates of compliance you may need when you want to sell your home. Our guide to rewiring a house goes into the certificate problem in detail, and the logic is identical here.

One more, newer point: GOV.UK notes that from 1 October 2026, applications for building control approval and initial notices for certain residential buildings may be subject to the Building Safety Levy. If your bathroom job needs an application rather than self certification, ask whether the quote allows for it.

The bathroom specific item to ask about is the electrical work. Lighting, extractor fans, shaver sockets and electric showers near a bath or shower fall into defined zones with specific requirements. This is the part of a bathroom that a general builder is least likely to be certified for, so the question to ask is which trade is doing the electrics and which scheme they belong to.

5. Worked example: the Kowalczyk bathroom in Derby

A 1990s three bedroom house in Derby with the original bathroom. Bath, basin, toilet, over bath shower. Everything works, the tiles are dated, the bath enamel is scratched and the sealant is going black no matter how often it is replaced. The same tired-but-working problem turns up in kitchens, where the cheaper answer is often respraying the kitchen you already have instead of replacing it.

They get two quotes from the same fitter.

Option one, same layout. New bath, basin, toilet, taps, screen, full retile, new extractor, new flooring. £5,900.

Option two, layout swapped so the bath sits under the window and the toilet moves to the opposite wall. Same suite, same tiles. £8,400.

The difference. £2,500, essentially all of it the soil pipe, the floor work and the making good.

Over a 15 year life. Option one is £393 a year. Option two is £560 a year.

The third option nobody quoted them. Replace the bath and screen, regrout and reseal, new taps, repaint, about £1,300, or £108 a year over 12 years.

They took option one. The honest observation is that the third option would have addressed every complaint on their list, because their complaints were the bath, the sealant and the look of it. The layout was not among them. That is the commonest way a bathroom budget doubles: a list of small annoyances gets answered with a whole room.

Where option two would have been right is if the room genuinely did not work, for instance if a family needed a shower rather than an over bath one, or if somebody in the household needed level access. Those are functional reasons and they justify the £2,500. Wanting the bath under the window does not, at £167 a year for fifteen years.

Frequently asked

Should I use a bathroom company or individual trades? A fitting company is one point of contact, one warranty and one person to chase, and you pay a management margin for that. Individual trades are cheaper and you become the project manager, which means you schedule the plumber, the tiler and the electrician in the right order and absorb the delay when one of them slips. If you have never done it, the margin is usually worth paying.

How long will I be without a bathroom? Typically seven to fourteen working days for a straightforward refit, longer if the layout moves or if there is rot or damage under the old suite. Plan on having no bathroom at all for at least half of it.

Does a new bathroom add value? Less than people expect, and less than a kitchen. A bathroom in poor condition costs you at sale, so fixing a bad one protects value. Upgrading an adequate one to a very good one rarely returns its cost. Spend accordingly.

What about the water bill? A refit is a good moment to think about it if you are metered, particularly if you are moving from a bath heavy household to a shower heavy one, though the two are closer than people assume. Our guides to whether a water meter is worth it and electric versus mixer showers have the arithmetic.

The honest verdict

Write down what is actually wrong with your bathroom before you get a quote. If the answer is "it looks tired and the sealant is black", a £300 to £1,600 refresh fixes it at a quarter of the cost per year of a full refit, and no one in the industry has any reason to tell you that.

If you do need a full refit, keep the layout unless there is a functional reason to change it. Moving the toilet is commonly £1,000 to £2,500 and it is the only line in the project where you can save thousands with a single decision. The suite, which is what everyone agonises over, moves the total by hundreds.

Whoever does the work, make sure the plumbing and the electrics are certified by someone registered with a competent person scheme, and get the paperwork before you pay the final invoice. That is the part that costs you nothing today and costs you a sale later.
