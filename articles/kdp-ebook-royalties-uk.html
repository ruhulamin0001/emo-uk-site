The 70% Option Wins At Almost Any Price. The Number That Actually Costs You Is Megabytes

Everyone frames the Kindle royalty choice as a decision between two rates. Read Amazon's own pricing page and it is not really a decision at all: for a normal text book the 70% option pays more at any price above 20p. What varies, and what almost no author checks, is the delivery cost Amazon deducts per megabyte before paying you.

The short version

  • UK VAT on eBooks is 0%, and has been since 1 May 2020, per Amazon's own royalty page. So a UK author's royalty calculation has no VAT deduction in it at all.
  • Delivery cost on Amazon.co.uk is £0.10 per megabyte, deducted only under the 70% option, rounded up to the nearest kilobyte, minimum 1p.
  • On a £2.99 book of 1MB you earn £2.02 at 70% and £1.05 at 35%. The 70% option pays 1.9 times as much.
  • 70% beats 35% at any price above twice your delivery cost. For a 1MB text book that is 20p. For a 10MB illustrated book it is £2.
  • A 5MB file costs you 28p a sale against a 1MB one on the same £2.99 price. Over 500 sales that is £140 of royalty lost to unoptimised images.
  • The 70% option is conditional. Territory, list price range, and being at least 20% below any physical edition all have to hold, or you drop to 35%.

1. The UK detail that changes the sum

Amazon's Digital Book Pricing Page, last updated 21 April 2026, gives the two royalty formulas:

  • 35% option: 0.35 times (List Price minus applicable VAT). No delivery cost is deducted.
  • 70% option: 0.70 times (List Price minus applicable VAT minus Delivery Costs), and only for sales in Amazon's listed Available Sales Territories, which include the United Kingdom.

Almost every guide to Kindle royalties works these through using Amazon's own German example, where VAT of 7% comes off before anything else. That is the right example for a German author and the wrong one for you.

On the same page Amazon states it plainly: as of 1 May 2020, the UK VAT rate applicable to eBooks is 0%.

So for a sale on Amazon.co.uk, the formulas collapse to something much simpler:

  • 35%: 0.35 times List Price
  • 70%: 0.70 times (List Price minus Delivery Costs)

That is the whole calculation for a British author selling to British readers. No VAT step, no grossing up, no dividing by 1.2. It also means the numbers you have seen in American and European guides understate what a UK sale is worth to you.

One caveat worth keeping in view: that 0% applies to UK sales. If a meaningful share of your sales come from other Amazon stores, the VAT of that country comes off those sales, and Amazon's page shows the working for Germany at 7%. The clean sum above is a UK sales sum.

2. When 70% actually beats 35%

Set the two formulas against each other and the answer is less of a judgement call than the framing suggests.

The 70% option pays more whenever 0.70 times (Price minus Delivery) is greater than 0.35 times Price. Rearranged, that is whenever your price is more than twice your delivery cost.

For a typical text-only novel or non-fiction book of around 1MB, the delivery cost is 10p, so the crossover is 20p. There is no realistic price at which the 35% option pays a UK author more for an ordinary text book.

At £2.99 with a 1MB file, on a UK sale:

  • 70%: 0.70 times (£2.99 minus £0.10) equals £2.02
  • 35%: 0.35 times £2.99 equals £1.05

That is 97p a sale, or 1.9 times as much money for the identical book. Over a thousand sales it is the difference between £2,020 and £1,050.

The crossover only becomes interesting when the file gets large. At 10MB the delivery cost is £1.00 and the crossover moves to £2.00. Price a 10MB illustrated book at £1.99 and the 35% option genuinely pays more: 0.35 times £1.99 is 70p, against 0.70 times (£1.99 minus £1.00) which is 69p. A one penny difference, but it is the only situation where the 35% option is the rational choice rather than an imposed one.

So the practical rule for a UK author: take 70% unless you are not allowed to, or unless your file is very large and your price is very low.

3. The delivery cost nobody checks

This is the part of the royalty equation that authors almost never look at, and the only part they can actually change after publication.

Amazon's page sets the delivery rate by store. On Amazon.co.uk it is £0.10 per megabyte. For comparison, Amazon.com is US $0.15/MB and the euro stores are €0.12/MB. File sizes are rounded up to the nearest kilobyte, and there is a minimum delivery cost of 1p for sales in pounds regardless of how small the file is.

Now hold the price at £2.99 and vary only the file:

  • 1MB: 0.70 times (£2.99 minus £0.10) equals £2.02
  • 3MB: 0.70 times (£2.99 minus £0.30) equals £1.88
  • 5MB: 0.70 times (£2.99 minus £0.50) equals £1.74
  • 10MB: 0.70 times (£2.99 minus £1.00) equals £1.39

Between a 1MB file and a 5MB one, the same book at the same price earns 28p less per sale, which is 14% of the royalty. At 10MB it is 63p less, nearly a third.

What makes a file large is almost always images. A cover thumbnail is not the problem; interior illustrations, scanned diagrams, high resolution photographs and decorative chapter headers are. A book with twenty photographs saved at print resolution can easily be five times the size of the same book with the same photographs saved sensibly for a screen.

Two honest limits on this advice. First, do not degrade a book that needs its images: a photography book is supposed to look like one, and 63p a sale is a reasonable price for that. Second, this only applies under the 70% option, because Amazon does not deduct delivery costs under 35% at all. That is the one genuine advantage the 35% option has, and it is almost never large enough to matter.

For anyone selling a text book, though, this is close to free money. Check your file size on your Bookshelf. If it is several megabytes and your book is words, something in it is bigger than it needs to be.

4. Why you might be stuck on 35% anyway

Amazon attaches real conditions to the 70% option, and failing any of them puts you on 35% whether you chose it or not.

Territory. The 70% rate only applies to sales in Amazon's listed Available Sales Territories. The United Kingdom is on that list, along with most of Europe, the United States, Canada, Australia and many others. Sales outside those territories pay at the 35% rate regardless of which option you picked.

List price range. Amazon requires your list price to sit inside published minimum and maximum figures for the 70% option, and those figures live on a separate KDP page. Check them before you price, because this is the condition that catches most people.

The 20% rule against print. If you choose 70%, Amazon requires you to set and adjust your list price so that it is at least 20% below the list price on Amazon for any physical edition of the same book. Publish a paperback and forget to revisit the ebook price and you can fall out of eligibility without touching the ebook at all.

Public domain content. Books consisting primarily of public domain content are only eligible for the 35% option.

Price matching. Amazon may sell your book below your list price to match a third party or its own physical edition. Under the 70% option your royalty is then calculated on the price Amazon actually sold at. And if price matching pushes the Amazon price below the minimum 70% list price requirement, Amazon's page says you will earn 35% royalty on it.

Brazil, Japan, Mexico and India need KDP Select enrolment for the 70% rate, on top of meeting the local list price requirements.

One clause deserves to be read slowly. Amazon states that if at any time your book does not meet the 70% requirements, the royalty will be as provided under the 35% option, and that it can adjust previously reported or paid royalties on that basis. Royalties already shown in your reports are not necessarily final.

5. Worked example: Iona in Inverness, 500 sales

Iona writes walking guides in Inverness. Her first book is 140 pages with 22 photographs, priced at £3.49, sold almost entirely to UK readers, and she is on the 70% option.

As published. The photographs went in at full camera resolution and the file came out at 8MB. Delivery cost is £0.80.

Royalty per sale: 0.70 times (£3.49 minus £0.00 VAT minus £0.80) equals £1.88.

After resizing the images for screen rather than print, the file drops to 2MB. Delivery cost is £0.20.

Royalty per sale: 0.70 times (£3.49 minus £0.20) equals £2.30.

The difference is 42p a sale. Across 500 sales that is £210, for an afternoon of work in an image editor and a re-upload. The reader sees the same photographs on the same screen.

What she should not do. She considered dropping the price to £1.99 to shift more copies. At 2MB that would pay 0.70 times (£1.99 minus £0.20), or £1.25 a sale. She would need 920 sales at £1.99 to match 500 at £3.49, an 84% increase in volume to stand still. The file size change earned her more than the price cut plausibly could, and cost her nothing.

Tax. Her royalties are trading income. Gross income over the £1,000 trading allowance in a tax year means telling HMRC, which at £2.30 a sale is about 435 sales. KDP also runs a tax interview at signup, and Amazon can withhold royalties until tax information is provided. The £90,000 VAT registration threshold is a long way off.

One last thing she checks. Her paperback lists at £8.99, so her £3.49 ebook is comfortably more than 20% below it and her 70% eligibility is safe. Had she priced the ebook at £7.99, it would not have been.

The honest verdict

For a UK author the royalty choice is barely a choice. UK VAT on ebooks is zero, delivery is 10p a megabyte, and the 70% option pays more at any price above about 20p on a normal text file. Take 70% and spend your attention elsewhere.

Elsewhere means file size, which is the only input in Amazon's formula you can change after publishing and the one nobody looks at. A 5MB book costs you 28p a sale against a 1MB one at the same price. Over a few hundred sales that is real money for an afternoon of resizing images.

The conditions are where the surprises are. Territory, the published list price range, the rule that your ebook must be at least 20% below any physical edition, public domain content, and price matching can all drop you to 35% without you doing anything. Amazon also reserves the right to restate royalties it has already reported.

And keep the volume fantasy in check. At £2.99 and 70% you clear the £1,000 trading allowance somewhere around 495 sales. That is the honest scale of a first book, and it is a long way from the numbers in the publishing courses.

Frequently asked

Is the 70% option always better? For a UK author selling a normal text book, effectively yes: it pays more at any price above twice the delivery cost, which is about 20p on a 1MB file. The exception is a very large file at a very low price, where the delivery deduction can outweigh the higher rate.

Do UK authors pay VAT on Kindle royalties? Amazon's pricing page states that as of 1 May 2020 the UK VAT rate applicable to eBooks is 0%, so no VAT is deducted from the royalty calculation on a UK sale. Sales in other countries have that country's VAT deducted, and your own UK tax position on the royalties you receive is a separate matter.

How do I find my book's file size? It is shown in your KDP Bookshelf against the published title. If it is more than a megabyte or two and your book is mostly words, images or embedded fonts are almost certainly the cause.

What happens if my book stops qualifying for 70%? Amazon's terms say the royalty becomes the 35% option's, and that Amazon can adjust previously reported or paid royalties accordingly. The most common causes are price matching pushing the price below the minimum, or a paperback price change breaking the 20% rule.
