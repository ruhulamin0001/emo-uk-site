The Cheapest Saving in Your House Is a Dial You Already Own

Almost every combi boiler in Britain is running hotter than it needs to, because that is how it was left on the day it was fitted. Turning the flow temperature down to 60C costs nothing at all and saves roughly 9% of the gas you burn. At October 2026 prices that is about £68 a year. There is a catch, and it is not the one people expect.

The short version

  • The saving is about 9% of your gas. Nesta, which ran the national Money Saving Boiler Challenge, puts the figure at around 9% of a gas bill when a condensing combi is turned down from 80C to 60C.
  • At the October 2026 cap that is roughly £68 a year. A typical household burns 9,500 kWh of gas. At 7.97p per kWh that is £757 of gas units, and 9% of it is £68.
  • Payback is immediate. The cost is zero, so there is no payback period to calculate. This is the only item on this site with that property.
  • It does not make your house colder. The flow temperature is how hot the water going to your radiators is, not how warm your rooms get. Your thermostat still decides that.
  • The catch is time, not comfort. Rooms take longer to come up to temperature, which matters most in a badly insulated house on the coldest mornings of the year.
  • It only works on a condensing combi. If you have a hot water cylinder, a system boiler, a heat pump or a back boiler, the rule is different and this article says so plainly.

1. What flow temperature actually is

There are two temperature settings on a combi boiler and almost everyone confuses them.

The one on the wall, the room thermostat, decides how warm your house gets. Set it to 20C and the heating runs until the room reaches 20C, then stops.

The other one is on the boiler itself, usually a dial or a digital setting marked with a radiator symbol. That is the flow temperature. It decides how hot the water is that the boiler pushes out to the radiators. It has nothing to do with how warm your rooms end up. It only decides how fast they get there, and how efficiently the boiler burns gas while doing it.

Most boilers are left at 70C or 80C by the installer, because that is the factory default and because a hot radiator is what a customer expects to feel on the day of the fitting. Almost nobody goes back and changes it.

2. Why 60C is the number, and not 55 or 65

Every boiler sold in the UK for the last twenty years is a condensing boiler. The condensing part is not marketing. It is a second heat exchanger that pulls extra heat out of the exhaust gases by cooling them until the water vapour in them condenses back into liquid. That recovered heat is free. You have already paid for the gas that produced it.

The problem is that the exhaust will only condense if the water coming back from your radiators is cool enough to cool it. Above roughly 55C of return temperature, the flue gases stay as vapour, go out of the wall, and take their heat with them. Your condensing boiler stops condensing and becomes an ordinary boiler with an expensive extra part doing nothing.

A condensing boiler set to 80C is not a condensing boiler. It is just a boiler with the efficient bit switched off by accident.

A flow temperature of 60C generally produces a return temperature in the low 40s or 50s, which is comfortably inside the condensing range. Going lower still, to 55C or 50C, recovers slightly more, but the returns diminish quickly and the warm-up time keeps getting longer. 60C is the setting that captures nearly all of the benefit while still heating a normal house at a normal speed, which is why it is the number the national campaign settled on.

One thing 60C does not change: your hot water. That is a separate setting on a combi, and you should leave it alone. Nothing in this article asks you to run domestic hot water cooler.

3. The arithmetic at October 2026 prices

Every figure below uses the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, direct debit, England, Scotland and Wales average. Gas is 7.97p per kWh with a standing charge of 29.68p a day, and that gas rate includes 5% VAT. The household is Ofgem's typical one, using 9,500 kWh of gas a year.

The saving applies to the gas you burn, not to the standing charge, because the standing charge is the same whether your boiler is efficient or not. So:

Gas units for the year. 9,500 kWh × 7.97p = £757.

9% of that. £757 × 0.09 = £68 a year.

Cost of making the change. £0.

Payback period. Immediate. There is nothing to pay back.

The table is the whole argument. The free change on the top row beats a £600 insulation top up by four to one, every year, forever, and you can undo it in ten seconds if you hate it. Nothing else in home energy has that shape.

It is worth being straight about where the 9% comes from. Nesta is a charity, not a manufacturer, and it has nothing to sell you here, which is more than can be said for most sources of energy saving percentages. Its figure came from a campaign that it estimates reached around 3.1 million households. That is a modelled average, not a measurement of your house, and your own number will differ depending on how badly your boiler was set up, how big your radiators are and how leaky your walls are. If your boiler was already at 65C the saving will be small. If it was at 80C it may well be more than 9%.

4. Who this does not work for

This is where most articles on the subject go quiet, so here it is in full.

If you have a hot water cylinder, do not drop the flow below 60C. Stored water needs to reach 60C periodically to keep Legionella bacteria down. On a system or regular boiler feeding a cylinder, the flow temperature has to stay high enough to get the tank there. 60C flow is the floor, not the target, and many installers will want 65C or more. This is a genuine health point and it is the one exception where saving gas is the wrong priority.

If you have a heat pump, this article does not apply. A heat pump already runs at a low flow temperature by design, usually 35C to 50C, and its controls handle the relationship between flow temperature and outside temperature automatically. Turning dials on a heat pump generally makes it worse, not better.

If you have undersized radiators, you will feel it. A radiator's output falls as the water in it gets cooler. A house fitted out around 80C flow with small radiators may struggle to reach temperature at 60C on the coldest week of the year. The symptom is not a cold house, it is a house that takes noticeably longer to warm up in the morning. If that happens, go to 65C rather than back to 80C and you keep most of the benefit.

If you rent, you can still do this. It is a setting, not an alteration. You are not modifying the property and you can put it back before you leave. It is one of the very few energy savings a tenant gets to keep the benefit of, along with the ones in our draught proofing payback guide.

5. Worked example: the Hussain house in Luton

A 1970s three bedroom semi in Luton, gas combi fitted in 2019 and never touched since. The flow dial was at 80C, which the installer left it on. Annual gas use from their last statement: 11,200 kWh, a bit above the Ofgem typical figure because the loft insulation is thin.

Gas units at the October 2026 cap. 11,200 kWh × 7.97p = £893 a year.

Saving at 9%. £893 × 0.09 = £80 a year.

What it cost them. Nothing. Two minutes with the boiler manual.

What changed in the house. The radiators run warm rather than too hot to touch. The living room takes about twenty minutes longer to get to 20C on a January morning, so they moved the timer forward half an hour. That half hour of extra running is already counted inside the 9%.

The useful comparison for the Hussains is this. They had been quoted £2,400 for a new boiler on the grounds that the old one was "inefficient". The old one was fine. It was set up badly. The dial did more for their bill in two minutes than the first two years of a new boiler would have.

Frequently asked

Will my house be colder? No. Your room thermostat still controls room temperature and it has not changed. The house reaches the same temperature, it just takes longer to get there. If your house is colder after the change, your thermostat has been knocked or your radiators are too small for 60C, not because 60C is too low in principle.

Does it affect my shower? No, as long as you only change the radiator setting. A combi has a separate hot water temperature control. Leave it where it is.

Which dial is it? On most boilers it is the one with a radiator symbol next to it, and the tap symbol is the hot water one. On digital boilers it is usually in a settings menu under "central heating temperature" or "CH flow". If you cannot tell which is which, the manufacturer's manual for your exact model number is the only reliable source, and every major manufacturer publishes them free as PDFs.

Is 9% guaranteed? No, and anyone promising you a guaranteed figure is guessing. It is a modelled average across millions of homes. Your saving depends on your starting setting, your radiators and your house. What is close to certain is that the saving is positive and the cost is zero, which is a rare combination.

The honest verdict

Do it today. This is the only recommendation on this site that carries no cost, no risk, no installer and no waiting.

Set the central heating flow temperature to 60C if you have a combi and no hot water cylinder. Leave the hot water setting alone. Leave your room thermostat alone. If the house takes too long to warm up in deep winter, move the timer earlier before you move the dial back, and if you still need to go up, go to 65C rather than 80C.

There is nothing to buy here. If a company contacts you offering to optimise your boiler settings for a fee, that is a two minute job you can do yourself with the manual, and paying for it would wipe out several months of the saving.
