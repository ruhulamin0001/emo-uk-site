A Will Kit and a Solicitor Pass the Same Legal Test, So What Is the Extra Money Actually For

GOV.UK sets out six conditions for a will to be legally valid, and a twenty pound kit from a stationer can satisfy every one of them. The price difference is not buying validity. It is buying drafting judgement, storage, and someone who carries professional liability if it goes wrong.

The short version

  • The legal test is six points, and it is short. Be 18 or over, make it voluntarily, be of sound mind, make it in writing, sign it in front of two witnesses both over 18, and have both of them sign in your presence.
  • A DIY will that meets those six points is as valid as any other. Price has nothing to do with it.
  • The witness trap is the one that costs real money. You cannot leave your witnesses, or their married partners, anything in your will.
  • Both witnesses must have a clear view of you signing, and you of them. They do not have to sign at the same time as each other.
  • What a solicitor sells is judgement and liability, not a better piece of paper. That matters most where there is property abroad, a business, a second family, a disabled beneficiary, or a likely challenge.
  • A will that is ambiguous is worse than no will, because the argument is paid for by the estate.

1. The six-point test, in full

GOV.UK lists what makes a will legally valid in England and Wales, and the list is short enough to print in full. You must:

  • be 18 or over
  • make it voluntarily
  • be of sound mind
  • make it in writing
  • sign it in the presence of 2 witnesses who are both over 18
  • have it signed by your 2 witnesses, in your presence

That is the whole test. There is nothing in it about a solicitor, nothing about a particular form of words, nothing about registration, and nothing about cost. A handwritten page that satisfies those six points is a valid will. A thousand pound document that fails one of them is not.

The signing detail is worth reading twice, because it is where home-made wills actually fail. GOV.UK is specific: you and your witnesses must sign the same document. When you sign, both witnesses must have a clear view of you and the act of signing. When they sign, you must have a clear view of them and the act of signing. They do not need to sign at the same time as each other. And if you make any changes later, the same signing and witnessing process has to happen again.

"A clear view" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. A witness who signs in the next room, or who signs a copy rather than the original, or who was in the room but not looking, is a problem that only surfaces after you are dead and cannot fix it.

2. The witness rule that voids gifts

One line on GOV.UK causes more expensive accidents than the rest of the page combined: "You cannot leave your witnesses (or their married partners) anything in your will."

Read that as an instruction about who to ask, not a footnote. The obvious people to grab as witnesses are the people in the house, which in practice means the family, which in practice means beneficiaries. If a beneficiary or a beneficiary's spouse witnesses the will, the will itself generally still stands. The gift to that person fails.

The consequence is that the person you most wanted to provide for can be the one person who gets nothing, because they were helpful on the day. No fee level protects you from this. A solicitor arranging the signing will simply not let it happen, and that is a genuine part of what you are paying for, but you can get the same protection for free by asking two neighbours instead.

The practical rule: witnesses should be people who are not in the will, not married to anyone in the will, over 18, and able to be found later if the will is questioned. Two colleagues or two neighbours are usually ideal.

3. What the solicitor's fee is actually buying

This site does not publish invented price tables, and will-writing is not a regulated fixed fee, so no specific figure is quoted here. What can be described accurately is the product.

Drafting judgement. A will kit gives you blanks. It does not tell you that "I leave my house to my children equally" creates a problem if one child lives in the house, or that naming a specific bank account fails if you switch banks, or that a gift to a named charity fails if that charity has merged. Drafting is the skill of writing instructions that still work under circumstances you have not thought of.

Structure for the awkward cases. Second marriages with children from a first, a beneficiary who receives means-tested benefits, a family business, property abroad, a beneficiary you expect to contest, or anyone under 18 inheriting. Each of these has a standard structural answer, usually involving a trust, and none of them fit in a kit.

Storage and findability. A will nobody can find is worth nothing. Firms store originals and you get a reference. If you write your own, tell your executor exactly where it is, and consider the National Will Register.

Liability. If a solicitor drafts a will negligently and a beneficiary loses out, the beneficiary has a route to recover. If you draft it and get it wrong, nobody is liable and the estate pays for the argument. This is the least advertised part of the fee and often the most valuable.

What the fee is not buying is legal force. That is worth stating plainly because it is sold the other way round.

4. Worked example: Tom in Kendal

Tom is 61, widowed, owns his house outright, has one adult daughter, a workplace pension and about £14,000 in savings. He wants everything to go to his daughter, and if she dies before him, to his sister.

Is this a kit case? On the facts, yes. There is one asset class of any size, one primary beneficiary, one substitute, no business, nobody under 18, no property abroad and no reason to expect a challenge. The six-point test is satisfiable at the kitchen table.

What he still has to get right, for free. Witnesses who are not his daughter, not his sister, and not married to either. Both watching him sign. Both signing while he watches. One document, no photocopies. And his executor told where the original lives.

What would flip him to a solicitor. Any one of these: his daughter receives means-tested benefits, so a direct gift could cut them off. He remarries, which in England and Wales revokes an existing will unless it was made in contemplation of that marriage. He buys a flat in Spain. He wants to leave his sister out and expects her to argue. Each of those turns a simple instruction into a structural problem, and structural problems are what the fee is for.

The number that actually matters in his case is not the will price at all. It is that dying without a valid will means the intestacy rules decide, and those rules do not know his daughter exists as a preference, they simply apply an order. For an estate of his size the whole exercise is worth doing on any budget.

5. How to choose, honestly

A kit is defensible if your estate is straightforward, everyone you want to benefit is an adult with no benefits exposure, you have no business and no foreign property, you expect no challenge, and you are confident about the witnessing.

Pay for drafting if any of the following is true: a second family, a beneficiary on means-tested benefits, a beneficiary under 18, a business or farm, property outside England and Wales, an estate near the Inheritance Tax thresholds, or a person you are deliberately excluding.

Either way, check the checker. Will writing is not a reserved legal activity in England and Wales, which means a firm calling itself a "will writer" is not necessarily regulated the way a solicitor is. If you want the liability protection described above, confirm you are instructing a regulated solicitor, or a member of a scheme with a complaints route and professional indemnity insurance, and get it in writing.

The honest verdict

The honest answer is that the legal document is the cheap part and almost everyone gets sold the opposite story. A twenty pound kit and a six hundred pound will can both be perfectly valid, and both can be perfectly useless.

If your affairs are genuinely simple, a kit plus disciplined witnessing is a reasonable choice and you should not feel talked out of it. If any of the structural flags in section five apply to you, a kit is not a saving, it is a deferred bill that your family pays with interest and stress.

And one thing applies at every price point: get the witnesses right. It is free, it takes one phone call to a neighbour, and it is the single most common way a will goes wrong.

Frequently asked

Does a will have to be typed? No. GOV.UK requires only that it is in writing. A handwritten will that meets the other five conditions is valid. Typed is easier to read and harder to dispute, which is a practical argument rather than a legal one.

Can my executor be a beneficiary? Yes. An executor can inherit under the will. The restriction is on witnesses, not executors. A beneficiary who acts as executor is entirely normal, and in small family estates it is the usual arrangement.

Does marriage cancel my will? In England and Wales, marriage or civil partnership generally revokes an existing will unless the will was expressly made in contemplation of that marriage. This is one of the most commonly missed points and it is a good reason to review a will whenever your relationship status changes.

Is a will made in England valid in Scotland? Scotland has a separate legal system with different rules, including legal rights for children and spouses that cannot simply be written out. If you are domiciled in Scotland or hold property there, take advice on that jurisdiction specifically rather than assuming an English will covers it.
