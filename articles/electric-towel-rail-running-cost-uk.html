An Electric Towel Rail Costs 7.9p An Hour

That is a small enough number that most people stop thinking about it, which is exactly where the money goes. The rail is not the problem. The switch is. And there is one month of the year where an electric rail is by far the cheapest way to heat anything in your house.

The short version

  • A 300 watt rail costs 7.90p an hour to run. A 500 watt rail costs 13.16p. Both at the Ofgem cap rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026.
  • On a timer for two hours a day, a whole heating season costs £33.16. That is the number people expect.
  • Left permanently on through the season, the same rail costs up to £397.96, or about £159 if it has a working thermostat cycling it at around 40 per cent. The gap between the best and worst case is a factor of twelve.
  • A timer and thermostat upgrade costs around £60 and saves around £331 a year against leaving it on. That is a payback of about nine weeks, the fastest on this page by a distance.
  • In summer the electric rail wins outright. Two hours of a 300 watt rail is 15.8p. Firing the gas boiler for the same two hours to warm one towel is about 53p, because you are heating the whole house to do it.

1. Finding your wattage, and what it costs an hour

Electric towel rails are sold by size and finish, and the number that actually matters is printed in small type. It is the element wattage, and on a domestic bathroom rail it is almost always between 150 and 500 watts.

As a concrete example, the Ecostrad Fina-E is a 300 watt rail measuring 500 by 1100mm, carrying an IP68 splashproof rating and a 5 year manufacturer's warranty. That is a fairly typical mid-range specification and 300 watts is the most common size fitted in a standard British bathroom.

If you are buying a dry element for an existing plumbed rail, the usual sizing rule is the rail's water volume in litres, multiplied by 30. A 10 litre rail takes a 300 watt element. Undersize it and it never gets properly hot; oversize it and you have simply bought a more expensive way to reach the same thermostat setting.

The cost per hour is the wattage divided by a thousand, multiplied by the unit rate. Ofgem's price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 puts electricity at 26.32p per kWh, and there is no VAT on electricity across that period and on to 31 March 2027.

  • 150 W: 3.95p an hour
  • 300 W: 7.90p an hour
  • 500 W: 13.16p an hour
  • 1,000 W, a large designer rail: 26.32p an hour

Seven and a half pence. That is the whole reason this appliance quietly costs people hundreds of pounds: at that price nobody bothers to turn it off.

2. Four ways people run them, and what each costs a year

All figures below are for a 300 watt rail. The heating season is taken as 210 days, roughly October to April.

One honest distinction first. A rail with a working thermostat does not draw its full wattage continuously. It heats up, reaches its set temperature and then cycles, so a 300 watt rail left switched on might average something closer to 120 watts. A rail with no thermostat, or one with the dial wound to maximum in a cool bathroom, draws close to its full rating. Both cases are shown, because both exist in real houses.

And here is the payback calculation that follows from it. A thermostatic element with a built-in timer, or a plug-in timer plus a thermostatic element, typically costs around £60 more than a plain on-off element.

£60 divided by £331.63 saved a year is 0.18 years, which is about nine weeks. Even measured against the gentler case of a rail that already has a thermostat and cycles at 40 per cent, the timer saves £92.85 a year and pays back in eight months. There is no version of this sum where the timer is not worth buying.

3. The summer case, where electric beats gas by three to one

The standard rule of thumb on this site is that electricity costs about 3.3 times as much as gas per unit, so gas heating wins. On this one appliance, in this one season, the rule reverses, and it is worth understanding why.

In July you do not want the house heated. You want one towel dry. If your towel rail is plumbed into the central heating, warming it means firing the boiler and circulating hot water, which in most systems means heating a good deal more than the bathroom.

Do the arithmetic. A three bedroom semi loses roughly 200 watts per degree. Even on a mild summer morning, running the heating circuit for two hours puts something like 6 kWh of heat into the house that nobody asked for. At 90 per cent boiler efficiency that is 6.67 kWh of gas, and at 7.97p per kWh it costs 53.1p.

Two hours of a 300 watt electric rail is 0.6 kWh at 26.32p: 15.8p.

The electric rail is 3.4 times cheaper, because it heats 300 watts instead of 3,000. Expensive fuel, tiny load, and the tiny load wins easily. This is the same logic that makes an electric blanket cheaper than heating a bedroom, and it is one of the few genuinely reliable rules in household energy: heat the thing, not the room.

It is also why an electric heater for one room can beat central heating despite the unit rate, provided you really are only heating one room and not leaving it running everywhere.

4. Dual fuel, and why it is usually the right answer

A dual fuel towel rail is plumbed into the central heating in the normal way, but also has an electric element fitted into the bottom. In winter it runs off the boiler along with every other radiator, costing you effectively nothing extra because the heating is on anyway. In summer you switch off the plumbed side and run the element.

That combination gets you the cheap fuel when the heating is on and the small load when it is not, which is the correct answer in both seasons. The extra cost over a plumbed-only rail is the element and the plumbing to accept it, typically £80 to £150 all in.

Set against the £53.1p a day it costs to fire the boiler for towels across a 150 day summer, that is £79.65 of boiler firing replaced by £23.70 of element, a saving of £55.95 a summer. On a £120 conversion that is a payback of 2.1 years, which is respectable rather than spectacular, and it is a real saving rather than a claimed one.

Two practical points. First, an element fitted to a plumbed rail needs the rail to be left full of water and the valves handled correctly, so read the specific instructions rather than assuming. Second, electrical work in a bathroom is notifiable in England and Wales, so a dual fuel conversion is an electrician's job, not a Sunday one.

5. Worked example: Marta in Taunton

Marta has a 500 watt chrome ladder rail in a family bathroom, wired to a fused spur with a simple on-off switch. It has been on continuously since she moved in three years ago, because that is how the previous owner left it and nobody thought about it.

She works out the running cost. 0.5 kW, on permanently, is 4,380 kWh a year at 26.32p: £1,152.82. She does not believe it, and she is right not to, because the element has a basic bimetallic thermostat that cycles it. She buys a £12 plug-in energy monitor, fits it for a fortnight, and finds the rail is averaging about 190 watts, so 38 per cent duty.

The real figure is 190 W × 8,760 hours = 1,664 kWh, or £437.96 a year. Still a great deal more than she assumed, and more than her fridge, freezer and washing machine combined.

She fits a £58 thermostatic timer element and sets it for 90 minutes before the morning showers and 90 minutes in the evening, three hours a day, through the season only.

New cost: 0.5 kW × 3 hours × 210 days = 315 kWh = £82.91 a year.

Saving: £355.05 a year. £58 divided by £355.05 is 0.163 years, which is eight and a half weeks.

The towels are, she reports, exactly as dry as they were before. The only thing that changed is that the rail is now off for twenty-one hours a day instead of warming an empty bathroom at two in the morning.

6. The honest verdict

Buy the towel rail. Then immediately buy the timer, because the timer is the product. At £60 against a saving of up to £331 a year it pays back in about nine weeks, and it is the difference between a £33 appliance and a £398 one.

If you are replacing a plumbed rail, specify dual fuel. Cheap fuel in winter, small load in summer, about a two year payback on the conversion, and no compromise in either season.

If your rail has no thermostat, replace the element before you replace the rail. An element is a fraction of the price and it is where all the cost is.

And if you have simply never thought about the one in your bathroom, spend £12 on a plug-in energy monitor and find out. On this appliance more than almost any other, the assumption and the meter reading tend to be a long way apart.

If you are buying

The two numbers worth comparing are the element wattage and the warranty length, because the finish will look the same in a year and the running cost will not. Ecostrad's Fina-E carries five years, which is a useful benchmark to hold other rails against. Anything with a thermostat and a seven day timer is worth the premium; anything with a plain on-off switch is not.

Frequently asked

Is it cheaper to leave it on low all day than to blast it twice? No. This is the same myth as leaving the central heating on low all winter. A heated object loses heat to the room continuously, so keeping it warm for twenty-four hours always uses more energy than warming it for three. The only situation where continuous running wins is where you are avoiding a genuine damp problem, and even then a timer set to run in blocks does the same job.

Will a towel rail heat the bathroom? Barely. 300 watts is about a fifth of what a small bathroom radiator puts out, and a bathroom loses heat quickly because of its ventilation. Treat a towel rail as a towel warmer that takes the edge off, not as the room's heat source, and size your actual heating separately.

Does chrome cost more to run than white? Not to run, but chrome rails have a lower heat output for the same size because of the finish, so manufacturers often fit a larger element to compensate. Check the wattage, not the colour.
