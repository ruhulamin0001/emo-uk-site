Is Taking In a Lodger Worth It? The £7,500 Promise Has Quietly Stopped Covering an Average Room

Every guide tells you that you can earn £7,500 a year tax free from a lodger. Almost none of them mention that the figure has been frozen since April 2016, or that the average UK room now rents for more than that. Here is the arithmetic that decides whether a lodger is genuinely worth it for you.

The short version

  • Rent a Room relief is £7,500 a year, and it has been frozen at that level since 6 April 2016. Before then it was £4,250. It has not moved in more than ten years.
  • The average UK room rent was £761 a month in Q2 2026, according to the SpareRoom rental index. That is £9,132 a year, which is £1,632 above the relief.

  • If you own the home jointly, the relief halves to £3,750 each. Couples are far more likely to breach it than a single owner.
  • Lodger income is ignored for Universal Credit. That is the one part of this that is genuinely better than most people expect.

1. What Rent a Room relief actually is

Rent a Room relief lets you take up to £7,500 a year from letting furnished accommodation in your only or main home without paying income tax on it. For the 2026/27 tax year, which runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, that figure is unchanged.

Three details do most of the damage when people get this wrong.

It is gross receipts, not profit. HMRC counts everything the lodger hands over. Rent, their share of the gas and electric, a charge for meals or laundry. Charge £600 rent plus £80 towards bills and that is £680 a month towards the threshold, not £600.

It halves if the income is shared. If two people receive it, typically a couple who jointly own the house, the threshold is £3,750 each. Not £7,500 each, and not £7,500 between you in the way people assume.

It applies per household, not per room. There is no limit on how many rooms you let, but the £7,500 covers all of them combined. Two lodgers at £450 a month each puts you at £10,800 and well over the line.

Below the threshold, the relief is automatic. Nothing to tell HMRC, no return to file because of it, unless you are already in Self Assessment for another reason. That simplicity is the best thing about the scheme.

The allowance has not risen since April 2016. Room rents have. That single sentence explains why the advice you read five years ago no longer describes what happens to your money.

2. Why the average room has overtaken the allowance

This is the part almost every guide still gets wrong, because it repeats a line that was true in 2016 and has quietly stopped being true.

According to the SpareRoom rental index, the average UK room rent reached £761 a month in Q2 2026, up 0.5% on the year. In Q1 2026 it was £747. Six of the UK's nine regions hit record highs in Q2 2026.

So the honest position in 2026 is this. If you charge the average UK room rent, you are not in the tax free scheme. You are in Self Assessment, you have a notification deadline, and you have a decision to make about how the excess is taxed.

The break-even is £625 a month for a sole owner, and only £312.50 a month each for a jointly owning couple. Below those numbers the scheme does exactly what it says. Above them, it becomes a partial relief rather than an exemption, and you should stop calling it tax free in your head.

3. The costs the headline leaves out

Tax is not the only thing that eats the number. Four things do, and only one of them is usually mentioned.

The single person council tax discount. If you currently live alone, you get 25% off your council tax bill. A lodger is a second adult in the property, and in almost all cases that discount goes. The average Band D bill in England for 2026/27 is £2,392, up 4.9% on the year, so the discount is worth roughly £598 a year. That is a real, immediate cost that appears on your next bill and never appears in the "£7,500 tax free" headline. If you already live with another adult, you were not getting the discount anyway and this cost is nil.

Running costs. An extra adult uses hot water, heating, electricity and, on a meter, water. Between £40 and £60 a month is a fair range for one person in a typical home, more if they work from home. And if you charge them a contribution towards those bills, it counts towards your £7,500.

Permission and insurance. Most residential mortgages require you to notify the lender, and most home insurance policies require you to tell the insurer. Neither usually costs much, but not telling them can invalidate cover at the worst possible moment. If you rent, you almost certainly need the landlord's written permission, and many tenancy agreements prohibit it outright.

Setting the room up. The accommodation must be furnished for the relief to apply. Working smoke alarms are a legal requirement in rented residential accommodation in England, and a carbon monoxide alarm is required in any room with a fixed combustion appliance.

4. Going over £7,500: Method A or Method B

Once your gross receipts pass £7,500, you must register for Self Assessment if you are not already in it. The deadline to notify HMRC is 5 October after the end of the tax year in which you first went over. So if you cross the line during 2026/27, you must tell HMRC by 5 October 2027.

You then choose between two ways of being taxed.

Method A is the ordinary property income calculation. You ignore Rent a Room relief entirely and pay tax on your actual profit, which is gross receipts minus a fair proportion of allowable expenses. HMRC applies this by default unless you elect otherwise.

Method B is the Rent a Room election. You pay tax only on the amount by which your gross receipts exceed £7,500, and you deduct no expenses at all.

For a normal lodger arrangement Method B usually wins by a wide margin, because £7,500 is far larger than any honest apportionment of your bills. Method A only makes sense in unusual years, for example one with major repair costs attributable to the let part of the house.

Worked example: Nadia in Luton

Nadia owns her three bedroom house outright in her own name and lives alone. Her council tax is at the England Band D average of £2,392, and with the 25% single person discount she currently pays £1,794. She lets her spare room at £700 a month, slightly under the UK average.

Gross receipts: £700 × 12 = £8,400. That is £900 over the threshold, so she is in Self Assessment.

Tax, using Method B: £8,400 − £7,500 = £900 taxable. Nadia is a basic rate taxpayer, so 20% of £900 = £180.

Council tax discount lost: 25% of £2,392 = £598.

Extra running costs: she budgets £45 a month for the extra person = £540.

What she actually keeps: £8,400 − £180 − £598 − £540 = £7,082 a year, or £590 a month.

That is still a very good outcome, and better than nearly any side hustle for the hours involved. But it is £1,318 less than the £8,400 headline, and it is below the £7,500 that the adverts imply she will keep tax free. Anyone budgeting on the headline is planning with a number that is 16% too high.

If Nadia had used Method A instead, with roughly £1,200 of apportioned expenses, she would be taxed on £7,200 of profit, a tax bill near £1,440. The election matters.

5. Who this genuinely suits, and who should not bother

It works well if you own your home, live alone or with one other adult, have a genuinely spare furnished room, and you are comfortable sharing a kitchen and bathroom. At £590 a month net, as in Nadia's case, it beats almost every side hustle on this site for money per hour, because after the first fortnight the effort is close to zero.

It also has a quietly useful feature for people on benefits. Lodger income is ignored for Universal Credit. It does not reduce your award, which makes a lodger one of the few ways to raise household income without the taper clawing most of it back. Council Tax Reduction is separate and decided by your local council, so check with them before committing.

It does not work if you rent and your tenancy forbids subletting, if the "spare" room is where your work or your children's things live, or if what you keep is too small to be worth the loss of privacy. Charging £350 a month in a low rent area, after £598 of council tax and £540 of running costs, leaves about £3,062 a year. Real money, but a very different proposition from £7,000.

One worry you can usually drop. A lodger who shares your living accommodation does not normally restrict Private Residence Relief when you sell, so your home stays free of capital gains tax. That changes if you let a self contained part of the property, or take several lodgers, so get advice before going beyond one.

A lodger is also not a tenant. Someone who shares your kitchen or bathroom is generally an excluded occupier, which means far simpler notice arrangements. Put the terms in a written lodger agreement anyway. Rent, notice, bills, guests, cleaning. The agreement is not there for the good months.

Frequently asked

Do I have to tell HMRC if I earn under £7,500? No. The relief is automatic below the threshold and there is nothing to report, unless you already complete a Self Assessment return for another reason, in which case you note it there.

Does the £7,500 include what my lodger pays towards bills? Yes. Gross receipts means everything they pay you, including contributions to gas, electricity, water and broadband, and any charge for meals or laundry. This is the single most common way people accidentally cross the line.

My partner and I own the house jointly. Do we get £15,000? No. Where the income is shared, the threshold is £3,750 each. That is £312.50 a month before you are into Self Assessment, which is below almost any room rent in the UK.

Will a lodger affect my Universal Credit? Lodger income is ignored for Universal Credit purposes. Council Tax Reduction is run separately by your local council and the rules vary, so confirm your own council's position before you take anyone in.

The honest verdict

Taking in a lodger is still one of the highest returning things a UK homeowner with a spare room can do. Nadia keeping £7,082 a year for what amounts to a few hours of admin is not a figure any normal side hustle gets near.

But stop treating £7,500 as the number in your head. It has been frozen since April 2016 while the average UK room went to £761 a month, and the two lines crossed some time ago. At an average rent you are over the threshold, in Self Assessment, and choosing between Method A and Method B. Add the £598 council tax discount you lose if you live alone, and the gap between the headline and the reality is well over £1,000 a year.

Do the sum on your own room rent, your own council tax band, and your own household. If the answer is still comfortably four figures, it is worth it. If you are letting a small room in a cheap area for £350 a month, look at that net figure honestly before you give up your spare room and your privacy for it.
