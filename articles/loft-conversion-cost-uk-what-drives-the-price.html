The Quote Is Not About the Loft. It Is About the Stairs

Two builders can look at the same roof and come back 25,000 pounds apart. The gap is almost never the insulation or the windows. It is where the staircase can physically go, and how much of the floor below it eats.

The short version

  • Four types, four price bands. A rooflight conversion runs about 22,000 to 30,000 pounds fitted. A rear dormer is 38,000 to 55,000. Hip to gable with a dormer is 50,000 to 70,000. A mansard is 60,000 to 90,000.

1. Why the stairs decide the price

Ask three companies to quote for the same loft and you will get three numbers that look like they are for three different houses. People assume the difference is the quality of the insulation, or the brand of window, or how much the firm thinks it can get away with. It is usually none of those.

A loft conversion needs a permanent staircase. A loft ladder does not count and never has. That staircase has to land somewhere on the floor below, it has to satisfy Approved Document K on pitch, going and headroom, and on a two storey house becoming a three storey house it becomes part of a protected escape route, which pulls in fire doors and mains wired alarms on the floors below.

If your house has a generous landing, the stair can rise over the existing one and you lose almost nothing. If it does not, the stair comes out of a bedroom. You have then spent 45,000 pounds converting a three bedroom house into a three bedroom house with a very nice attic. The valuation follows the bedroom count, not the square metres, and the money does not come back.

This is the first question to ask any surveyor, and it should be asked before the word dormer is used. Where does the stair go, and what does it cost me downstairs.

2. The four types, and what each one actually buys

A rooflight conversion, often called a Velux conversion after the manufacturer, changes nothing about the shape of the roof. Floor is strengthened, insulation goes in, windows go into the existing slope. It is the cheapest by a wide margin and the fastest on site. It only works if you already have the head height, which in practice means a post war house with a reasonably steep roof or an older property with a tall attic.

A rear dormer boxes out the back roof slope to create a flat ceiling and full height across most of the room. This is the standard suburban conversion and the one most people picture. It costs more because it is structural work at height with scaffolding, steels and a new flat roof.

Hip to gable applies to a semi or an end terrace with a sloping side roof. The hip is built up into a vertical gable wall, which adds volume before the dormer even starts. It is the most transformative and the most disruptive.

A mansard rebuilds the whole roof at a near vertical pitch. It gives the most space and almost always needs full planning permission, because it visibly changes the roof from the street. In a conservation area it is often refused outright.

4. Permitted development, and the rules that bite

The Planning Portal states the allowance plainly: a loft extension is permitted development up to 40 cubic metres for a terraced house, or 50 cubic metres for anything else, under Schedule 2, Part 1, Class B of the General Permitted Development Order. That is added volume, not room volume, and any previous roof extension counts against it. A house that had a small dormer done in 1998 may have very little left.

Three more conditions catch people out. Materials must be similar in appearance to the existing house. On the principal elevation, the side that faces the highway, nothing may extend beyond the existing roof slope, which is why front dormers almost always need an application. And a balcony is not permitted development, though a Juliet balcony with no platform normally is.

Permitted development is not permission to skip building control. It is a separate system. You will still need a building regulations application, and you will still need the fire strategy, the insulation standard and the stair to be signed off. If you ever sell, the buyer's solicitor will ask for the completion certificate, and a conversion without one is a price reduction waiting to happen.

5. Worked example: the Okonkwo family in Rugby

Ada and Chidi Okonkwo have a three bedroom 1960s semi in Rugby, worth about 340,000 pounds. Their third child is two. They want a fourth bedroom with a shower room and they have two options.

Option one, move. A four bedroom house in the same catchment is 400,000 pounds. The stamp duty on that, using the GOV.UK residential rates, is nothing on the first 125,000, two per cent on the next 125,000 which is 2,500 pounds, and five per cent on the remaining 150,000 which is 7,500 pounds. Total stamp duty 10,000 pounds. Estate agent at 1.2 per cent plus VAT on a 340,000 pound sale is 4,896 pounds. Conveyancing on both the sale and the purchase, with searches and Land Registry fees, is about 2,800. Removals 1,400. A level two survey 650. An EPC 70. The transaction costs come to 19,816 pounds, and on top of that they have to find the 60,000 pound difference between the two houses. Moving costs them 79,800 pounds, rounded.

Option two, convert. Their landing runs front to back, so the stair rises over the existing flight and costs them no bedroom space. A rear dormer with an ensuite is quoted at 48,000 pounds including building control fees and decoration. It adds about 20 usable square metres, which is 2,400 pounds a square metre.

Converting is 31,800 pounds cheaper than moving, and that is before anyone values the house again. It is not a payback in the usual sense, because nobody gets a cheque. It is 31,800 pounds of spending avoided to arrive at the same bedroom, which is the only comparison that matters here.

Two things would flip it. If the stair had to eat a bedroom, the conversion buys nothing and they should move. And if they expect to leave within three years, the conversion will not have been absorbed into the local price ceiling, and they will get back less than they put in.

Frequently asked

Will a loft conversion add more to my house than it costs? Sometimes, and it depends far more on the street than on the work. Every road has a price ceiling that the best house on it cannot break through. If your three bedroom semi is already close to what the four bedroom houses sell for, the conversion will not clear its own cost. Look at what four bedroom versions of your exact house actually sold for on the Land Registry, not at what agents are asking.

Do I need my neighbour's agreement? If you are building on or into the party wall, which hip to gable and most dormers on a semi or terrace do, the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies and you must serve notice. This is separate from planning and separate from building control, and skipping it is the mistake that stops work on site.

Is it cheaper to do it at the same time as other work? Yes, meaningfully. Scaffolding is a fixed cost that does not care how many jobs it serves. If the roof, the guttering or the render need doing in the next five years, doing them off the same scaffold is the cheapest they will ever be.

What about a garage conversion instead? Usually cheaper per square metre and far less disruptive, though it rarely produces a bedroom that valuers count the same way. It is a genuine alternative rather than a consolation prize.

The honest verdict

If your landing can take the staircase without losing a bedroom, and you intend to stay more than five years, converting is the cheaper way to get the room by a wide margin, and a rear dormer at roughly 2,350 pounds a usable square metre is the sweet spot for most suburban houses.

If the stair has to come out of a bedroom, do not do it. You will spend 40,000 pounds or more to end up with the same number of bedrooms you started with, and the valuation will tell you so.
