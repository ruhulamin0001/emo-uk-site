The Expensive Part Of Asbestos Is Not The Removal. It Is The Fourteen Days You Cannot Work

Most people budget for asbestos as a removal bill. The removal is rarely the worst of it. The Control of Asbestos Regulations require fourteen days notice before licensed work begins, and a builder standing still for a fortnight costs more than the skip.

The short version

  • All licensable asbestos work must be notified to the enforcing authority at least 14 days before it starts, on form ASB5. In domestic premises the enforcing authority is the HSE.
  • Licensed work is the higher risk material: sprayed coatings, pipe lagging, loose fill insulation, asbestos millboard, and asbestos insulating board where the job is not short duration.
  • Short duration has a precise legal meaning: no more than 2 hours in any 7 day period, with no one person working more than 1 hour inside that 2 hours.
  • Asbestos cement, the corrugated garage roof, is usually non licensed work. It still has to be done under the regulations, but it does not carry the 14 day wait.

1. The bill people budget for is the wrong bill

Ask anyone what asbestos costs and they will give you a removal price. Removal is a line on the invoice, and on a domestic job it is often a smaller line than people fear. The cost that actually hurts is time, and it is written into the regulations rather than into anybody's quote.

The Health and Safety Executive is clear that all licensable work with asbestos must be notified to the appropriate enforcing authority using form ASB5 at least 14 days before the work starts. For domestic premises the enforcing authority is the HSE itself. There is no fast track for a family living in a building site.

So the sequence that costs money runs like this. Trades start. On day three somebody opens a boxed in soffit or lifts an airing cupboard panel and finds asbestos insulating board. Work stops. A licensed contractor is found, notifies, and waits fourteen days. Your kitchen fitter, who was booked for that fortnight, is now somewhere else until November.

2. What is licensable and what is not

HSE defines licensable work by risk, not by material name. In practice the tests are these: exposure that is not sporadic and of low intensity, or where the risk assessment cannot demonstrate the control limit will be met, plus work on asbestos coating, and work on asbestos insulation or insulating board that is not short duration.

Short duration has a legal definition and it is tighter than people assume. HSE states it as work taking no more than 2 hours in any 7 day period, with no person working more than 1 hour within that 2 hour period. That is one hour of one person's work, not a morning.

HSE's own examples of licensable work are sprayed coatings, sometimes called limpet asbestos, removal or disturbance of pipe lagging, any work involving loose fill insulation, work on asbestos millboard, cleaning up significant quantities of loose debris containing asbestos dust, and work on asbestos insulating board where the risk assessment says it will not be short duration.

It is an offence to carry out licensable work with asbestos without a licence. That is HSE's wording, not a caution added by a contractor trying to sell you something.

Non licensed work is everything else, and it still falls under the Control of Asbestos Regulations. Some of it is notifiable non licensed work, which carries its own record keeping duties but not the fourteen day wait. Asbestos cement products, the corrugated garage roof and the old water tank, are the common domestic examples.

4. Worked example: the Whitfield household in Kettering

A 1968 semi, kitchen and utility being refitted. Fitter booked for three weeks from 5 October, plumber and electrician slotted around him.

Without a survey. Day two, the boxing around the old boiler flue comes off and turns out to be insulating board. Everything stops. Licensed contractor notifies HSE, earliest start 14 days later. Removal itself is 1,350 pounds and takes a day. The fitter cannot hold three weeks open, so the job restarts in mid November. Direct cost 1,350 pounds, plus roughly 14 days of stalled programme at about 250 pounds a day of trade time and rebooking, call it 3,500 pounds.

With a survey. A refurbishment and demolition survey of the two rooms, 320 pounds, carried out four weeks before work starts. Board identified. Notification runs during the design period and removal happens the week before the fitter arrives. Cost 320 pounds plus the same 1,350 pounds removal, and nothing stops.

Payback on the 320 pound survey: it breaks even if it saves 1.3 days. It saved fourteen.

That ratio is why every builder who has been caught once now insists on a survey before opening anything in a pre 2000 house. The removal price was identical in both scenarios. The survey did not make the asbestos cheaper. It made the calendar work.

5. Which survey you actually need

There are two, and buying the wrong one is a common and avoidable waste.

A management survey is the light touch one. It identifies asbestos that could be disturbed during normal occupation and use, and it does not go into the fabric. It is the right survey for a landlord managing a building, and the wrong survey before a refit, because the board inside your boxed in flue is exactly the thing it will not look at.

A refurbishment and demolition survey is intrusive. It opens up the areas due to be worked on and is destructive by design. It is the one you need before a kitchen, a loft conversion, a rewire or anything structural. Typical domestic cost runs roughly 250 to 450 pounds for a targeted survey of specific rooms, more for a whole house.

If your house was built or refurbished before 2000, assume asbestos is possible and scope the survey to the rooms being touched. If it was built after 2000, the ban on all asbestos containing materials in the UK was complete by the end of 1999, and a survey is generally unnecessary.

Damp and asbestos surveys often get bundled together by the same firm, which can be efficient, though the two reports answer very different questions. We looked at what the other one actually tells you in what a damp survey costs and what it misses.

Frequently asked

Can I remove asbestos cement from my own garage roof? Legally a householder may work on their own home, and asbestos cement is non licensed work. That does not make it sensible. The material must not be broken or power cut, it needs double bagging and a licensed tip, and many councils will not take it at all or charge per sheet. Get a price from a contractor before deciding, because the disposal is the awkward part rather than the labour.

Is textured ceiling coating dangerous to live with? Undisturbed and in good condition, the HSE position on asbestos containing materials generally is that they are safest left alone and managed. Sanding it, drilling it or scraping it is what creates risk. If you are not touching it, the cheapest correct action is usually nothing.

Who pays if asbestos is found in a rented home? The landlord. The duty to manage asbestos sits with whoever is responsible for maintenance of the premises, and in a tenancy that is the landlord, not the tenant.

Does finding asbestos have to be declared when selling? There is no standalone asbestos declaration, but if you hold a survey or a removal record, questions on the property information form about alterations and known defects will usually catch it, and concealing a known report is the kind of thing that unravels expensively after completion.

The honest verdict

If your house predates 2000 and you are about to open up walls, ceilings, floors or a boxed in flue, buy the refurbishment survey before you book the trades. At roughly 320 pounds it pays for itself the moment it saves more than about a day and a half of stalled work, and a licensed find costs fourteen.

If you are not disturbing anything, do not go looking. Asbestos in good condition and left alone is managed, not an emergency, and a survey you commission out of anxiety buys you a document rather than a saving.

Never let a general builder remove insulating board because they say they have done it before. It is an offence to do licensable work without a licence, and your insurer will take a close interest in who did it. The reason the licensing rules are this strict is what the dust does thirty years later, and there is a separate payment route for families already living with that: the dust disease scheme open to dependants until 31 October 2026.
