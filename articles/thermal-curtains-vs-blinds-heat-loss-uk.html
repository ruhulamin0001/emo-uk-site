Thermal Curtains Will Not Pay For Themselves. Buy Them Anyway, But Only The Lined Ones, And Only If You Need Curtains.

Every retailer selling thermal curtains claims they cut heat loss, and none of them publishes a saving in pounds that anyone has checked. So we did what a money site should do: we found the largest saving the window itself can possibly deliver, from the Energy Saving Trust, and used it as a ceiling. The result is that curtains, blinds and film are comfort purchases with a small energy bonus, and the only way to buy them well is to know that going in.

The short version

1. Why the saving has a ceiling, and what it is

Heat leaves a room through its windows, and anything you hang over a window can only reduce the share that leaves that way. That sounds obvious, but it is the whole argument, because it means there is a maximum saving for window dressing, and a public body has already published a figure close to it.

The Energy Saving Trust's windows page, last updated 20 May 2026 and read again on 4 September 2026, says that installing A-rated double glazing in an entirely single-glazed semi-detached house saves £85 a year in Great Britain. That is the saving from the best practical window upgrade there is, working 24 hours a day, on every window in the house, at July 2026 fuel prices. It is the ceiling.

Curtains are worse than double glazing on three counts. They are not sealed, so warm air circulates behind them and reaches the glass, particularly where they hang in front of a radiator. They only work when they are closed, which for most rooms is the evening and night, perhaps 14 of the 24 hours in midwinter and fewer in the shoulder months. And they are usually only fitted in living rooms and bedrooms, not the kitchen, bathroom or landing. Put those three together and a realistic figure for a fully single-glazed semi is somewhere between a third and a half of the ceiling: £30 to £50 a year.

Most UK homes are already double glazed. For them, the ceiling drops sharply: the window is already losing far less heat, so there is far less for the curtain to catch. We would put a double-glazed semi at £10 to £20 a year for a full set of well-fitted lined curtains. These are our estimates, bounded by the Energy Saving Trust's figure, and we would rather give you a defensible range than a retailer's unaudited percentage.

If you want the per-window physics, a square metre of single glazing loses about five times as much heat per degree of temperature difference as a square metre of modern A-rated double glazing; that ratio is why the whole-house figure is what it is, and why the same curtain does five times as much on a single-glazed window as on a new double-glazed one.

Two things stand out. The cheapest option, shrink-fit film, is the only one whose payback is short, because it costs almost nothing and it is sealed to the frame, which is exactly what a curtain is not. It is also the option the Energy Saving Trust describes as short-lived and hard to make look acceptable, and it comes off in spring. The most expensive option, new windows, is the one with the longest payback of all, which is the subject of our separate double glazing article.

Blinds do less than curtains unless they are the cellular type, because a roller blind hangs flat inside the reveal with air gaps at every edge. Cellular blinds trap air in their honeycomb pockets and are the only blind marketed on insulation with any physical basis; even so, the manufacturers' claims are their own, and we have not found an independent UK test to cite.

3. Payback for a houseful, and where it is never

Take a three-bed semi with eight windows that would be curtained: two living room, one dining, three bedroom, two landing and hall. All thermal-lined, at £40 to £150 a window, is £320 to £1,200.

Only one cell in that table is a payback inside the life of the curtains, and it requires single glazing and the cheapest curtains. For everyone else, the energy saving is real but it is not the reason to buy, and a retailer telling you a thermal curtain will "slash your bills" is selling you fabric on a claim it cannot support.

The right comparison is the premium for the thermal lining, not the whole curtain. If you are buying curtains regardless, the lined version of the same curtain typically costs £10 to £30 more per window. At £4 to £6 a year on a single-glazed window, that premium pays back in two to seven years, and on a double-glazed window in five to fifteen. That is a sensible purchase. The whole curtain, bought for energy, is not.

4. The part the arithmetic misses: comfort and the thermostat

There is one way a curtain can save more than the arithmetic above allows, and it is indirect. A bare single-glazed window at night has a cold inner surface, and the air that falls off it is what makes a room feel draughty even when the thermostat says 21. Cover it and the room feels warmer at the same temperature. The Energy Saving Trust's draught proofing page puts turning the thermostat down by one degree, from 22 to 21, at £120 a year in Great Britain. If a lined curtain lets you do that in the room you sit in, the saving is far larger than the curtain's direct effect, and it is the only honest route to a big number.

Three practical rules that decide whether you get any of it. Close them at dusk, not at bedtime, because the hours between are the coldest ones you are heating. Do not drape them over a radiator; a curtain in front of a radiator sends its heat up behind the fabric and out through the glass, and a shorter curtain or a shelf above the radiator fixes it. And fit them close to the wall with a pelmet or a track that returns to the wall at the ends, because the gaps at the top and sides are where the circulation happens.

For a rented home, curtains and shrink-fit film are the two options that need no permission and leave with you. Blinds need screw holes; magnetic secondary glazing needs a fixed frame strip that most landlords will accept but some will not.

5. Worked example: Aoife in Carlisle

Two-bed Victorian terrace, single-glazed sash windows at the front, uPVC double glazing at the back, gas central heating

Aoife's living room has one large single-glazed sash window with an original wooden frame. The bedrooms are double glazed. She wants to know whether to spend on thermal curtains for the whole house or just the front.

The living room window. Single glazed, room heated every evening. Realistic saving from a lined curtain, drawn at dusk: £5 to £8 a year, at the top of our per-window range because the room is heated and the window is large. A lined curtain pair for the bay at about £120 pays back in 15 to 24 years on energy alone. The lining premium over the unlined pair she was going to buy anyway, about £25, pays back in three to five years.

The bedrooms. Double glazed, heated lightly. Realistic saving from lined curtains: £1 to £2 a year each. Not worth a penny of premium for the energy; she buys whichever she likes the look of.

The sash window itself. The gaps around a sash are a draught, not a glazing loss, and draught-proofing strips for one sash window cost £15 to £30 in materials. The Energy Saving Trust's whole-house draught proofing figure is £55 a year; a single leaky sash in the main room is a meaningful share of that. This is the better spend, and it is the one she had not thought of.

Decision. Lined curtains for the front room, standard curtains upstairs, brush strips on the sash, and a rolled-up towel across the sill until the strips arrive. Total extra spend on energy grounds: under £60.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy thermal curtains to save money. The most any window covering can save is bounded by what replacing the window would save, which the Energy Saving Trust puts at £85 a year for a whole single-glazed semi, and curtains get a fraction of that. On a double-glazed house the saving is £10 to £20 a year for the lot, and the payback on a houseful runs past the life of the fabric.

Do buy the lined version of curtains you were buying anyway, especially for a single-glazed room you heat every evening, because the lining premium pays back and the comfort is real. Then put the money you were going to spend on the rest of the house into draught proofing, which pays back in four to five years and is the sum that actually works.

Frequently asked

Do thermal curtains really save money on heating? A little. The most any window covering can save is limited by what the window loses; the Energy Saving Trust puts replacing all single glazing in a semi with A-rated double glazing at £85 a year, and curtains that only work when drawn get a fraction of that, roughly £30 to 50 a year for a fully single-glazed semi and £10 to 20 for a double-glazed one.

Are thermal blinds or thermal curtains better? Lined curtains that reach the sill and return to the wall do more than a roller blind, because a blind hangs flat with air gaps at every edge. Cellular honeycomb blinds are the exception and are broadly comparable to a lined curtain, but their insulation claims are the manufacturers' own.

Is secondary glazing film worth it? On a single-glazed window it is the only option in our table that pays back inside five years, because it costs £5 to 15 and is sealed to the frame. It lasts one winter and looks like what it is.

Should curtains cover the radiator? No. A curtain hanging in front of a radiator sends its heat up behind the fabric and out through the glass. Use a sill-length curtain, or fit a shelf above the radiator, so the warm air comes into the room.
