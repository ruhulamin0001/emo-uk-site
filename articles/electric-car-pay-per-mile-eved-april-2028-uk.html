Pay-Per-Mile Electric Car Tax From April 2028: £300 a Year at 10,000 Miles, and How the Bill Actually Gets Collected

The government has confirmed a mileage charge on electric and plug-in hybrid cars from April 2028, and on 13 July 2026 published its response to a consultation that drew more than 5,000 replies. The rate did not move: 3p a mile for electric, 1.5p for plug-in hybrids. What did move is how the checks work. Here is the arithmetic at your mileage, the collection system as the Treasury now describes it, and the things eVED is widely said to do that it does not.

The short version

  • The rate is 3p a mile for electric cars and 1.5p for plug-in hybrids, from April 2028, uprated with CPI from 2029/30. Source: HM Treasury, eVED consultation (26 November 2025) and consultation response (13 July 2026), both read 5 September 2026.
  • At your mileage: 5,000 miles is £150 (£12.50 a month, the Treasury's own example); 8,000 miles is £240; 10,000 miles is £300; 12,000 miles is £360. A plug-in hybrid pays exactly half.

  • Our verdict: an average electric driver will pay about £240 a year more from 2028, against roughly £480 a year the average petrol driver already pays in fuel duty. It narrows the running-cost gap; it does not close it.

1. The rate, and what it costs at your mileage

Electric Vehicle Excise Duty, eVED, was announced at Budget 2025 and consulted on between 26 November 2025 and 18 March 2026. The Treasury's consultation document sets the rate at 3 pence per mile for fully electric cars, "around half of the 6 pence per mile the average petrol or diesel driver pays in fuel duty", and 1.5 pence per mile for plug-in hybrids, which still pay fuel duty on their petrol miles. The consultation response published on 13 July 2026 restates both figures unchanged. The rate will be uprated in line with CPI from 2029/30.

The 5,000-mile row is the Treasury's own worked example: "5,000 x 3p = £150, monthly eVED liability £12.50". The rest is the same multiplication. Add the £200 standard rate, which continues, and a 10,000-mile electric driver's annual vehicle tax from 2028 is £500, before any expensive car supplement. For comparison the document puts the average petrol or diesel driver's fuel duty at about £480 a year on about 8,000 miles, which is the figure the whole policy is built around.

2. How it is collected: estimate, pay, settle

The Treasury ruled out trackers, ruled out a tax on charging electricity, and ruled out a separate pay-as-you-go system. Instead eVED bolts on to the existing VED renewal, run by DVLA. From 1 April 2028, at your car's next renewal, you will be asked for the current odometer reading and an estimate of the miles you expect to drive in the coming year. DVLA turns that into a liability at 3p or 1.5p, and you pay it the same way you pay VED now: up front for 12 months, for six months, or by monthly Direct Debit. At the end of the period you submit the actual reading. Drive more than you estimated and there is a balancing payment, which DVLA intends to let you spread into the following year; drive less and the credit rolls forward.

The check on your honesty is the MOT. Mileage is already recorded at every MOT and is already public on GOV.UK, so DVLA will compare your declared reading against the last MOT figure. A declared mileage lower than the MOT reading is evidence of mis-declaration. Change of circumstances, a new job, an illness, a move, can be notified mid-year to adjust the estimate. For new cars, dealers will be able to bundle a first year of estimated mileage into the on-the-road price.

Three lifecycle rules. When a car is sold, the mileage already paid for stays with the car and is visible to the buyer, so a paid-up surplus is worth slightly more and a deficit slightly less. A car on SORN carries its balance to the next licensing. A car being scrapped needs a final reading at the scrapyard.

3. What the July 2026 consultation response changed

The consultation received over 5,000 responses. The government's reply, published 13 July 2026, keeps the design but drops the most-criticised piece: the proposal that cars under three years old, which have no MOT, should attend an extra mileage check at an accredited garage around their first and second anniversaries. That requirement will not go ahead. Fleets and leasing companies, who register most new electric cars, get simplified arrangements including estimated readings and bulk licensing. And the government says future eVED legislation will make a working odometer a legal requirement, create offences for tampering with one and for supplying tampering devices, with simple checks at MOT. The response notes the same design will apply in Northern Ireland, where the first MOT falls at four years rather than three.

Status, plainly: this is a government commitment with a consultation response behind it. The legislation to create the tax has not yet passed. The GOV.UK pages now carry a "published under the 2024 to 2026 Starmer Labour government" banner, which marks that they were published under the previous ministry, not that the policy has changed, and nothing published since reverses the April 2028 start. Treat it as coming, and watch the 28 October Budget for the Finance Bill measures that would enact it.

4. What eVED does not do

It does nothing before April 2028, and even then it starts at each car's first renewal after that date, so a car taxed in March 2028 does not pay until March 2029. It does not apply to petrol, diesel or self-charging hybrid cars, which carry on paying fuel duty. It does not apply to vans, motorcycles, buses or lorries at launch. It does not change the £200 standard rate, the £10 first-year rate or the £440 expensive car supplement, all of which continue, and we set those out in our 2026/27 electric car road tax guide. It does not record where or when you drive; the only data point is the odometer. It does not exempt drivers who are exempt from VED on disability grounds, because they are not exempt from fuel duty either. And it does not exempt miles driven abroad.

5. A worked example in Bolton

Rachel in Bolton drives an electric hatchback 12,000 miles a year, mostly a 40-mile round trip to work. Her car was registered in 2024 with a £34,000 list price, so today her road tax is £200 and nothing else. From her first renewal after April 2028 she will estimate 12,000 miles, be charged £360, and can pay it as £30 a month alongside the £200 standard rate, roughly £47 a month in total against about £17 today.

Suppose she over-estimates and actually drives 10,500 miles: she has paid for 1,500 miles she did not use, £45, and it is credited against the following year. Suppose she under-estimates and drives 14,000: she owes £60 more, which DVLA plans to let her spread over the next year. Her MOT each spring records the odometer, so a declared figure below the MOT reading would be flagged. Her petrol-driving neighbour on the same mileage pays roughly £720 in fuel duty at the Treasury's 6p-a-mile average, so Rachel's tax on miles is half his even after 2028, and her electricity is cheaper than his petrol on top.

6. The honest verdict

From April 2028 an electric car will cost 3p a mile in tax, £300 a year at 10,000 miles, paid through the same renewal as the £200 standard rate. The July 2026 consultation response confirmed the rate, dropped the extra garage checks for young cars and promised odometer offences. Nothing about it applies before 2028.

If you are deciding whether to go electric this year, the honest comparison is that from 2028 you will pay about half the tax per mile of a petrol driver, not none. That still leaves electricity, servicing and the £10 first-year rate on your side. If you already drive one, the thing to do in 2028 is estimate honestly and pay monthly; the thing to do now is nothing.

Frequently asked

When does pay-per-mile start? April 2028, at each car's first VED renewal on or after that date. Nothing is charged per mile before then.

How will the government know my mileage? You report your odometer reading at renewal, estimate the year ahead, and report the actual reading at the end. DVLA checks it against the mileage recorded at your MOT, which is already public. There are no trackers and no record of where or when you drove.

Does it replace the £200 road tax? No. eVED is charged in addition to the standard rate. A 10,000-mile electric driver will pay £200 plus £300 from 2028.

What if my car is under three years old and has no MOT? The original proposal for extra mileage checks at a garage was dropped in the 13 July 2026 consultation response. Your own reading is used until the first MOT.
