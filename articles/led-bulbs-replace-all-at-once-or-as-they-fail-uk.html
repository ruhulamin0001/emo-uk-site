Replacing Every Halogen At Once Pays Back In Ten Months. Waiting For Them To Blow Costs You £1.08 A Month Each

Most people replace bulbs one at a time, when one dies. For LED against halogen that instinct is expensive, and it is one of the few cases on this site where the arithmetic says spend the money today rather than wait. It is also a case where doing the obvious extra step, swapping working LEDs for newer ones, never pays back at all.

1. The 80% figure, and what it does not mean

The Energy Saving Trust's lighting guidance states that LED light bulbs are just as powerful as halogen lights but use 80% less electricity to do so. It also states that if you replace the remaining halogen spotlights in your home with LED bulbs you could save £45 a year in Great Britain.

That wording is doing more work than it looks. The 80% is a ratio between two technologies. The £45 is a whole-house figure for a house that still has halogen spotlights left in it. If your house was rewired to LED five years ago, your saving from this page is zero, and no amount of bulb shopping changes that.

The Energy Saving Trust also notes that almost all halogen lights have been replaced by LEDs on the market, and that the halogens you still find at home are mainly in spotlight fittings. That is the practical target: the recessed downlighters in kitchens, bathrooms and hallways, usually GU10 fittings, usually installed in sets of six or more.

2. Costing one bulb, so you can cost your own house

The £45 is an average. Your house is not average, so here is the method to price your own.

Ofgem's price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 sets electricity at 26.32p a kWh. There is no VAT on electricity until 31 March 2027, so that is the full rate.

The sum is: watts, divided by 1,000, times hours used a day, times 365, times 26.32p.

A 50 W halogen spotlight on for three hours a day: 50 divided by 1,000 is 0.05 kW. Times 3 hours is 0.15 kWh a day. Times 365 is 54.75 kWh a year. Times 26.32p is £14.41 a year.

Its LED equivalent draws about 5 W for the same light output, which is the 80% reduction plus a little. 5 W on the same schedule is 5.475 kWh a year, or £1.44 a year.

The gap is £12.97 a year for one bulb, which is £1.08 a month. That is the number to hold on to, because it is what a single halogen still burning in your ceiling costs you every month you leave it there.

Sense-check it against the Energy Saving Trust's £45. Forty-five pounds divided by £12.97 is 3.5. So the national figure corresponds to about three and a half halogen spotlights' worth of use. If you have twelve downlighters in a kitchen you use heavily, your saving is well above £45. If you have two in a downstairs loo, it is well below.

4. All at once, or as they fail

The instinct is to replace bulbs when they blow. For halogen that instinct costs money, and the amount is easy to state: £1.08 a month for every halogen spotlight still in place.

A halogen spotlight is often quoted at around 2,000 hours of life. At three hours a day that is nearly two years. So waiting for a halogen to fail can mean paying £1.08 a month for another twenty months, which is £21.60, to avoid spending a few pounds now.

We are not going to print a bulb price. Prices move, and typing an Amazon price into a static page is both unreliable and against the Associates terms. Assume £3 a bulb for the arithmetic below and check the real price before you buy.

Twelve GU10 fittings at an assumed £3 each is £36. Against the Energy Saving Trust's £45 a year, that is £36 divided by £45, or 0.8 years, about ten months. Against the per-bulb figure, twelve bulbs saving £12.97 each is £155.64 a year, and the payback is under three months. The true answer for your house sits between those two, depending on how many hours those fittings actually run.

Either way it is under a year, and the alternative is a slow drip of £1.08 a month per fitting for as long as you wait. Do them in one go, on one afternoon, with the power off at the consumer unit.

One practical note before you buy twelve of anything. Some older GU10 fittings are wired through a transformer for low voltage halogens, and mains-voltage LED GU10s will not work in those. Check one bulb before you buy the set.

5. The upgrade that never pays back

Once every halogen is gone, this page is finished with you. The saving lived entirely in the halogen-to-LED step.

Marketing for newer LED generations talks about improved efficacy, measured in lumens per watt. The improvement is real and it is small. Going from 5 W to 4 W for the same light saves 1 W, which on our three hours a day is 1.1 kWh a year, or 29p. There is no bulb price at which that is worth doing while the old one still works.

The same logic applies to smart bulbs bought for the energy saving. A smart bulb draws a little standby power of its own to keep its radio alive, so on pure electricity it is slightly worse than the dumb LED it replaces. Buy them for the control if you want the control, which our page on smart plugs and standby covers, but do not buy them expecting a saving.

The free saving that is still on the table: the Energy Saving Trust puts switching lights off as you leave a room at around £7 a year in Great Britain and £9 in Northern Ireland. That costs nothing and works on any bulb.

6. Worked example: Ffion in Wrexham

Ffion has ten 50 W halogen GU10 downlighters in her kitchen and hall, and everything else in the house is already LED. The kitchen set of six runs about four hours a day. The hall set of four runs about two.

Kitchen: six bulbs at 50 W for four hours a day is 0.3 kW for four hours, or 1.2 kWh a day, 438 kWh a year, £115.28 a year. The LED replacements at 5 W each come to 43.8 kWh, or £11.53. Saving: £103.75 a year.

Hall: four bulbs at 50 W for two hours a day is 146 kWh a year, £38.43. LEDs: 14.6 kWh, £3.84. Saving: £34.59 a year.

Total saving: £138.34 a year. Ten bulbs at an assumed £3 is £30. £30 divided by £138.34 is 0.22 years, or about eleven weeks.

Ffion's saving is three times the Energy Saving Trust's £45 because she has a heavily used kitchen full of spotlights, which is exactly the house the national average is built from a mixture of. If your kitchen looks like hers, use her numbers, not the average.

What she should not do next is replace the LEDs in the rest of the house. That would cost her real money to save 29p a bulb a year.

7. The honest verdict

If you still have halogen spotlights, replace every one of them this weekend rather than waiting for them to blow. On the Energy Saving Trust's £45 a year for a typical house, twelve bulbs at an assumed £3 each pay back in about ten months, and on a heavily used kitchen the payback is closer to eleven weeks. Every halogen you leave in place costs you about £1.08 a month until it dies.

Once they are gone, stop. Replacing a working LED with a slightly more efficient LED saves about 29p a year and never pays back. Buy smart bulbs for the control if you want the control, not for the electricity. And switch the lights off as you leave a room, which the Energy Saving Trust puts at around £7 a year and costs nothing at all.

Frequently asked

How do I know if my spotlights are halogen or LED? A halogen gets too hot to touch within a minute and comes on at full brightness instantly with a faint warmth to the light. An LED stays cool enough to touch. If the bulb is marked 50 W it is halogen; an LED for the same light is marked around 5 W.

Is it worth replacing a CFL with an LED? Marginally. A CFL replacing a 50 W halogen already uses 11 W against the LED's 5 W, so you save about £3.17 minus £1.44, which is £1.73 a year. Replace them when they fail rather than making a job of it.

Do LEDs really last longer? The Energy Saving Trust notes that LEDs last significantly longer than halogen bulbs. That matters most in fittings that are awkward to reach, where the cost of the ladder and the afternoon exceeds the cost of the bulb.

What about the harsh white light people complain about? That was an early LED problem. The Energy Saving Trust notes some early LEDs were unpopular for having too harsh a white colour compared with the warmer tones of incandescent or halogen. Modern bulbs are sold by colour temperature: around 2,700K matches the warm halogen look, 4,000K and above is the cool white.
