A Solar Diverter Only Pays Below One Particular Export Rate

This is the cleanest buying decision in the whole of home energy, because it comes down to a single comparison you can do on the back of an envelope. And it has an answer that reverses completely depending on what currently heats your hot water.

Ruhul Amin Published 11 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • A diverter does not create energy. It changes what you do with energy you already have. Instead of exporting a spare kilowatt hour, you use it to heat water. So the question is simply which is worth more.
  • If your hot water currently comes from a gas boiler, the break-even export rate is 8.86p per kWh. Below that, divert. Above it, export and let the gas heat your water.
  • If your hot water comes from an electric immersion, the break-even rate is 26.32p per kWh. No Smart Export Guarantee tariff in Britain pays that, so the diverter always wins.
  • Payback on a roughly £650 installed diverter: about 3 years for an immersion heated house on a low export rate, and 13 years or never for a gas heated one on a good rate.
  • Go and look up your own SEG rate before you read the sales pitch. It is on your export supplier's tariff page and it is the only number that matters.

1. What a diverter actually does

If you have solar panels, there are moments, mostly sunny weekday afternoons when nobody is home, when the roof is making more electricity than the house is using. That surplus goes out to the grid, and under the Smart Export Guarantee your export supplier pays you a rate per kilowatt hour for it.

A solar immersion diverter watches the flow at your meter and, the instant it sees export starting, ramps up the immersion element in your hot water cylinder to soak up the surplus instead. It does this continuously and proportionally, so it uses whatever is spare rather than switching a 3 kW element hard on and dragging power from the grid.

The best known UK device is the myenergi eddi, which can redirect surplus to any electrical resistive load up to 3.68 kW and comes with a 3 year warranty as standard, both stated on myenergi's own product page. That 3.68 kW limit comfortably covers the 3 kW immersion element in a standard British cylinder. Installed, these devices typically land somewhere around £550 to £750.

Notice what the device does not do. It does not generate anything and it does not reduce your import. It takes a unit you were going to sell and uses it instead. So the entire value proposition is the difference between what that unit was worth sold and what it is worth used. Everything else is detail.

2. The break-even export rate, derived

Here is the whole calculation, and it takes four lines.

What you give up when you divert a kilowatt hour is your export payment, which is your SEG rate.

What you gain is one kilowatt hour of heat in the cylinder that you now do not have to buy from somewhere else. What that is worth depends entirely on what would otherwise have heated it.

If your cylinder is heated by a gas boiler. The Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 puts gas at 7.97p per kWh. A boiler running at about 90 per cent efficiency needs 1.111 kWh of gas to deliver 1 kWh of heat, so delivered heat costs 8.86p per kWh.

If your cylinder is heated by an electric immersion. Electricity under the same cap is 26.32p per kWh, and an immersion element is effectively 100 per cent efficient at putting that into the water, so delivered heat costs 26.32p per kWh.

So the rule is:

  • Gas heated cylinder: divert if your SEG rate is below 8.86p per kWh. Export if it is above.
  • Immersion heated cylinder: divert if your SEG rate is below 26.32p per kWh, which is to say, always.

That is the article. Everything from here is how big the numbers are and how fast the payback runs.

One honest gap, stated plainly: we have not verified a specific supplier's current SEG rate in this piece, because they change frequently and vary hugely between suppliers and between fixed and variable export tariffs. The two break-even thresholds above do not depend on that. They are derived entirely from the Ofgem cap rates, which we did read today. Look up your own rate and compare it to 8.86p or 26.32p.

4. Payback, and how much a diverter really catches

The 1,000 kWh figure used above needs justifying, because it is the one that moves most.

A typical 4 kWp domestic array in Britain generates somewhere around 3,400 kWh a year. Without a battery or a diverter, households typically use about 40 per cent of that on the spot and export the rest, so roughly 2,000 kWh a year goes to the grid.

A diverter cannot catch all of that. It can only divert when there is surplus and the cylinder is not already up to temperature, which in practice means it fills the cylinder on a good morning and then has nowhere to put anything until the water cools or gets used. Across a British year, with December to February contributing almost nothing, 800 to 1,200 kWh is a realistic catch for a household with a normal hot water demand. We use 1,000.

Now the payback, in the usual shape.

Installed cost, divided by the annual net gain, equals payback in years.

  • Immersion heated cylinder, SEG 4p: £650 divided by £223.20 is 2.9 years. Excellent, and comfortably inside the device's life.
  • Immersion heated cylinder, SEG 15p: £650 divided by £113.20 is 5.7 years. Still good.
  • Gas heated cylinder, SEG 4p: £650 divided by £48.60 is 13.4 years. Marginal at best, and longer than many people keep a house.
  • Gas heated cylinder, SEG 15p: never. It has a negative return.

If you are in the marginal case, there is a better thing to do with £650. A cylinder jacket and pipe lagging costs a fraction of that and pays back in months, and it makes any diverter you fit later work better, because a well insulated cylinder holds the heat you diverted into it instead of dribbling it into the airing cupboard. Do the cheap thing first, always.

A battery is the other competing use of the same surplus, and it beats a diverter on flexibility because stored electricity can do anything while stored heat can only be hot water. It also costs five to ten times as much, which is the trade.

5. Worked example: Stuart in Perth

Stuart has a 4.2 kWp array on a bungalow with no gas supply. His hot water comes from a 3 kW immersion element in a vented cylinder, which is why his electricity bill has always looked alarming.

He checks his export tariff and finds he is on a variable SEG rate. Whatever the figure, he already knows the answer: his hot water costs him 26.32p per kWh to make, and no export tariff in the country pays that. He is on the always-divert side of the line.

He has an eddi fitted for £690. Over the following twelve months he logs 1,140 kWh diverted, mostly between March and October.

Value of that heat at 26.32p: £300.05. Export income given up, at his rate, £91.20. Net gain £208.85 a year.

£690 divided by £208.85 is 3.3 years. The device carries a three year warranty, so it pays for itself at roughly the moment the warranty expires, which is a slightly uncomfortable coincidence but a real result.

The thing he did not expect: in June and July the cylinder is at temperature by eleven in the morning and the diverter has nothing to do for the rest of the day. He now runs the dishwasher and washing machine on a timer at midday to soak up the rest, which costs nothing to arrange and is worth more than any hardware upgrade. That principle, using power when you have it rather than when you are used to, is the same one behind an off-peak EV tariff.

6. The honest verdict

Electric immersion and a hot water cylinder: buy one. The heat you are replacing costs 26.32p a unit and nothing you can export for comes close. Payback is roughly three to six years depending on your export rate.

Gas boiler and a cylinder: look up your SEG rate first, and only buy if it is below 8.86p per kWh. Above that you would be selling a unit for more than the heat it buys you, and the device makes you poorer every year it runs.

Combi boiler and no cylinder: do not buy one. There is nowhere for the heat to go.

And whatever your situation, lag the cylinder before you buy any electronics. It costs a fraction as much, pays back in months, and makes everything downstream of it work better.

If you are buying

The specification that matters is the maximum resistive load the device can handle, which needs to exceed your immersion element, and whether it can drive two loads if you have a second cylinder or a towel rail. The myenergi eddi handles up to 3.68 kW and carries three years, which is the benchmark to hold other devices against. Before any of it, price a cylinder jacket.

Frequently asked

Will diverting reduce my SEG payments? Yes, and that is the point, and it is exactly why the comparison in section two matters. Every unit you divert is a unit you do not get paid for. Anybody presenting a diverter as pure free hot water is quietly ignoring half of the sum.

Does it work with a heat pump instead of an immersion? A diverter drives resistive loads, so it heats water in a cylinder through an immersion element at an efficiency of one. A heat pump makes three or four units of heat per unit of electricity, so if you have a heat pump the sensible thing is to let the surplus run the heat pump rather than an element. Some energy management systems handle this; a simple diverter does not.

Is there a legionella risk from solar heated water? Worth taking seriously. Water sitting at lukewarm temperatures is the risk, and a diverter that only ever gets the cylinder to 45 degrees on a cloudy week creates exactly that. Any cylinder heated this way should still get a periodic full heat cycle to 60 degrees, and reputable devices include a boost function for the purpose. Set it and do not switch it off to save money.
