The Convenience Is Real. So Is the Cut, and You Will Not Find It on the Pricing Page

Sub-affiliate networks do something genuinely useful: they turn ordinary outbound links into commission without you applying to anything. They are paid out of your commission, and the most quoted one in the UK describes that payment on its own payments page as a small percentage without saying what it is.

  • Joining costs nothing up front. Skimlinks states plainly that it is free for publishers to sign up, with no financial commitment, and that it works on a revenue share basis instead.

1. What a sub-affiliate network actually does

A normal affiliate arrangement means applying to a merchant or a network, being accepted, and then building tagged links by hand for that merchant only. If you mention thirty retailers across a site, that is thirty applications, thirty sets of terms and thirty link formats.

A sub-affiliate network sits between you and all of them. You add one piece of code or one account, and ordinary outbound links to participating retailers become commission-earning automatically. Skimlinks is the best known of these in the UK. Sovrn and a few others work the same way.

The value is not really the technology. It is that you stop having a relationship with each merchant and start having one relationship with the middle. That is a genuine saving in admin, and for a site that mentions many retailers lightly, it is the difference between monetising those mentions and not bothering.

It also changes who carries the approval risk. You are not applying to each programme and being rejected for being too small. The sub-affiliate network is the accepted publisher, and you sit underneath it.

2. The cut, and what is published about it

Here is the part worth being precise about, because being imprecise is how this topic is usually handled.

On its own Payments, Commissions and Costs page, Skimlinks answers the question "What does Skimlinks charge me?" like this: "Zilch! It's totally free for publishers to sign up to Skimlinks and access our services. You can try Skimlinks out with absolutely no risk on your part and no financial commitment either."

Then, immediately after: "Instead we work on a revenue share basis, meaning that for every sale your commerce content drives, we take a small percentage of it, for enabling the technology that powers the sale, build the reporting and negociate better rates from merchants."

That page does not say what the percentage is. It says small. Widely repeated third party figures put the publisher share at around three quarters, but that is not what the company publishes on its payments FAQ, and this site does not print a number it has not read from the source.

That matters for a practical reason rather than a moralising one. If you cannot see the share, you cannot compare the net rate against joining a merchant directly, which is the only comparison that decides the question. So the first action for anyone weighing this up is not to read reviews. It is to log in, or write to the network, and get the applicable share for your account in writing.

There is a second reason the share alone is not the whole comparison, and it is in Skimlinks' own wording above: part of what they claim to do is "negociate better rates from merchants". If the negotiated gross rate is higher than the rate you would get as a small publisher applying alone, a share of the larger number can beat all of the smaller one. That is a real argument and it can only be settled with both figures.

3. What is published, which is quite a lot

It is only fair to note that a good deal of the operational detail is published clearly, and more clearly than several direct programmes manage.

Two things stand out. First, the roughly 90 day clearance is not a sub-affiliate problem, it is how the whole industry works, and it applies equally to direct programmes. Our piece on how long a first affiliate sale actually takes goes through where that delay comes from.

Second, the refusal to estimate earnings is to the company's credit and matches what Amazon does in its own agreement. Nobody serious in this industry promises you a number.

4. When it is genuinely the right answer

The decision is not about the network's character. It is about the shape of your own link profile.

It is worth it when your links are wide and shallow. Many merchants, few sales each. Thirty retailers at two sales a month is an administrative impossibility to run directly and a good fit for one integration. The share you give up buys you revenue you would otherwise not have collected at all, which is a clean trade.

It is poor value when your links are narrow and deep. If eighty percent of your commission comes from three merchants, you are paying a share on your main income to avoid three applications you only have to make once. Join those three directly and keep the network for the long tail, which is a perfectly normal way to run both.

It is the only option when you cannot get accepted. New sites are turned down by direct programmes routinely. Sitting under a sub-affiliate network while you build something worth accepting is a reasonable stage, not a compromise.

Watch the overlap with Amazon. Amazon Associates is its own programme with its own Programme Policies, including rules on redirecting links and on link formats that must be satisfied whatever else is running on the page. If you run both, check how the automatic link conversion interacts with your Amazon links specifically, because a sale that is not correctly tracked because the link was not properly formatted simply does not earn.

Worked example: Tomasz in Peterborough

Tomasz runs a cycling site. Last year it earned roughly £1,800 in commission. The split is £1,300 from two big retailers and £500 spread across about twenty smaller shops.

Everything through a sub-affiliate network. He gives up the share on all £1,800, including the £1,300 he could have earned by filling in two application forms once.

Two direct, the rest through the network. He keeps the full commission on £1,300 and gives up a share only on the £500 long tail, which he had no realistic way of collecting otherwise.

The size of the difference. He cannot calculate it yet, because the share is not published. So his first step is to get the applicable rate for his account in writing. If the share were, for the sake of argument, a quarter, the split approach would be worth in the region of £325 a year, which is a lot of money for two forms.

The counter-argument he has to test. If the network's negotiated rate with those two retailers is materially better than the rate he would get applying as a one-person site, some or all of that £325 disappears. He needs both numbers before he moves anything, which is the actual homework.

The honest verdict

Sub-affiliate networks are not a scam and the convenience is not imaginary. Skimlinks publishes its threshold, its timing and its payment methods more plainly than plenty of direct programmes do, and it declines to promise earnings, which is the correct thing to do.

The honest problem is narrower than the usual criticism. The one number that decides whether you should use it for a given merchant is the one number not on the payments page. A share of a better negotiated rate can genuinely beat all of a worse one, but you cannot know which you are in without both figures, and only one of them is public.

So the practical answer is a split, not a side. Go direct on the handful of merchants that produce most of your income, because that is a one-off form for a permanent gain. Use a sub-affiliate network for the long tail, where the alternative is earning nothing at all. And before you decide which merchants sit in which group, ask for your applicable revenue share in writing, because everything above is arithmetic once you have it and guesswork until you do.

Frequently asked

What percentage does Skimlinks take? Its payments page does not say. The wording is that for every sale your content drives, it takes a small percentage, in exchange for the technology, the reporting and negotiating better rates from merchants. Third party figures circulate widely but they are not what the company publishes there, so ask for the share applicable to your account.

When do I get paid, and how much do I need to earn first? Skimlinks pays at the end of each month once your balance passes the threshold, which it publishes as $65, €55 or £50 depending on region. Before that, commissions have to be approved by retailers and paid by the affiliate network, which it describes as normally around 90 days in total.

Can I use a sub-affiliate network and direct programmes at the same time? Yes, and for most sites that is the sensible arrangement. Join directly for the merchants that produce most of your commission and leave the long tail to the network. Check the interaction with any programme that has its own link format rules, Amazon Associates in particular.

Is it worth it for a brand new site? Often yes, for a reason that has nothing to do with the rate. New sites are frequently rejected by direct programmes, and sitting under a network that has already been accepted is a way to earn anything at all while you build. Revisit the arrangement once you can see which merchants actually produce your income.
