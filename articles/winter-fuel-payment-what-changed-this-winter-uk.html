Winter Fuel Payment claims open on Monday, and the £35,000 rule is not what most people think

Claims for winter 2026 to 2027 open on 21 September. Most eligible people never need to claim at all. And the income threshold that decides whether you keep the money is measured on you individually, not on your household.

1. Who qualifies, and the date of birth that decides it

For winter 2026 to 2027, the qualifying condition on GOV.UK is a date of birth. If you were born on or before 27 June 1960, you could get between £100 and £300 to help with heating bills.

The exact amount depends on your age and your living arrangements, which is why GOV.UK publishes a range rather than a single figure. Most eligible people are paid in November or December 2026.

Claims for this winter open from 21 September 2026. Keep reading before you rush to claim, because for most people claiming is unnecessary.

2. The £35,000 rule, and why it is not means testing

This is the part that has caused the most confusion, and the confusion is understandable because the mechanism is genuinely unusual.

If your total income is over £35,000, HMRC will take the Winter Fuel Payment back.

Read that carefully. It does not say you will not get it. It says you get it, and then it is recovered. The payment is made first, and the recovery happens afterwards through the tax system. For someone taxed through PAYE, HMRC's own published material describes recovery spread across the tax year: for a typical winter payment of £200, that works out at roughly £17 a month deducted across the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

So the practical experience for a higher income pensioner is money arriving in November or December, and a slightly smaller monthly income for the following tax year, rather than a letter saying no.

This matters because a lot of people above the threshold assume they should decline or not claim. If you are paid automatically, the payment and the recovery are both handled without you doing anything.

3. The detail that changes the answer for couples

Here is the single most under-reported fact about this rule, and it changes the outcome for a lot of households.

Your partner's income does not count towards your total. The £35,000 test is applied to you as an individual.

The consequence is that a couple with a combined income well above £35,000 may both keep their payments in full, provided neither of them individually exceeds the threshold. A household on a joint income of, say, £50,000 split fairly evenly between two people is in a completely different position from a household on £50,000 where one person earns nearly all of it.

Almost every conversation we have seen about this treats it as a household income test. It is not. If you concluded that your household was over the line and stopped paying attention, it is worth checking your own individual income figure rather than the joint one.

4. Whether you actually need to claim

Most eligible people get the Winter Fuel Payment automatically and do not need to do anything at all.

You would generally need to make a claim if you do not receive a qualifying benefit and have not had the payment before, which is why GOV.UK publishes a claim route and a claim form, and why the claim window opening on 21 September is news.

The honest advice here is to check the GOV.UK page on whether you need to claim before filling anything in. Claiming when you would have been paid automatically wastes your afternoon and does not increase the payment.

What this payment does not do, so that it is clearly stated: it is not a discount on your energy bill, it is a payment to you. It does not change your tariff, it does not interact with the price cap, and it is separate from the Warm Home Discount, which is a different scheme with different rules.

One more separation worth making. The Winter Fuel Payment is not the same as Pension Credit, and it is not the same as the Warm Home Discount. Pension Credit is an ongoing means tested benefit that many eligible pensioners never claim, and it is usually worth far more over a year than any winter payment. If money is tight, checking Pension Credit entitlement is a better use of an afternoon than anything on this page.

5. A worked example: a couple who assumed they had lost it

Sylvia and Raymond live in Norwich. Both were born in the 1950s, so both are over the date of birth condition comfortably.

Their incomes are as follows.

  • Sylvia: State Pension plus a workplace pension, total around £22,000.
  • Raymond: State Pension plus a larger workplace pension and some savings interest, total around £31,000.

Their combined income is about £53,000, comfortably over £35,000, and on that basis Raymond had concluded they were no longer entitled to anything and stopped reading about it.

That conclusion is wrong. The test is individual. Sylvia at £22,000 is under the threshold. Raymond at £31,000 is also under the threshold. Neither of them is over £35,000 as an individual, so neither faces recovery, even though their household total is well above it.

Had Raymond's pension been £38,000 instead, his payment would have been recovered through his tax code across the year, and Sylvia's would not have been touched. The two are assessed separately.

The action for Sylvia and Raymond this week is small and specific: check the GOV.UK page on whether they need to claim, and if they are due to be paid automatically, do nothing and expect the money in November or December.

And if you are comfortably over £35,000 on your own income, this article does not find you any money. What it does tell you is that you do not need to decline the payment or feel you have done something wrong. The recovery is designed into the system and handled by HMRC.

Frequently asked

When do Winter Fuel Payment claims open? Claims for winter 2026 to 2027 open from 21 September 2026. Most eligible people are paid automatically and do not need to claim at all.

How much is the Winter Fuel Payment this year? Between £100 and £300 if you were born on or before 27 June 1960, depending on your age and circumstances. Most people are paid in November or December 2026.

Does my partner's income count towards the £35,000? No. GOV.UK states that your partner's income does not count towards your total. The threshold is applied to you as an individual.

What happens if my income is over £35,000? You still receive the payment, and HMRC recovers it afterwards. For PAYE taxpayers this is typically spread across the tax year, around £17 a month for a £200 payment.
