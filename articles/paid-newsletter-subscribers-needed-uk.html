A five pound subscription arrives as four pounds and change

Paid newsletters are sold on subscriber counts. The number that matters is what lands in your account after the card fee and the platform cut, and how that compares with hours you could have worked instead.

1. What comes off a five pound subscription

Three things stand between the price on your page and the money in your bank.

The card fee. Stripe's published UK standard pricing, read on 12 September 2026, is 1.5 per cent plus 20p for standard UK cards, and 2.5 per cent plus 20p for EU cards. On a £5 monthly subscription paid by a UK card that is 7.5p plus 20p, so 27.5p, or 5.5 per cent. Note which part does the damage: the percentage is 7.5p, the fixed fee is 20p.

The platform cut. Newsletter platforms take a percentage of subscription revenue, and the rates move. This site does not quote live platform rates, because a stale number here is worse than none. Read the figure off your own platform's pricing page today and put it in the sum below. The worked examples show it at nought and at a ten per cent illustration so you can see the shape either way.

Tax. Per GOV.UK you can receive up to £1,000 of gross trading income in a tax year under the trading allowance without needing to tell HMRC, although there are circumstances where a return is still required. Gross means before fees, not after, which catches people out.

2. The arithmetic, monthly against annual

Take 100 paying readers at £5 a month, all on standard UK cards.

  • Headline: 100 x £5 x 12 = £6,000 a year.
  • Card fees, monthly billing: 1,200 charges x 27.5p = £330.
  • Platform at a 10 per cent illustration: £600.
  • Left: £5,070, which is 84.5 per cent of the headline.

Now bill the same readers £60 once a year instead of £5 twelve times.

  • Card fees, annual billing: 100 charges x (90p + 20p) = £110.
  • Platform, same 10 per cent: £600.
  • Left: £5,290.

Annual billing is £220 better on identical revenue, purely because the 20p is charged once per reader instead of twelve times. That is the single highest return change available to a small newsletter, and it costs nothing to make.

It cuts the other way at low prices. At £3 a month the card fee is 24.5p, which is 8.2 per cent before any platform cut. Below about £4 a month the fixed fee starts to dominate, and a slightly higher price with the same reader count is worth more than a lower one.

3. Against a Saturday shift

The comparison nobody makes is with the alternative use of the hours.

Farhan, Oldham. He writes one issue a week. Research, drafting and editing take about five hours, so 260 hours a year. He has 100 subscribers at £5 a month on annual billing, so £5,290 net on the sums above.

  • Effective rate: £5,290 divided by 260 hours = £20.35 an hour, before tax.
  • The benchmark: the National Living Wage for 21 and over is £12.71 an hour from April 2026.
  • Break-even against that wage: 260 hours x £12.71 = £3,305 of net income, which is about 63 paying readers at £60 a year.

So the honest answer to "how many subscribers do I need" is roughly 63 before the writing pays minimum wage for the time, on those assumptions. Not 1,000. But also not 10, which is where most newsletters stop.

Two caveats that make it harder than it looks. Free to paid conversion on a small list is usually low single digits, so 63 paying readers implies a list in the low thousands. And the 260 hours excludes admin, promotion and answering email, which are real.

The verdict

Do the sum before you price. Take your platform's current cut off its own page, add 1.5 per cent plus 20p per charge for a standard UK card, and see what actually lands.

Then make the one free change: offer annual billing and default to it. On the example above it was worth £220 a year on the same revenue from the same readers.

And be honest about the hour count. A paid newsletter that clears the National Living Wage for the time it eats is doing genuinely well. One that does not is a hobby with an invoice attached, which is a perfectly good thing to be, as long as you know that is what you have.

Frequently asked

Do I have to tell HMRC about newsletter income? Per GOV.UK you can get up to £1,000 of gross trading income in a tax year under the trading allowance, and if your gross trading income is at or below that you may not have to tell HMRC, though there are circumstances where you still must. Gross means before fees.

Why does the 20p matter so much? Because it is charged per transaction regardless of size. On £5 it is 4 per cent on its own. On £60 it is 0.3 per cent. Billing frequency, not price, is what you control.

What about readers paying with a non-UK card? Stripe's published UK pricing shows a higher rate for EU cards, 2.5 per cent plus 20p, so an international audience costs more to serve. Check the current rate for the card types you actually take.
