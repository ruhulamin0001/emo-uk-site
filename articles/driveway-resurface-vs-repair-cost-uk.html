It Is Not the Size of Your Driveway. It Is Where the Water Goes

Almost everyone believes there is a size limit on paving a front garden. There is, but it only applies to one kind of surface. Lay something water can drain through, or send the rainwater to a lawn or border, and you can pave any size you like without planning permission. Get that backwards and you have built something you may be told to take up.

The short version

  • Permeable surfacing needs no planning permission, at any size. The Planning Portal states it plainly: gravel, permeable concrete block paving or porous asphalt, or rainwater directed to a lawn or border to drain naturally.
  • Over five square metres of impermeable surface does need permission, if the water cannot run to a permeable area. That is the only size threshold and it only bites on one kind of surface.
  • These rules cover houses, not flats or maisonettes, and an Article 4 Direction or a planning condition can remove permitted development rights entirely. Check yours before you order materials.
  • A dropped kerb is a separate application. It is illegal to drive over a pavement without one, and the kerb is not covered by the paving rules.
  • Repair is £200 to £900, resurfacing is £1,200 to £5,000. Divide by years of service and the repair usually wins, unless the base has failed.
  • The base is what decides it. A sound base with a tired surface is a resurfacing job. A base that has moved or was never laid properly needs digging out, and no surface treatment will save it.

1. The rule, stated exactly

Here is what the Planning Portal, which is run as a joint venture between TerraQuest and the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, says about paving a front garden in England. This was read on 13 September 2026.

You will not need planning permission if a new or replacement driveway of any size uses permeable or porous surfacing which allows water to drain through, such as gravel, permeable concrete block paving or porous asphalt, or if the rainwater is directed to a lawn or border to drain naturally.

If the surface to be covered is more than five square metres, planning permission will be needed for laying traditional, impermeable driveways that do not provide for the water to run to a permeable area.

Read those two paragraphs together and the structure becomes clear. There is no general size limit on paving your front garden. There is a size limit on sealing your front garden. The rule exists because of surface water flooding: a street of impermeable front gardens sends its rainfall into the drains all at once instead of into the ground.

You are not being limited on how much you pave. You are being asked where the water will go.

That distinction matters commercially, because it means the compliant option is often the cheaper one. Gravel is the cheapest driveway surface there is and it is automatically compliant. Permeable block paving costs a little more than standard block paving, not a lot, and removes the planning question entirely. The expensive path is impermeable tarmac or concrete plus a drainage solution, or impermeable plus a planning application.

Three exclusions the Planning Portal names, and they matter:

  • The permitted development allowances apply to houses, not to flats and maisonettes, not to other buildings, and not to houses created through certain permitted development conversions.
  • They do not apply where a planning condition, an Article 4 Direction or another restriction limits permitted development rights. Conservation areas commonly carry these.
  • The rules cover the front garden only. Fences, walls, gates and a dropped kerb are separate questions, and patios and driveways elsewhere on the plot follow different rules.

So the sequence before you spend anything is: check whether your property is in a conservation area or subject to an Article 4 Direction, then choose your surface, then price it. Doing that in the other order is how people end up with a tarmac drive and a letter.

2. Surface or base, which has failed

This is the diagnosis that decides repair against resurfacing, and it is straightforward to make yourself.

Signs the surface has failed but the base is sound. Block paving with sunken sand joints, weeds through the joints, faded colour, a few cracked blocks, tarmac that has gone grey and porous on top but is still flat. The drive is level. Nothing rocks under your feet. This is a surface job.

Signs the base has failed. Ruts or dips where the car wheels sit. Blocks that rock. Standing puddles after rain in the same places every time. Tarmac cracking in a spider web pattern rather than single straight cracks. An edge that has spread outwards. This is a dig out, and no amount of resurfacing will fix it, because the thing that moved is under the bit you can see.

The reason this matters financially is that a contractor quoting to overlay new tarmac on a failed base is selling you two to four years. The same job on a sound base is selling you fifteen. Same price, entirely different purchase, and the only way to tell them apart is to look at whether the drive is flat.

Two things jump out. The repair rows are the cheapest per year by a wide margin, exactly as they are for roofs, and for the same reason: they are cheap only while the thing underneath is still sound. And the bottom row, the one that needs permission, is not cheaper than the permeable alternatives that do not. It is the same money with a planning application attached.

Gravel deserves a specific mention because it is consistently underrated. It is the cheapest per year of any full replacement in the table, it is automatically compliant, it drains, it is easy to repair in patches, and it makes a noise when somebody walks on it, which some households value. Its drawbacks are real: it migrates, it is harder with a wheelchair or pushchair, and it needs edging and a membrane to behave. A gravel stabilisation grid deals with most of the migration for a modest addition to the cost.

4. The dropped kerb, and the other permissions

A dropped kerb is the lowered section of pavement you drive across to get onto your property. The Planning Portal is blunt about it: it is required if you wish to drive over a pavement onto your property, and it is illegal to do so without one. You have to apply to your local authority.

This is a separate application from anything to do with the paving, it has its own fee and its own criteria, and the highway authority can refuse it for reasons that have nothing to do with your garden: sightlines, proximity to a junction, a bus stop, a street tree, a utility cover in the wrong place.

The expensive sequencing error is to lay the driveway first and apply for the kerb second. If the kerb is refused, you have a very smart parking space you cannot legally reach. Apply for the dropped kerb before you commission the driveway, every time.

Two other things worth checking at the same time. If you are removing a front wall or hedge to create access, that may need permission separately, particularly in a conservation area. And if a street tree is anywhere near, expect the highway authority to have firm views.

5. Worked example: the Sandhu driveway in Slough

A 1960s semi in Slough with a block paved drive of about 26 square metres, laid around 2008. Weeds through the joints, the colour has gone, and there is a dip where the driver's side wheel sits. They get quotes for three options.

Option A, clean, re-sand and reseal. £420. Over 4 years, £105 a year.

Option B, lift and relay the sunken section only, then re-sand the whole drive. £1,100. Over 12 years, £92 a year.

Option C, full dig out and new permeable block paving. £3,800. Over 28 years, £136 a year.

The dip is the deciding fact. A dip under one wheel means the sub base has compacted locally, which is not something cleaning and sealing will touch. Option A at £105 a year looks competitive but it is buying four years of a better looking drive with the same dip in it, and the dip will keep getting worse.

Option B is the answer here, and it is also the cheapest per year of the three. Lifting a localised area, correcting the sub base and relaying the same blocks costs a fraction of a full replacement, and because they are reusing the existing blocks the colour matches, which it never does if you buy new ones for a patch.

Option C is the right answer only if the dips are widespread, or if the blocks themselves have degraded, or if they want something different. On planning, note that all three options are permeable or repair work, so none of them raises a planning question at all. If they had wanted to replace the whole thing with impermeable tarmac and no drainage, 26 square metres is well over the five square metre threshold and they would have needed permission.

Frequently asked

Is block paving permeable? Standard block paving is not, because the blocks and the bedding are designed to shed water. Permeable block paving is a specific product with wider joints, a permeable jointing material and an open graded sub base underneath. They look similar and they are not the same thing. Ask for it by name and get it written on the quote, because the whole planning position depends on it.

What about resin bound? Resin bound gravel is generally permeable, which is one of its selling points. Resin bonded is generally not. The two names are one letter apart and the planning consequence is completely different, so confirm which one you are being quoted for.

Does a driveway add value? In an area with difficult on street parking, meaningfully yes. In an area with plenty of parking, very little. Either way, if you are doing it for value rather than for use, make sure the dropped kerb is achievable first, because a drive nobody can legally enter adds nothing.

What if my drive is at the side or back? The Planning Portal notes that its front garden rules cover the front garden only, and that there are different rules for patios and driveways that are not part of a front garden. Check your specific situation rather than assuming the five square metre rule applies everywhere.

Do I need building regulations approval? A driveway itself generally does not, unlike the roof and electrical work covered in our guides to roof replacement and rewiring. But if the work involves retaining walls, steps or changes to levels, ask, because those can pull it in.

The honest verdict

Check whether your drive is flat before you get a single quote. If it is flat and just looks tired, repair it. At £42 to £300 a year the repair rows beat every replacement in the table, and a contractor who goes straight to a full dig out on a sound base is not doing you a favour.

If you are replacing it, choose a permeable surface. Gravel or permeable block paving removes the planning question completely at any size, and gravel in particular is the cheapest per year of any full replacement. Impermeable tarmac without drainage over five square metres is the only option in this article that needs permission, and it is not cheaper than the ones that do not.

And if you need a dropped kerb, apply for it first. Everything else on this page is reversible. A refused kerb after a finished driveway is not.
