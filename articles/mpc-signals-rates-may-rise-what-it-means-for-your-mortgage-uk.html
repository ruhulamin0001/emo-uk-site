Everyone is waiting for a rate cut. The Bank has just said the next move may be up

Bank Rate has sat at 3.75% through three meetings. Most coverage treats that as a pause before cuts. On 17 September the Governor said something different, and it changes how a fixing decision looks.

The short version

  • Bank Rate is 3.75%, maintained at three consecutive meetings: 18 June, 30 July and 17 September 2026.
  • Inflation is 3.1%, against the Bank's 2% target, and the Bank expects it to rise further.
  • The Governor said the more likely outcome may be a rise. Andrew Bailey said the longer energy volatility persists, the more likely it is the Bank will need to raise Bank Rate.
  • The cause is energy. Conflict in the Middle East has disrupted energy supply, pushing up motor fuel and utility bills.
  • The next decision is 5 November 2026, with a Monetary Policy Report.

1. What the Bank actually said on 17 September

On 17 September 2026 the Monetary Policy Committee maintained Bank Rate at 3.75%. That was the third consecutive meeting at that level, following 18 June and 30 July.

A third hold is easy to read as a pause on the way down. The Bank's own published summary does not read that way. Its key points that day were that inflation has risen to 3.1%, that it expects inflation to go up further as higher energy prices feed through, and that the longer energy costs stay high, the greater the risk of more persistent inflation.

The Governor's own words, published alongside the decision, were that the longer the volatility persists, the bigger the impact on inflation, and the more likely it is the Bank will need to raise Bank Rate to bring inflation back to the 2% target.

That is not a forecast of a rise. It is a statement about which direction the risk points. Those are different things, and the difference matters more than almost anything else in this article.

2. Why the direction of risk has turned

The Bank's stated cause is energy. Conflict in the Middle East has disrupted the transportation and supply of energy, raising its price and pushing up both motor fuel costs and household utility bills.

Two things are holding the effect back for now, on the Bank's own account. There is so far little evidence of significant knock-on effects into wider prices and wages. And demand is being restrained: mortgage rates and business borrowing costs are higher than before the conflict, making people more cautious about spending, while there are more people looking for work than there are jobs available, so employers feel less pressure to raise salaries.

That is a finely balanced picture rather than an alarming one. But it explains why the Committee is not cutting, and why it is talking about the possibility of raising.

3. What a direction of risk does to a fixing decision

Here is the mechanism that most household coverage skips.

Fixed mortgage rates are not priced off today's Bank Rate. They are priced off swap rates, which reflect what markets expect Bank Rate to average over the next two or five years. This is why a fixed rate can rise in a week when the MPC held, and fall in a week when it cut. The two are answering different questions.

So the practical consequence of the Bank shifting its language towards a possible rise is not that your fixed rate changed on 17 September. It is that the expectations feeding into fixed pricing now have a different balance in them than they did when everyone assumed the next move was down.

For someone on a tracker, a hold means no change this month, and a rise would feed through directly and quickly.

For someone on a fix that still has years to run, none of this touches you until it ends. That is the entire point of having fixed.

4. A worked example: a remortgage six months out

Siobhan owns a flat in Belfast. Her five year fix ends in March 2027. She has £148,000 outstanding.

Between now and March there are two MPC decisions: 5 November 2026, which comes with a Monetary Policy Report, and 17 December 2026. The February 2027 meeting falls on 4 February, also with a Report, just before her fix ends.

Here is what she can actually control, and it is not the Bank Rate.

  • Most lenders let you secure a new rate some months ahead of your current deal ending, and let you switch to a better one if rates fall before completion. That is an option with no premium attached, and it is the single most useful thing available to her.
  • Her loan to value determines which rate band she is offered. If her balance and her property value put her near a band boundary, an overpayment before she applies can move her into a cheaper band. That is a bigger lever for her than a 0.25 percentage point move in Bank Rate.
  • Product fees are part of the price. A lower rate with a large fee is not automatically cheaper on a balance of £148,000, and the comparison has to be done on total cost over the fixed period, not on the headline rate.

We are deliberately not publishing a monthly payment figure for Siobhan. To do that honestly we would need her exact rate, remaining term and repayment type, and we do not have them. A made up payment figure would look authoritative and mean nothing.

What we can publish is the general arithmetic, which is the part that does not depend on knowing Siobhan. A quarter point is worth roughly £14 a month for every £100,000 borrowed on a 25 year repayment mortgage. These are our own calculations on the standard repayment formula, 25 year term, capital and interest, and the rates are illustrative rather than quotes. Two things are worth reading off the table rather than out of the news. The first is that a single quarter point is a smaller number than the coverage implies: on a £200,000 loan it is about the price of a takeaway a month. The second is the column that matters more, which is the gap between a good rate and a poor one. Being on 5.00% rather than 4.50% on that same £200,000 costs £690 a year, which is two full quarter point rises, and unlike the MPC vote it is a thing you can actually do something about.

Where the table stops being useful: it assumes a repayment mortgage and a 25 year term. On interest only the difference is simply the rate applied to the balance, so a quarter point on £200,000 is £500 a year flat. On a term much shorter than 25 years, more of every payment is capital, so a rate change moves the payment proportionally less than the table shows.

5. The honest limits of all of this

Nobody reading this, and nobody writing it, knows what the MPC will do on 5 November. The Committee itself does not know, because it will decide on data that has not been published yet.

What has changed is worth stating precisely and no more strongly than that. For most of this year the assumption embedded in household coverage has been that rates are on a path downwards. The Bank's September communication points the risk the other way, and gives a reason: energy.

If you are on a fix with years left, this changes nothing for you. If you are on a tracker, it is a reason to check that your budget survives a rise rather than only a fall. If you are remortgaging within six months, it is a reason to secure a rate you can still improve on, rather than waiting for a cut that the Bank itself is not promising.

And if none of those describe you, the correct response to this article is to close it. A change in the direction of monetary policy risk is genuinely interesting and, for a great many households, entirely without consequence this month.

Frequently asked

Is the Bank of England going to raise interest rates? Nobody knows, including the Committee. What the Bank said on 17 September is that the longer high energy costs persist, the more likely it is that a rise will be needed. That is a direction of risk, not a decision.

What is Bank Rate now? 3.75%. It has been maintained at that level at three consecutive meetings: 18 June, 30 July and 17 September 2026.

Why is inflation rising? The Bank points to conflict in the Middle East disrupting energy supply and transport, which has pushed up motor fuel costs and utility bills. Inflation stood at 3.1% against a 2% target.

Should I fix my mortgage now? That depends on your own numbers, not on a rate forecast. If your deal ends within about six months, securing a rate you can still switch away from if pricing improves costs nothing and removes the guesswork.
