Teaching Local Businesses to Use Claude Costs About GBP 29 a Session to Deliver and Five Hours of Your Time. What to Charge, Why Most Firms Will Not Pay for Training, and What They Will Pay For

Somewhere near you is a plumber, a lettings agent and a nursery manager who have heard they should be using AI, tried a chat window once, and stopped. Teaching them is a real service and it needs almost no capital. It also fails in a predictable way, because small firms do not buy training, they buy a job done. This guide prices a session from the vendors' and GOV.UK's own figures, sets a floor for the fee, and describes the version of the service that gets bought.

1. What the service actually is

Strip the word training away and the service is this: two hours in a small business's own office, on their own machines, with their own documents, ending with one repeatable job set up in an AI tool and a one-page guide that a member of staff can follow next week without you. The tool is usually Claude, because its free plan covers chat, web search, file creation and memory, and its paid plan adds Cowork, which works inside the client's folders and is described in the Cowork guide. Which tool matters less than the discipline of leaving one working thing behind.

The jobs are unglamorous and that is the point: turning a folder of enquiry emails into drafted replies in the firm's tone, producing a weekly summary from a till export, tidying a quote template, drafting job adverts, summarising a supplier contract. The small business AI tools guide has the wider list of what helps and what does not. Anything involving customer personal data needs an agreement first, covered in section 6.

3. The price floor, from the hours

A two-hour session is not two hours. Before it, an hour to learn what the firm does and what it repeats, and an hour to build and test the workflow on similar material so it works first time in front of them. After it, an hour of follow-up: the guide, one email, one call when the thing breaks on Tuesday. Five hours is a fair total for a first session with a client, and it does not fall much for a second session with the same firm.

Whether a small firm will pay that for something called training is the next question, and the answer is usually no.

4. Why training does not sell, and what does

A two-person firm does not have a training budget. What it has is a list of things that are not getting done. Offer the owner a two-hour AI course and you are asking them to spend money and time to become slightly better at a tool they are not sure they need. Offer to set up the enquiry-reply draft they are currently doing by hand at ten at night, show them it working on their own inbox, and leave a sheet so the Saturday member of staff can run it, and you are selling the removal of a Tuesday-night job. Same two hours, same tool, different purchase.

Three practical consequences. Name the workflow in the offer, not the tool. Price the outcome, not the hours, and let the hours in section 3 set your floor privately. And accept that most conversations end with the owner saying they will think about it, because a firm that has never paid for software help does not start with a stranger; the ones that say yes are the ones where you already knew someone, which is also the pattern the first GBP 1,000 with AI guide describes for its local business route.

The other honest limit is capacity. Five hours a session, sold one at a time to firms you can drive to, is a ceiling of a handful a month around a job, and there is no published data on how many people doing this in the UK get past that. This article does not pretend there is.

5. A worked example in Hull

Grace in Hull works in the office of a heating firm and has quietly set up Claude to draft their quote follow-ups. Her manager's contacts are her market: a tiling firm, a lettings agent and a dog groomer, all people she has met. She offers each one a specific job, not a course. For the lettings agent it is turning viewing notes into a standard follow-up email; for the tiler, a quote template that produces a clean PDF from a voice note; for the groomer, a weekly reminder message to lapsed customers from the booking export. She charges GBP 150 a session and delivers on Saturdays.

Her cost per session is GBP 4.43 of her own Pro plan spread across the month, about GBP 11 of fuel at the HMRC rate, and 50p of printing, so GBP 15.93, plus GBP 8 a session for the public liability policy she bought after getting three quotes, a figure she has and this article does not. Her time is about five hours a session, so three sessions in a month is 15 hours for GBP 450 in fees and about GBP 72 in costs, or GBP 378 net, about GBP 25 an hour. The lettings agent asks for a second session in month two. The groomer does not, because the reminder message worked and there is nothing left to teach, which is the correct outcome for the groomer and the wrong one for a business built on repeat sessions.

Her gross trading income passes GBP 1,000 in month three, so she registers for Self Assessment by the 5 October after the end of the tax year, as the side hustle tax rules guide explains, and claims the Pro subscription, the mileage at 55p and the insurance as expenses. Class 4 National Insurance does not arise until annual profits pass GBP 12,570.

6. Insurance, data and the UK admin

Public liability insurance is not a legal requirement for a sole trader visiting premises, but some clients ask for it and it is cheap enough that most trainers carry it; get three quotes, because the price is not published anywhere reliable and this article will not guess it. Professional indemnity is a separate question and a bigger one if you ever configure something that a client then relies on for money or compliance; for a beginner the safe rule is to set up drafts and summaries and leave anything that sends money or files with a regulator alone.

Data is the part people skip. In a session you will be looking at a client's emails, customer names and prices. Under UK GDPR the firm is the controller and you are, at most, a processor for the two hours; a short written note of what you will access, that nothing leaves the premises, and that you will not copy anything is enough for most small firms and protects both of you. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for small organisations. If the client wants Claude itself to process customer data on an ongoing basis, that is a conversation about which plan they are on and what its terms say, not something to decide in a two-hour session.

The honest verdict

Teaching local businesses to use AI tools costs almost nothing to deliver, about GBP 16 to GBP 29 a session before insurance, and about five hours of your time each, which puts the fair fee in the low hundreds and the minimum wage floor near GBP 90. It is a real service for someone who already knows a few business owners and can set up one working job in front of them. It is not a training business, because small firms do not buy training, and it does not scale past a handful of sessions a month around a job. Sell the outcome, keep off anything that touches money or personal data without an agreement, and expect the best clients to stop needing you, which is the point.

Frequently asked

Do I need a qualification to teach AI tools? No. There is no licence or qualification for this. What clients check is whether the thing you set up works on Tuesday without you, which is why the service is a working workflow plus a one-page guide rather than a course.

What should I charge? This article does not quote market rates because none are reliably published. From the hours, the floor that clears the National Living Wage is about GBP 90 a session and about GBP 154 at GBP 25 an hour, before insurance. Price the outcome above that floor.

Does the client need to pay for Claude? Not for the session; the free plan covers chat, web search, files and memory. If the job needs Cowork inside their folders, that is on Pro at USD 20 a month before tax, about GBP 17.71 with VAT, and it is the client's subscription, not yours.

Can I claim my petrol? As a sole trader you can use HMRC's simplified expenses at 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles in 2026 to 2027, then 25p, instead of actual costs. Keep a mileage log.
