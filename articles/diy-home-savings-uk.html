DIY Home Jobs That Save Real Money (and Ones to Never Attempt)

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Checked August 2026

The short version
  • Most of the money in DIY is saved on small, frequent, low-risk jobs, not on the dramatic ones.
  • Gas work is illegal for anyone not Gas Safe registered. Not discouraged, illegal.
  • Most electrical work in England and Wales is notifiable under Part P. Doing it yourself and not notifying it can cost you at sale.
  • Draughtproofing, bleeding radiators and resealing a bath are the best value hours you will spend on a house.
  • The expensive mistake is not the job you cannot do. It is the job you half-do and then pay someone to undo.

One rule that decides everything

Before you start any job, ask a single question: if I get this wrong, what is the worst outcome?

Filling a crack badly means sanding it back and doing it again. Wiring a consumer unit badly means a fire, or a house you cannot legally sell without remedial certification. Those two things are not on the same scale, and the difference is not about skill. It is about consequence.

Everything below is organised by that question rather than by difficulty, because plenty of dangerous jobs are technically easy and plenty of safe jobs are fiddly.

Ten jobs genuinely worth doing yourself

Draughtproofing — letterbox, keyhole, loft hatch, floorboards, skirting gaps£150 to £400The best return in this entire article. Materials are a few pounds, the work is an afternoon, and it cuts heat loss immediately. Nobody calls a tradesperson for it, and almost nobody does it.

Clearing gutters and downpipes£80 to £200Only if you are steady on a ladder and someone is home. Blocked gutters cause damp walls, which is a genuinely expensive problem.

What you must never attempt

Gas. Any work on gas pipework, boilers, hobs, fires or flues must be done by an engineer on the Gas Safe Register. This is not advice, it is the law under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations. Doing it yourself is a criminal offence, it voids your home insurance, and carbon monoxide does not announce itself. There is no version of this that is worth the saving.

Consumer units, new circuits and anything notifiable under Part P. In England and Wales, replacing a consumer unit, adding a new circuit, or doing most electrical work in a bathroom or outdoors is notifiable building work. A registered electrician self-certifies it. If you do it yourself you must notify building control and pay for inspection, which usually costs more than hiring an electrician did. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own equivalent regimes.

Structural work. Removing a wall, cutting into joists, altering a chimney breast. Even a non-load-bearing wall can carry services, and the word "non-load-bearing" is doing a lot of work in most people's confident assessment. You need a structural engineer and building regulations approval.

Asbestos. Common in UK homes built or refurbished before 2000. Artex ceilings, old floor tiles, garage roofs, boiler flues, soffits. Do not sand it, drill it or break it. Get it tested. If it is intact and undisturbed, leaving it alone is often the correct answer.

Roof work above single storey. Falls from height are the leading cause of serious DIY injury in the UK. A roofer has scaffolding, insurance and the experience to spot what else is wrong while up there.

The grey area: legal but usually a bad idea

  • Plastering. Perfectly legal, genuinely hard. A plasterer does a room in a day to a standard you will not reach in three. If you must, do a small hidden area first and accept it will need sanding.
  • Tiling a wet area. The tiling is the easy part. The waterproofing behind it is what fails, and it fails invisibly for two years.
  • Laying laminate or engineered flooring. Achievable and satisfying, but subfloor preparation, expansion gaps and door undercuts are where it goes wrong. Watch three full videos before starting, not three minutes.
  • Fitting a kitchen. The carcasses are straightforward. The worktop, the plumbing and the electrics are not, and those are the parts you will end up paying for anyway.

Buy, hire or borrow

Tool spending is where DIY savings quietly disappear. A rough rule:

  • Buy what you will use more than three times: a decent cordless drill, a screwdriver set, a spirit level, a utility knife, a filling knife, a caulking gun, safety glasses. Mid-range is fine, cheapest is a false economy on anything with a motor.
  • Hire anything large and occasional: floor sander, SDS breaker, tile cutter, scaffold tower, carpet cleaner. A day's hire is a fraction of the purchase price and you do not have to store it.
  • Borrow. Many UK areas now have a library of things or a tool library, often run by a council or community group, lending power tools for a small membership. Underused and genuinely cheap.

Buy your consumables at a trade merchant rather than a DIY shed where you can. You do not need an account at most of them, and paint, timber, screws and sealant are frequently cheaper.

Insurance, building regs and selling the house

This is the part that turns a saving into a cost years later.

  • Home insurance. Most policies exclude damage caused by faulty workmanship, including your own. If your DIY plumbing floods the kitchen, that is likely on you.
  • Building regulations. Notifiable work without the paperwork shows up in the conveyancing enquiries when you sell. The buyer's solicitor asks for certificates, you cannot produce them, and the fix is either regularisation with building control or an indemnity policy the buyer may refuse.
  • Keep the evidence. Photograph what is behind a wall before you close it up. Keep receipts, certificates and the electrician's minor works certificate. It costs nothing at the time and it is worth real money later.

Questions people ask

Can I do my own electrics at all? In England and Wales you can do non-notifiable work, such as replacing a socket faceplate or light fitting like for like, or adding a fused spur to an existing circuit outside a special location. Anything in a bathroom, outdoors, a new circuit or a consumer unit is notifiable. If in doubt, ask an electrician what category the job falls into. That question is free.

Is a Gas Safe engineer really necessary for disconnecting a cooker? Yes, if it is a gas cooker. Disconnecting and capping a gas supply is gas work.

How do I find a tradesperson who is not going to overcharge me? Three written quotes, all itemised, all from people who have been to the property. Check Gas Safe or NICEIC or NAPIT registration numbers on the official register, not on the van. Be suspicious of anyone who needs a large deposit for materials on a small job.

What is the single highest-value DIY afternoon? Draughtproofing, on any older UK house. It costs very little, needs no skill, and you feel the difference the same evening.

The verdict

Do the small, reversible, unglamorous jobs and do them properly. Pay a professional for anything involving gas, notifiable electrics, structure, asbestos or heights, and do not treat that as a defeat. The saving in DIY comes from doing twenty cheap things well, not from attempting one expensive thing badly.
