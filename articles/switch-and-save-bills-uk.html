The Insurance Loyalty Penalty Was Banned in 2022. Most Advice Has Not Caught Up.

Switching is still one of the best-paid hours available to a UK household, but the reasons have changed. Two regulators closed the biggest loopholes, which means some of the advice you have been given no longer works, and the money has moved somewhere else.

Ruhul Amin Updated 26 August 2026 · 9 min read · Rules checked August 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Insurance price walking has been banned since 1 January 2022. Your renewal quote must be no higher than the equivalent new-customer price through the same channel. The old "they punish loyal customers" advice is out of date for home and motor insurance.
  • That does not mean renewal is the best price. It means the penalty is no longer built into the renewal specifically. Shopping around still beats it, because different insurers price the same risk very differently.
  • Broadband and mobile changed too. Contracts taken from 17 January 2025 must state any future rise in pounds and pence upfront, rather than as an inflation-linked percentage.
  • Ofcom already forces providers to tell you when your contract is ending and what their best deal is. Most people delete that text.
  • Energy is where the biggest single free win is, and it is not switching supplier. Ofgem confirmed the cap at £1,723 on direct debit against £1,861 if you pay on receipt of a bill. That is £138 a year for the same energy.
  • The order matters. Payment method, then contract end dates, then haggle, then switch. Most people do it backwards.

1. What the regulators actually changed

Almost every article about switching still opens with the same line: companies quietly raise prices for loyal customers, so loyalty is punished. That was accurate for years. In two of the biggest categories it is now substantially wrong, and repeating it sends people looking for money in the wrong place.

What changedWhenWhat it means for you
FCA ban on price walking in home and motor insurance1 January 2022Your renewal price must be no higher than what an equivalent new customer would be quoted through the same channel
Ofcom end-of-contract notificationsSince 2020Your provider must tell you when your contract ends, what you will pay after, and what their best available deal is
Ofcom rules on mid-contract price risesContracts from 17 January 2025Any in-contract increase must be set out in pounds and pence at the point of sale, not as CPI plus a percentage

Read the first row carefully, because it is the one people get wrong in both directions. The ban stops insurers charging you more because you renewed. It does not stop a different insurer being cheaper, and it does not stop your premium rising because claims costs across the market went up. Those are different things.

The rule did not make renewal the best price. It removed one specific trick. Shopping around still wins, and now it wins for a more honest reason: insurers genuinely disagree about how risky you are.

2. Where the loyalty penalty genuinely still exists

So where is the money now? Ranked by how reliably it is there.

  1. Broadband and mobile, after the contract ends. This is the clearest remaining case. Contracts typically end and the price steps up to a standard rate that nobody would have signed up for. Ofcom makes them warn you. The warning arrives as a text or an email in the middle of a busy week, and most people never act on it.
  2. Energy, if you are sitting on the default variable tariff. The price cap exists to limit that tariff, which tells you what it would otherwise be. Fixed deals are frequently available below the cap.
  3. Insurance add-ons and excess levels, rather than the headline premium. Since the headline is regulated, this is where the variation lives now: legal cover, breakdown cover, key cover, and an excess that quietly rose.
  4. Anything on auto-renewal that you forgot exists. Not a loyalty penalty exactly, but the same money. UK adults spend an estimated £39 a month on direct debits they no longer need, which is £468 a year. Our no-spend challenge guide covers the audit that finds it, and it is worth more than most switching.

3. Energy: the £138 nobody mentions

The largest single free saving in UK household bills right now is not a switch at all. It is how you pay.

Ofgem confirmed the price cap on 26 August 2026 for the period from 1 October. Same energy, three different prices:

How you payCapped annual figure, typical householdAgainst direct debit
Prepayment meter£1,678−£45
Monthly direct debit£1,723—
Paying on receipt of a bill£1,861+£138

If you pay on receipt of a bill, moving to monthly direct debit is worth about £138 a year and costs you nothing but a phone call. No comparison site, no switch, no exit fee, no risk. It is the single highest-value line in this entire article and it gets almost no coverage because it is not a switch and nobody earns commission on it.

Two honest caveats. Direct debit means a fixed monthly amount that can build a credit balance, which some households dislike, and you can ask for that balance back. And prepayment being cheapest on the capped rates is not a reason to move to it deliberately, because it carries other disadvantages.

On the tariff itself: fixed deals below the cap have been available through 2026. Compare unit rates and standing charges against your own annual kWh, not headline annual figures against each other, and check the exit fee. 4. The haggle that still works, and the script

You often do not have to leave. Retentions teams have real authority to discount, and the conversation is shorter than people expect.

Before you call, have two things: a competitor's actual quote with the price written down, and your own contract end date. Without those you are asking for a favour. With them you are presenting a decision.

What to actually say

"My contract ends on the 14th. I have a quote from [provider] at £[x] a month for [equivalent service]. I would rather not go through the hassle of moving. Can you match it?"

Then stop talking. The silence is the negotiation. The most common mistake is filling it by softening your own position.

If the first answer is no, one more line: "Can you put me through to retentions or cancellations?" The first person you speak to frequently cannot approve a discount. The department whose job is to stop you leaving can.

Be genuinely willing to leave. If you are not, you will accept the first small offer, and they can tell.

5. Do it in this order

Most people start by comparing, which is the third step, and give up before finishing. This order gets the free money first.

  1. Fix your payment methods. Energy on direct debit rather than on receipt of a bill. This is free and immediate.
  2. List every contract end date in one place, with a calendar reminder set six weeks before each. This one habit does more than any comparison site, because switching only works if you do it at the right moment.
  3. Audit recurring payments for things you no longer use. One evening, and it repeats every year.
  4. Compare before every renewal. Never let anything auto-renew unexamined.
  5. Haggle with the quote in your hand. Ask them to match or beat it.
  6. Switch if they will not. The receiving provider usually handles it.

6. Worked example: one afternoon, one household

The Okonkwo household, three bedroom semi

Energy. They pay quarterly on receipt of a bill because that is how it was set up years ago. Moving to monthly direct debit: about £138 a year, one phone call, no switch.

Broadband. Contract ended fourteen months ago and the price stepped up. A competitor quotes £6 a month less for the same speed. They call, ask for retentions, and are matched: £72 a year.

Mobile, two handsets. Both out of contract on plans that still include the cost of phones paid off long ago. Moving both to SIM only: a substantial monthly saving, and the exact figure depends entirely on what they were on, which is why we are not inventing one. Our smartphone guide shows why buying the handset separately beats a bundled contract by £150 to £350 over 24 months.

Recurring payments. One evening with the banking app finds three forgotten subscriptions at £38.98 a month, which is £467.76 a year, including a magazine unread for fourteen months.

Insurance. They compare before renewing and find a cheaper insurer. The saving is real, but note the reason: not because their old insurer was punishing loyalty, which is now banned, but because insurers price the same house differently.

The pattern worth noticing: the two largest items, the payment method and the forgotten direct debits, involved no switching at all. The switching produced less than the housekeeping did.

7. The traps

  • Introductory prices that step up. Always check the price after the offer ends, not the headline. Multiply the post-offer price by twelve and compare on that.
  • Auto-renewal. Designed to keep you paying more. Diary every renewal date so it never catches you.
  • Comparing on price alone. Cheaper insurance with a £750 excess and no legal cover is not the same product. Compare like for like or you have not saved anything, you have removed protection.
  • Exit fees. Check before you move mid-contract. A £60 saving with a £75 exit fee is a loss.
  • Cancelling a direct debit without cancelling the contract. The contract carries on, you still owe the money, and it can end up in collections. Cancel with the company first, always.
  • Switching for the sake of activity. If the numbers do not clearly favour it, staying is a decision too.

The honest verdict

Switching still pays better per hour than almost anything else you will do this year, but the reasons have moved. The insurance loyalty penalty was regulated away in 2022 and broadband price rises had to be made explicit from January 2025, so the easy narrative is out of date.

What is left is unglamorous and reliable: get your energy off billing on receipt, which is worth £138 a year for one phone call, diary every contract end date, audit the direct debits you forgot, and compare before every renewal with a real quote in your hand.

Do those five things once and you will find several hundred pounds. Then do the diary part every year, because that is the only bit that has to repeat.

Frequently asked

If price walking is banned, why did my insurance still go up? Because the ban stops you being charged more than an equivalent new customer, not because premiums are frozen. Claims costs, repair costs and your own circumstances all move the price. Compare anyway, because insurers disagree about risk.

Does the FCA ban cover all insurance? It applies to home and motor insurance. Other products have their own rules, so do not assume it covers everything you buy.

Can my broadband provider still raise the price mid-contract? For contracts taken from 17 January 2025, any rise must have been set out in pounds and pence before you signed. Older contracts may still be on the previous inflation-linked terms. Check what you actually agreed to.

Is it worth switching energy supplier at all right now? Only against your own consumption. Take your annual kWh from a recent bill, price it at the capped rates and at the fixed deal's rates, and compare the totals. "Below the cap" is measured against a typical household that may look nothing like yours.

Will switching hurt my credit score? Switching energy or broadband generally involves a soft check or none. Insurance quotes use soft searches. Applying for credit-based products is different. If you are about to apply for a mortgage, keep applications to a minimum in the months before.
