The Claimant Count Turned Upwards This Morning, and That Is the Number to Watch

The ONS released its September labour market bulletin at 7am today. Most of the headline rates barely moved, which is why the two figures that did move matter. One of them is the count of people claiming a benefit because they are out of work, and it went the wrong way.

Ruhul Amin Published 15 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Claimant Count: 1.692 million in August, up on the month and the year. July was 1.665 million. This is the figure that turned.
  • Vacancies: 702,000 for June to August, down 8,000 or 1.1% on the quarter. Outside the pandemic the last time it was this low was August to October 2014. Fewer vacancies means a longer shortlist for each one, which is where how recruiters actually search LinkedIn starts to decide who gets seen.
  • Total pay growth: 3.9%, down from 4.2%. The ONS says it was last lower than 3.9% in September to November 2020, when it was 3.7%.
  • Unemployment 4.9% and the employment rate 75.1%. Both broadly unchanged on the quarter. These are the two that did not move. What does move is the law itself: the employment law changes landing in October 2026 alter day-one rights rather than the numbers.
  • Payrolled employees: 30.2 million on the August flash estimate, down 26,000 on the month and 145,000 on the year.
  • Economic inactivity fell to 20.9%, down 0.1 percentage points on both the year and the quarter. A small piece of good news.

Read down that table and the pattern is clear. The survey based rates, which are the ones the news leads on, did not move. The administrative counts, which come from tax and benefits systems rather than from a survey, all moved in the same direction, and it is not the good one.

2. The Claimant Count is the one that turned

In the August bulletin the Claimant Count for July was 1.665 million, and the ONS described it as having decreased on the month and on the year. In this morning's bulletin the August figure is 1.692 million, described as having increased on the month and on the year.

That is a reversal of direction, not just a change of size. A month ago the series was falling. It is now rising, by 27,000 on the month.

Two caveats the ONS attaches, and they are real. The latest month is provisional and gets revised after first publication, as work capability assessments conclude and more information arrives. And revisions in recent months have tended to be downwards, so today's 1.692 million may be smaller by the time it settles. Claimant Count statistics are designated as official statistics in development.

Even with both caveats, a turn is a turn. It is also consistent with everything else in the administrative data this morning: payrolled employees down 26,000 on the month, and vacancies down 8,000 on the quarter.

3. Pay growth at a five year low

Regular pay growth, which excludes bonuses, is unchanged at 3.5%, and the ONS notes it has been relatively stable over five consecutive three month periods after a year of slowing.

Total pay growth, which includes bonuses, is the one that fell. It is 3.9%, down from 4.2% in the previous three month period. The ONS puts a date on that: it was last lower than 3.9% in September to November 2020, when it was 3.7%.

The gap between the two tells you where the fall came from. Regular pay held; bonuses did not. That is the normal early sign of employers trimming discretionary spending rather than cutting headline salaries.

In real terms it is still positive but thin. Regular pay grew 0.6% after inflation on CPIH, total pay 0.9%. There is also a wide public and private split: 6.3% for the public sector against 2.9% for the private sector, with the ONS noting public sector growth continues to be affected by the timing of pay awards this year.

4. Worked example: Nneka in Coventry

Nneka works in the private sector and is due a pay review in November. She wants to know what to ask for.

The number that applies to her is not the 3.9% headline. It is the 2.9% private sector regular pay growth figure, because that is what employers like hers are actually paying on average.

Put the two figures together. If she asks for a 6% rise on the basis of a headline she read somewhere, she is asking for roughly double what the average private sector employer is granting. If she asks for 2.9%, she gets the average and stands still against the cost of living by about 0.6 percentage points in real terms.

The useful framing, and the one the data actually supports, sits between them. Something in the region of 4% to 5% is above average without being absurd, and the argument for it has to be about her specifically rather than about the economy, because the economy-wide number does not support it.

There is one more thing in this bulletin that is directly relevant to her. Workforce jobs fell by 48,000 between March and June 2026, with self employment jobs down 43,000 of that. If the fallback plan behind her pay conversation is going freelance, that component of the market is contracting faster than the employee component, and our guide to setting freelance rates in the UK is worth reading before she treats it as a safety net.

5. What to be careful about in these numbers

The ONS spends an unusual amount of this bulletin telling users how carefully to read it, and that deserves passing on rather than burying.

The Labour Force Survey is still recovering. Response levels have improved to close to pre-pandemic levels, with the achieved sample rising from 80,078 individuals in January to March 2026 to 81,193 in April to June 2026. The ONS still advises focusing on long term movements rather than short term changes.

The LFS is moving out of accredited status. On 11 August 2026 the ONS wrote to the Office for Statistics Regulation confirming that LFS and Annual Population Survey outputs currently designated official statistics in development will move to official statistics, and that it will not seek reaccreditation, prioritising the Transformed Labour Force Survey instead.

Employee measures disagree with each other. LFS employees rose 111,000 on the year to 29.8 million. RTI payrolled employees fell. The ONS says RTI currently provides the most reliable measure of employees, and is publishing new research linking RTI to the LFS on 28 September 2026.

The Bank of England saw this first. The ONS granted the Bank exceptional pre release access at 10am on Monday 14 September, so the data were available for the Monetary Policy Committee meeting held that day. The rate decision follows at noon on 17 September.

The next labour market release is 20 October 2026.

The honest verdict

Nothing in this morning's release is dramatic, and that is itself the story. Unemployment and employment did not move. What moved were the two things measured from administrative records rather than from a survey: the Claimant Count turned upwards to 1.692 million, and payrolled employment kept drifting down.

For a household, the practical read is narrow and specific. Private sector pay growth of 2.9% is the number to take into a pay conversation, not the 3.9% headline. And if you are in work and were thinking about moving, the vacancy count at 702,000 is the lowest outside the pandemic since 2014, which argues for lining something up before handing anything in.

Frequently asked

Which figure in this release matters most? The Claimant Count, because it reversed direction. It fell in the August bulletin and rose in this one, from 1.665 million in July to 1.692 million in August. The survey based rates were broadly unchanged.

Why did total pay growth fall while regular pay growth held? Total pay includes bonuses and regular pay does not. The two figures diverging in that direction indicates bonus payments softening rather than headline salaries being cut.

Is the Claimant Count the same as unemployment? No. The Claimant Count measures people receiving a benefit principally because they are unemployed, including Jobseeker's Allowance and Universal Credit claimants in the searching for work group. Unemployment comes from the Labour Force Survey and uses an international definition. The ONS recommends using them together.
