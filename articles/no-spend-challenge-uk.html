A No Spend Month Will Not Fix Your Finances. Finding the Forgotten £39 a Month Will

The idea is simple: spend nothing for a month except the essentials. The problem is that almost everyone quits in week two, and the people who finish usually watch the money drain straight back out in March. The version that works treats the month as a diagnostic, not a cure.

1. What a no spend month actually means

A no spend month is a fixed period, usually 30 days, where you buy nothing outside a list of essentials you agree with yourself in advance. Rent or mortgage, energy, council tax, food, medicine, transport to work and existing debt payments stay. Everything else stops.

That last sentence is where most people go wrong, because "everything else" is not obvious. Is a haircut essential? A birthday present for your mother? The train fare to a wedding you agreed to in April? If you have not decided before day one, you will decide in the moment, and in the moment you will say yes.

The challenge is popular because it is simple to explain and it feels decisive. It is not a budgeting system. It is a short, blunt experiment that shows you where your money actually goes when you stop it moving.

2. What it realistically frees up, in pounds

Start from the real numbers rather than the ones in the videos. The average UK household budget is around £3,106 a month, based on ONS Family Spending data uprated for inflation to mid 2026. Of that, roughly £1,400 a month goes on essential bills according to MoneySuperMarket's Household Money Index. For an average adult in a UK city, what is left after tax, bills and essential living costs is about £575 a month.

So the honest ceiling on a no spend month is your discretionary spend, and for most households that is somewhere between £300 and £700. Nobody stops all of it. Realistically you keep somewhere between a third and half.

Expect £200 to £400 for a typical household. One time. If a guide promises you £1,000 from a no spend month, it is either talking to somebody on a much higher income or it is not talking about a month.

The month tells you where the money goes. It does not stop it going there. Those are two different jobs, and only one of them needs willpower.

3. The £39 a month nobody notices

Here is the part of your spending that a no spend month does not touch, because it happens without you doing anything.

UK consumers spend an estimated £1.6 billion a year on subscriptions they do not use. Citizens Advice put the figure for unused subscriptions at £688 million a year. Around £602 million a year comes specifically from free or discounted trials rolling into paid contracts, affecting more than 3.5 million people. In 2025, 40% of UK adults had at least one unwanted subscription auto renew.

Per person, the commonly quoted figure is about £39 a month on direct debits that are no longer needed. That is £468 a year. And 48% of people say the reason they have not cancelled is simply that the direct debit makes it easy to forget.

Now compare the two. A no spend month is thirty days of saying no to yourself, for a one off £200 to £400. An evening with your bank statement is one sitting, for £468 a year, every year, with no willpower required afterwards. The second one is worth more and costs less.

What to do, in order:

  • Open your banking app and filter to direct debits and recurring card payments. Recurring card payments are the ones people miss, because they are not direct debits and do not appear in that list.
  • Write down every one with the amount and the date. Do not judge yet, just list.
  • For each, ask one question: have I used this in the last 60 days? Not "would I", not "might I". Have I.
  • Cancel the contract with the company first, then cancel the payment. Cancelling only the direct debit leaves the contract running and you still owe the money.
  • Set a calendar reminder for any free trial you keep, two days before it converts.

One thing worth knowing: the rules are about to get better, but not yet. The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 includes a subscription contracts regime that forces businesses to let you cancel online if you signed up online, to send reminders, and gives you a new 14 day cooling off period on auto renewals. It has been delayed and is not expected to take effect until spring 2027. Until then, the cancelling is on you.

4. The second leak is your fridge

WRAP, the UK waste body, estimates that UK households throw away around 4.4 million tonnes of edible food a year, worth about £17.5 billion. For a household of four that works out at roughly £1,000 a year in food that was bought and then binned. WRAP's own figure for halving household food waste is about £10 a week, or around £500 a year.

This matters here because a no spend month usually makes food waste worse, not better. People do a large panic shop on day one to avoid shopping later, then throw a third of it away. If you are going to do the month, plan five meals, not thirty.

5. Why it dies in week two

Week one is easy because it is novel. Week four is easy because the end is visible. Week two is where it collapses, and it is almost always for one of four reasons.

  1. The rules were never written down. An undefined rule is not a rule, it is a mood. The first genuinely awkward situation, a colleague's leaving lunch, ends it.
  2. There was no destination for the money. If the money you did not spend just sits in your current account, your brain treats it as available. By week three it is gone and you cannot say where.
  3. It was all or nothing. One slip becomes proof that the whole thing failed, so people abandon the remaining eighteen days that would still have counted.
  4. It replaced spending with resentment. A month of denying yourself everything reliably produces a spending binge in week five that costs more than the month saved.

6. The five rules that keep it alive

  1. Write the exception list before day one. On paper. Mine would be: prescriptions, anything already committed to another person, and travel to work. Yours will differ. The point is that it exists before you need it.
  2. Allow one spend day. Pick a date, mid month, with a cash limit. A planned release valve prevents an unplanned blowout.
  3. Move the money the same day. When you decide not to buy the £34 thing, transfer £34 out of your current account immediately, into a separate pot. Money you can see is money you will spend.
  4. Give it a target with a name. "Saving money" fails. "£380 towards the car service in November" survives, because you can tell whether you are winning.
  5. Do the direct debit audit in week one, not at the end. This is the single highest value hour of the whole month and people leave it until they have lost interest.

7. A worked example with real numbers

Priya, 34, Leeds. Take home pay £2,150 a month.

Fixed costs: rent £850, council tax £132, energy £96, water £34, phone £22, travel £78, insurance £41, minimum credit card payment £45, food £280. Total £1,578. That leaves £572 of discretionary spend, which is almost exactly the UK city average.

What the no spend month did. Priya cut discretionary spending from £572 to £248 across September. Saved: £324, once.

What the direct debit audit did. In week one she found three payments she had forgotten: a streaming service at £12.99, a fitness app at £9.99 and a magazine subscription at £16.00. Total £38.98 a month, which is £467.76 a year. She had been paying the magazine for fourteen months without reading it, so that alone had already cost her £224.

What the food change did. Instead of one big shop she planned five meals a week and shopped twice. Her bin waste roughly halved. Using WRAP's figure that is about £10 a week, so £520 a year.

The comparison that matters: the month she had to fight for produced £324 once. The two changes that needed no ongoing willpower produced £987.76 every year, roughly three times as much, and they keep producing it in year two.

8. Who should skip this entirely

  • If you are carrying debt at 25% APR or higher. Your money does more work paying that down than sitting in a no spend pot. Deal with the interest first.
  • If the problem is income, not spending. If your essentials already exceed what comes in, no amount of not buying coffee closes that gap. That is a different problem and it needs a different answer, whether that is a benefits check, a pay rise conversation or extra earnings.
  • If you have done one before and binged afterwards. Do the direct debit audit on its own and skip the month. You get most of the money and none of the rebound.
  • If you already track every pound. You already know where it goes. The diagnostic has nothing to tell you.

The honest verdict

A no spend month is a good diagnostic and a poor cure. It will show you, uncomfortably and quickly, what you buy without deciding to. That is genuinely useful information and most people have never seen it laid out.

But the money that changes your year is not the money you refuse to spend in September. It is the £39 a month that leaves your account whether you are paying attention or not, and the food you buy and then bin. Both of those are fixed with an evening and a habit, not with willpower.

If you only do one thing from this guide, open your banking app tonight and list your recurring payments. That single hour is worth more than the whole month.

Frequently asked

Will a no spend month hurt my credit score? Spending patterns are not on your credit file, so no. One caution: if you close a credit card during it, your total available credit falls and your utilisation percentage rises, which can nudge your score down.

Should I cancel the direct debit or contact the company? Contact the company and cancel the contract first, then cancel the payment. If you only cancel the direct debit, the contract carries on and you still owe the money, which can end up in collections.

What counts as an essential? Whatever you wrote down before day one. That is the only honest answer, and it is why writing it down matters more than the definition itself.

Is a no spend month worth it if I already budget carefully? Probably not. Do the recurring payment audit instead, which is worth doing even for people who budget, because these payments are invisible precisely because they are automatic.
