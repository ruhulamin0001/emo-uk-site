The £500 Question Everyone Gets Backwards When They Buy a House

People choose a survey level by price, which is the one criterion that does not matter, because the difference between the levels is not effort, it is permission. A Level 2 surveyor is allowed to leave things out that a Level 3 surveyor is not. Here is what that means at the moment you are deciding.

The short version

  • The RICS Home Survey Standard sets three levels. Level 1 is a condition report, Level 2 is the old HomeBuyer, Level 3 is the old full building survey.
  • All three use the same 1, 2, 3 condition ratings. What changes is how much the surveyor is required to explain, investigate and advise.
  • A Level 2 is a visual inspection of accessible areas. It is not required to move furniture, lift carpets, or enter a roof space that is awkward to reach.
  • The typical price gap is £300 to £600. A single missed defect in a roof, a wall or a drain is a four-figure number, which makes the gap a very cheap option.
  • Neither a Level 2 nor a Level 3 includes a valuation by default. A lender's valuation is a separate product done for the lender, not for you, and it is not a survey.

1. What the three levels actually are

Since the RICS Home Survey Standard came in, residential surveys in the UK are described by level rather than by the old trade names, which is a genuine improvement because the old names told you nothing.

Level 1, a condition report. A visual inspection producing condition ratings and very little narrative. It does not advise on repairs, does not diagnose causes, and does not give a valuation. It suits a modern house in visibly good order that you are buying for the reassurance of a second pair of eyes.

Level 2, the HomeBuyer. The same visual inspection, but with explanation. What was seen, what it means, what action you may need to take, and in most versions advice on repairs and ongoing maintenance. This is the default choice, and for a conventional post-war house in reasonable condition it is usually the right one.

Level 3, the building survey. A substantially more detailed inspection. The surveyor describes the defects, identifies likely causes, sets out the consequences of leaving them, and recommends what to do next and in what order. It is the level intended for older, larger, altered or unusual properties, and for any house you plan to change significantly.

A Level 3 report does not include a market valuation as standard, and neither does a Level 1. Valuation is a separate service. If you want one, ask for it and expect it to be priced separately.

2. What a Level 2 is permitted to leave out

This is the section that should decide your choice, and it is almost never the section people read.

Every level of survey is a non-invasive visual inspection. No surveyor at any level is lifting floorboards, opening up walls, or taking down ceilings without your instruction and the seller's consent. That much is common ground.

What separates the levels is the depth of investigation expected within that visual constraint. At Level 2, the surveyor inspects what is readily accessible and reports what is readily apparent. A roof space reached only by an awkward hatch over a stairwell, a cellar with a locked door, a flat roof with no safe access, and anything behind a fitted wardrobe or a stored-to-the-ceiling garage can all reasonably be recorded as not inspected.

At Level 3, the expectation shifts. The surveyor is expected to take more trouble to gain access, to look further, and, crucially, to explain the cause and consequence of what they find rather than simply flagging it. A Level 2 that says "evidence of past movement, further investigation advised" and a Level 3 that says "historic movement consistent with clay shrinkage, stable, monitor" are describing the same wall and pointing you in completely different directions, one of which involves another specialist and another fee.

Read the "not inspected" list in any report you receive. On a Level 2 it is usually longer than buyers expect, and everything on it is a risk you have accepted without realising.

3. The condition ratings, and the one people misread

All levels use the same traffic-light system for each element of the building.

  • Condition rating 1. No repair currently needed. Normal maintenance must continue.
  • Condition rating 2. Defects that need repairing or replacing, but that are not serious or urgent.
  • Condition rating 3. Defects that are serious, or that need repairing, replacing or investigating urgently.
  • NI. Not inspected.

The rating people misread is NI, because it looks like an absence of a problem and is in fact an absence of information. A roof rated NI is not a roof that is fine. It is a roof nobody looked at.

The second most misread is a condition rating 2 in quantity. One or two are normal in any house over thirty years old. Eleven of them, spread across the roof, the rainwater goods, the pointing and the windows, is not eleven small problems. It is a house that has not been maintained for a decade, and the cumulative bill behaves very differently from the individual lines.

Worked example: the Pargeter household, Shrewsbury

Buying an 1890s end-terrace at £285,000. The estate agent suggested a Level 2 at £520. The Level 3 quote was £890, a gap of £370.

The house had been extended twice, had a flat roof over the rear addition, and had been rented for nine years. Three of the four Level 3 triggers in one property.

What the Level 3 found. A flat roof at the end of its life, described with an estimated two to four years remaining rather than simply rated 3. Historic movement at the rear corner, explained as long-settled and not requiring action, which is the finding that would have frightened them into an unnecessary structural engineer's fee if it had appeared as a bare rating. And a cast iron soil stack corroded at the joint, which the surveyor reached by asking to move a wheelie bin.

The numbers. The flat roof was renegotiated at £4,200 off the price. The movement finding saved an engineer's report at roughly £600. The soil stack was £450 they budgeted for rather than discovered in February.

Return on the £370 upgrade: about £5,250, or roughly fourteen times the extra fee. That will not happen on every house. It only has to happen on one.

5. What to do with the report once it arrives

Most buyers read a survey once, feel briefly alarmed, and file it. That is a waste of several hundred pounds, because the report is a working document and it has three uses.

Use one, the phone call. Ring the surveyor. It is included, almost nobody does it, and a ten-minute conversation will tell you which of the twelve findings actually matters. Surveyors write defensively because they carry professional liability; on the phone they will usually tell you plainly what they would do if it were their house.

Use two, the quotes. Take the condition rating 3 items and get written builder quotes before exchange. A seller will argue with a surveyor's adjective and will not argue with two priced quotations for the same work. This is how survey findings turn into a price reduction rather than a worry.

Use three, the plan. The rating 2 items are your maintenance schedule for the next five years. Put them in a list with rough costs and dates and you have turned an alarming document into a budget, which is a considerably better way to own a house than discovering the same items one emergency at a time.

6. The honest verdict

Choose the level by the house, not by the price. Pre-1930, previously extended or altered, a flat roof anywhere, an ex-rental, or a property you intend to change: Level 3, every time.

A conventional house built after about 1960, unaltered, in visible good order, and you are not planning works: Level 2 is proportionate and sensible.

Whichever you buy, read the "not inspected" list first and ring the surveyor about anything on it that matters. That phone call is free, and it is the single highest-value thing you can do with a survey report.

Frequently asked

Is a lender's valuation a survey? No. It is a brief assessment carried out for the lender to confirm the property is adequate security for the loan. It carries no duty to you and it is not designed to find defects.

Can I negotiate on the price after a survey? You can, and survey findings are the most common reason a price is renegotiated in England and Wales. Bring quotes rather than estimates: a seller will argue with a surveyor's opinion and struggle to argue with two builders' written prices.

Does a survey cover the drains? Not beyond what is visible. Lifting a manhole cover may happen at Level 3 if it is safe and accessible, but a camera survey of the drain runs is a separate specialist job at a separate fee.

What about new build homes? A new build normally comes with a ten-year structural warranty, and a snagging inspection is the more useful purchase. That is a different product again and is usually booked shortly before or after legal completion.
