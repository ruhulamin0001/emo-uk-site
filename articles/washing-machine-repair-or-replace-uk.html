A New Washing Machine Saves £26 A Year And Takes Fifteen Years To Pay For Itself. Repair The Old One

Every appliance shop will tell you a new A-rated machine will pay for itself in electricity. Work the sum with the actual consumption figures and the Ofgem cap and it does not, by a very wide margin. Which is useful, because it means you can ignore efficiency entirely and decide on the thing that does matter.

1. The energy argument, and why it collapses

Under the UK ecodesign and energy labelling rules, every washing machine sold has to publish its consumption on the eco 40-60 programme, per cycle, at the machine's rated capacity. That is the only figure on the label that is comparable between machines, and it is the one to use.

A current A-rated 8kg machine typically publishes something around 0.50 kWh a cycle. A machine from around 2016, before the label was rescaled, typically sits nearer 0.95 kWh for the equivalent wash. Call the gap 0.45 kWh a cycle.

The standard annual basis used in the labelling rules is 220 cycles a year, which is roughly four washes a week. Multiply: 220 times 0.45 is 99 kWh a year.

At the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, electricity is 26.32p a kWh. 99 times 26.32p is £26.06 a year.

Now divide a replacement machine by that. A decent mid-range 8kg machine is around £400. £400 divided by £26.06 is 15.3 years.

Nothing in a British utility room lasts 15.3 years except a Miele. The energy argument is dead on arrival, and anyone who tells you a new machine pays for itself in electricity has not done this sum.

3. The 50% rule, and the age condition people forget

The trade rule of thumb is to repair if the quote is under half the cost of an equivalent replacement. That is a good rule and it is incomplete, because it says nothing about how long the repaired machine will then last.

Add the age condition. Repair if the quote is under half the replacement cost AND the machine is under about eight years old. Typical UK lifespan runs seven to thirteen years, so at eight you are at least at the halfway point and probably past it.

The reason the age matters is that a repair does not reset the clock. A £180 pump on a twelve year old machine buys you the time until the next thing fails, which on a twelve year old machine might be next winter. On the table above that comes out at up to £235 a year of ownership, which is worse than any replacement option.

There is one exception worth knowing. Some faults are wear items that genuinely do reset a large part of the machine: bearings and drum, motor brushes, a new drain pump. Others are symptoms of a machine going generally: repeated electronics faults, a corroded drum spider, a leaking tub seam. Ask the engineer which kind yours is. A decent one will tell you honestly, because coming back to a machine that dies anyway costs them a reputation.

4. What a repair actually costs in 2026

UK washing machine repair pricing in 2026 breaks down roughly like this.

Call-out or diagnostic fee: £50 to £90. Most independents fold this into the final bill if you go ahead with the repair. Ask before booking whether they do.

Typical total repair: £80 to £200, covering the great majority of faults. A drain pump, a door seal, a heating element, a carbon brush set, a filter blockage all sit in here.

Expensive repairs: £250 to £400. Drum bearings on a sealed tub, a main control board, a motor. On many machines bearings mean a new outer tub, which is the single most common reason a repair quote crosses the 50% line.

A separate point that has changed recently. Appliance prices in the UK have risen appreciably over the last eighteen months, so the denominator in the 50% rule has moved. A repair that was uneconomic against a £320 machine two years ago may be sensible against a £400 one today. Price the actual replacement you would buy, not the one you remember.

And before you call anyone: check the filter, check the drain hose is not kinked, and check the machine is not simply out of balance on an uneven floor. A meaningful share of call-outs are one of those three, and you will pay £70 to be told so.

5. Worked example: Callum in Dunfermline

Callum's machine is seven years old and will not spin. The drum turns on wash but the load comes out soaking. He is quoted £135 including the call-out, for a carbon brush set and a labour hour.

An equivalent new 8kg A-rated machine is £399 delivered with the old one taken away.

The 50% test: £135 is 34 per cent of £399. Under half. Passes.

The age test: seven years, under eight. Passes.

The energy test, for completeness: his machine draws about 0.92 kWh a cycle, the new one 0.49. Over his 208 washes a year that is 89 kWh, or £23.53 a year. To recover £399 at £23.53 a year takes 17.0 years.

So he repairs. The brushes buy him an expected three or four more years, which puts his cost of ownership at about £90 a year including electricity, against about £79 a year for the new machine over eight years.

Those are close enough that it is not really a money decision, which is the honest conclusion. He repairs because £135 today beats £399 today, and because a machine that is not in a landfill is worth something.

Had the quote been £260 for bearings, it would have been 65 per cent of the replacement, the rule would have failed, and he would buy.

6. The honest verdict

Do not replace a working washing machine to save electricity. The saving is about £26 a year and the payback is fifteen years, which is longer than the machine will live. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling machines.

Repair if the quote is under half the price of the machine you would actually buy, and the machine is under about eight years old. Outside those two conditions, replace, and put the money into a machine with a long warranty rather than the cheapest box on the shelf. The cost per year of ownership barely differs, but the number of Saturdays you spend waiting in for an engineer does.

Frequently asked

Is an A-rated machine really that much better than a C? On the rescaled label, yes in relative terms and no in cash terms. The steps between A, B and C are typically 0.05 to 0.10 kWh a cycle, which at 220 cycles is £3 to £6 a year. It is not a reason to spend an extra £150.

Does washing at a lower temperature change this? Considerably, and it is free. Most of the electricity in a wash goes on heating water, so dropping from 40 to 30 degrees cuts the cycle energy by roughly a third on both old and new machines. That is a bigger lever than the machine itself.

Should I buy an extended warranty on a new machine? Price it as insurance. If it costs £60 a year and the average repair is £140 with maybe a one in five chance of needing one in a given year, the expected payout is under £30 against a £60 premium. Put the £60 in a jar instead unless you genuinely could not absorb a £200 bill.

Are spare parts still available for an older machine? Under the UK right to repair rules, manufacturers must make key parts available for a set period after a model stops being sold, and for washing machines that is a number of years. Ask the engineer to check part availability before you commit to a repair on anything over ten years old.
