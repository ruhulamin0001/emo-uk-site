Nobody at HMRC Ever Wrote Down a £1,000 Limit on Your Bike

The figure every Cycle to Work leaflet quotes does not come from the tax system at all. It comes from a consumer credit licence. Here is what the rules actually say, what the scheme is worth at each tax rate, and the ownership fee that decides whether it is worth joining.

The short version

  • HMRC does not set a limit on the value of a Cycle to Work bike. There is no cap in the tax rules at all. The £1,000 figure everyone quotes comes from the scheme provider’s consumer credit authorisation, not from the tax system.
  • Bicycles and cycling safety equipment are one of only five benefits that an employer does not have to value or report to HMRC when they are provided through salary sacrifice.
  • A basic rate taxpayer saves 28% of the sacrificed amount in income tax and National Insurance. A higher rate taxpayer saves 42%.
  • The end-of-scheme ownership fee decides whether the scheme is worth using. On a £2,000 e-bike a 7% fee leaves a basic rate taxpayer 21% better off. A 25% fee leaves them 3% better off, which is close to nothing.
  • Running cost is the part nobody prices. A 500Wh e-bike battery charged 150 times a year costs about £20 a year at the capped electricity rate of 26.32p per kWh.
  • Payback on a real commute is about two and a half years for a driver swapping 2,400 commuting miles a year for an e-bike, on the arithmetic below.

1. Where the £1,000 figure actually comes from

Ask anyone who has used Cycle to Work and they will tell you the limit is £1,000. Ask where that rule comes from and the answer stops.

It is not a tax rule. HMRC’s guidance on salary sacrifice sets no ceiling on the value of a bicycle provided this way. It sets a different condition entirely, which we come to next.

The £1,000 comes from consumer credit law. A Cycle to Work arrangement is, in legal terms, a hire agreement. Firms need Financial Conduct Authority authorisation to enter into regulated consumer hire agreements, and for years the standard route was a group consumer credit licence covering agreements up to £1,000. Providers working under that limit could not offer a more expensive bike, so £1,000 became the number printed on every leaflet.

Providers holding their own full FCA authorisation are not bound by it. Several now advertise bikes at £3,000 and above. Nothing changed in the tax rules to allow that. The tax rules never stopped it.

The limit on your bike is set by whoever runs your employer’s scheme, not by HMRC. If the answer matters to you, ask your employer which provider they use and what that provider’s ceiling is.

2. What the tax rules actually say

HMRC’s guidance for employers on salary sacrifice, last updated on 6 April 2026, makes two points that matter here.

The first is generous. Normally, tax exemptions on benefits in kind do not survive a salary sacrifice arrangement. There are five exceptions, and cycling is one: pension contributions, employer-provided pensions advice, workplace nurseries, certain pre-October-2018 childcare arrangements, and bicycles and cycling safety equipment, including cycle to work. For those five, the employer does not have to value or report the benefit.

The second is a hard constraint. A salary sacrifice arrangement must not reduce cash earnings below the National Minimum Wage, and HMRC requires employers to cap the deduction so the minimum is maintained.

That rule is the real limit for a lot of people, and it bites hardest on part-time staff. Take someone on the National Living Wage of £12.71 an hour:

  • Full time, 37.5 hours a week: gross pay is about £24,785 a year. Sacrificing £2,000 over twelve months leaves roughly £22,785, which is comfortably above the minimum for those hours. No problem.
  • Part time, 20 hours a week: gross pay is about £13,218 a year. Sacrificing £2,000 leaves £11,218, which works out at roughly £10.79 an hour. That is below the minimum wage, so the employer must refuse it or spread the sacrifice over a longer term.

So the honest answer to how expensive a bike you can get is: whatever your provider allows, capped by what your own pay can absorb without dropping below the legal minimum.

4. Running cost, which nobody prices

This series compares running cost rather than sticker price, so here is the running cost of an e-bike done properly.

A typical commuter e-bike carries a 500 watt hour battery, which is 0.5 kWh. Under the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 the capped electricity unit rate is 26.32p per kWh, VAT-free for that period. A full charge costs about 13.2p.

Charge it 150 times a year and the electricity bill is £19.74.

Two caveats. Charging losses mean the real draw is a little above battery capacity, so treat £20 as a floor. And the zero rate of VAT on electricity runs to 31 March 2027. If it is not extended, 5% VAT returns and the figure becomes roughly £21.

Against that, add a realistic maintenance allowance. Chains, brake pads, tyres and an annual service on a bike ridden 2,400 miles a year is perhaps £100 to £150. That is the honest running cost line, and it is still trivial next to a car.

Worked example: Farhana in Luton

Farhana commutes six miles each way, roughly 200 days a year. That is 2,400 miles. She currently drives a small petrol car and is a basic rate taxpayer.

What driving those miles costs. HMRC’s approved mileage rate of 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles is designed to cover fuel plus wear, tyres, servicing and a share of depreciation. Commuting is not business mileage, so she cannot claim it, but the rate is still a fair guide to what a mile costs to run. Taking a deliberately conservative 25p a mile for fuel and consumables alone, her commute costs about £600 a year.

What the e-bike costs to run. About £20 of electricity plus £130 of maintenance, so roughly £150 a year.

Annual saving: £600 minus £150 is £450 a year, and that ignores parking, which in her case is another £180. Including parking, £630.

Payback. Her employer’s scheme charges a 7% ownership fee, so her net cost is £1,580. Against the £630 she actually saves, the payback is £1,580 divided by £630, which is 2.5 years. Against the £450 saving excluding parking, it is 3.5 years.

Her bike should last a good deal longer than that, so on her numbers the scheme pays. If her employer used a provider charging 25% at the end, her net cost would be £1,940 and the payback 3.1 years including parking. Still worth it, but noticeably less so.

5. Four things that quietly go wrong

You leave before the term ends. Leave partway through and most schemes take the balance from your final net pay, so you lose the tax advantage on the unpaid portion. If a job move is plausible within a year, factor that in.

The sacrifice affects other things. Reducing gross pay can affect statutory maternity and sick pay, mortgage affordability, and in some schemes pension contributions calculated on actual rather than notional salary.

You buy for the scheme rather than for the riding. A £3,000 bike you use twice is not a 42% saving on anything. The cheapest route to a lower commuting cost is a bike you will actually get on in February.

You forget the lock. A stolen bike wipes out every year of saving in the table above at once.

6. Frequently asked

Can I really get a £3,000 bike on Cycle to Work? If your employer’s provider holds full FCA authorisation rather than working under the old group licence, and your pay can absorb the sacrifice without falling below the National Minimum Wage, then yes. Ask your employer who runs the scheme.

Do I own the bike at the end? Not automatically. The scheme is a hire agreement. Ownership transfers only when you pay the fee or sign an extended use agreement, and the cost of that transfer is the number that decides whether the whole thing was worth doing.

Does it work if I am self-employed? No. Salary sacrifice requires an employment contract to vary. A sole trader buys the bike and, if it is genuinely used for business, claims it through their own accounts instead.

Can I use it for leisure too? The exemption expects mainly qualifying journeys, meaning commuting and travel between workplaces. There is no requirement to log rides, and leisure use alongside commuting is fine.

7. The honest verdict

Cycle to Work is worth using if two things are true: your provider’s ownership fee is in single figures as a percentage, and you are replacing journeys you currently pay for. On those conditions a basic rate taxpayer is 21% better off on a £2,000 e-bike and a higher rate taxpayer 35%, with a payback of around two and a half to three years on a real commute.

It is not worth using if your scheme charges 25% at the end and you are a basic rate taxpayer. A 3% saving is not worth locking your pay into a twelve month agreement for. Buy the bike outright, keep the flexibility, and spend the difference on a better lock.

Whatever you decide, stop repeating the £1,000 figure as though HMRC wrote it. It came from a consumer credit licence, and plenty of schemes have long since moved past it.
