Three Members At £10 Beat Ten Members At £3, And It Is Not Close

Patreon's headline is a clean 10%. Underneath it sits a second layer of payment processing, currency conversion and payout fees that the pricing page mentions but never puts a number on. That second layer is per transaction, which is why the shape of your tiers decides your income more than the size of your audience does.

  • Then, in Patreon's own words, plus payment processing, currency conversion, and payout fees, and applicable taxes. The pricing page puts no figure on any of those.

1. What the 10% covers, and what it does not

Patreon's pricing page is refreshingly short. It says Patreon is free to start, that you only pay when you start earning, and that the rate is 10% of the income you earn on Patreon. For that you get monthly and annual subscriptions, membership tiers, one time payments, hosting for audio, images, video and livestreams, email newsletters, chats and DMs, exportable email lists, member analytics, free trials and discounts, and podcast hosting with RSS distribution and a Spotify integration.

What that 10% genuinely buys, and the reason it is not simply expensive, is a set of problems you would otherwise own yourself. Patreon states that it handles chargebacks, disputes and fraud so your payouts stay stable, and draws an explicit contrast with platforms that push that risk onto the creator. It says there is no need to set up Stripe or deal with tax paperwork, and that it takes care of most applicable tax collections and remittance. It supports more than 16 currencies for both pay ins and payouts.

For a UK creator, the tax line is the substantial one. Selling a digital membership to someone in Germany or Spain raises a VAT question that has nothing to do with your UK turnover. If Patreon is handling collection and remittance, that question is largely not yours. Compare that with running your own checkout, where it is entirely yours from the first sale.

This is the same trade we set out in our guide to what Substack pays a UK writer, and the two platforms sit on opposite sides of it. Substack's 10% leaves you holding your own Stripe account, your own chargebacks and your own card fees. Patreon's 10% does not. That is a real difference hiding behind an identical headline number.

2. The fee layer with no published number

Directly beneath the 10%, Patreon's pricing page adds one line: plus payment processing, currency conversion, and payout fees, and applicable taxes.

It does not say what any of those cost. Not a percentage, not a fixed amount, not a range. On a page whose entire purpose is pricing, that is worth noticing rather than skipping past, and it is the single most important thing to understand before you plan a Patreon around a target income.

Be clear about what is and is not being claimed here. This is not an accusation of a hidden charge. Patreon discloses that the layer exists, in plain words, on its own pricing page. The point is narrower and more useful: you cannot model your Patreon income from the pricing page alone, because the page gives you one of the two numbers you need.

Three things follow, and all three are worth acting on.

Treat 10% as your floor, never your expectation. Any plan that assumes you keep 90% is assuming the second layer is zero, and Patreon has told you it is not.

Find the real rate in your own account, not in an article. Once you have taken a handful of payments, your payout statements show exactly what came off. That figure is specific to your currency, your members' countries and your payout method, which is precisely why no article, including this one, can responsibly give you a number for it.

Assume the processing part is charged per transaction. Card processing almost universally is, and that assumption changes what you should do next more than the exact rate would.

One more consequence for a British creator. Currency conversion is named as a separate fee. If you are paid in pounds and a meaningful share of your members pay in dollars or euros, conversion is a recurring cost of having an international audience, not a one off. It does not mean you should turn away overseas members. It does mean an international membership is more expensive to run than a domestic one of the same size, which nobody mentions when they tell you to grow your audience.

3. Why tier design beats audience size

Here is the arithmetic that makes this article worth reading, and it holds whatever the processing rate turns out to be.

A percentage fee is neutral to price. Ten per cent of £3 and ten per cent of £10 are the same proportion. A per transaction fee is not neutral to price at all. The same fixed charge is a large slice of £3 and a small slice of £10.

So take two creators with identical gross income of £600 a month.

Creator A has 200 members at £3. That is 200 transactions a month, and 2,400 a year.

Creator B has 60 members at £10. That is 60 transactions a month, and 720 a year.

Same revenue. Creator A pays more than three times as many fixed fees. If the fixed component is 20p, that is £480 a year against £144, a difference of £336 on identical income. If it is 30p, the gap is over £500.

And the fixed fee is only the measurable half. Creator A also has 200 relationships to service, 200 chances of a failed card each month, and 200 people who might cancel. Creator B has 60. Smaller memberships at higher prices are cheaper to run and less work, which is the opposite of what the growth advice implies.

Annual billing does the same thing again. A member paying £10 a month generates twelve transactions a year. The same member on an annual plan generates one. If you offer an annual option at two months free, you give up around 17% of that member's revenue and remove eleven fixed fees plus eleven chances for the payment to fail. Whether that trades well depends on your retention: if your average member stays five months, the annual plan is clearly better; if they stay three years, it is not.

The honest conclusion from all of this: do not launch a £1 or £2 tier. It looks generous and it is the one price point where the fee structure works hardest against you. If you want a cheap way in, make it a free tier with less content rather than a paid tier that barely clears its own processing.

4. Worked example: Owen in Plymouth, 120 members

Owen makes a local history podcast in Plymouth. After eighteen months he has 120 paying members, split 90 at £4 and 30 at £12.

Gross. 90 at £4 is £360 a month. 30 at £12 is £360 a month. Total £720 a month, £8,640 a year. Exactly half his income comes from a quarter of his members.

Patreon's 10%: £864 a year.

Transactions. 1,440 a year, of which 1,080 come from the £4 tier. If the fixed processing component were 20p, the £4 tier would carry £216 of it against £72 for the £12 tier. The tier producing half the revenue produces three quarters of the per transaction cost.

The decision in front of him. He is considering adding a £2 tier to bring in casual listeners. Take 40 new members at £2: that is £960 a year gross, another 480 transactions, and forty more people to look after. Against that, persuading just seven of his existing £4 members to move up to £12 adds £672 a year with no new transactions at all and nobody new to service.

The £2 tier is not obviously wrong. It might be how someone discovers the show before upgrading. But it should be judged as a marketing cost with a modest revenue tail, not as a revenue line, and that is not how most creators think about it.

Tax. £8,640 of gross membership income is far above the £1,000 trading allowance, so this goes on a Self Assessment return. Gross means the full £8,640, not what lands in his account after Patreon's cut, and the fees are then deducted as an expense. If the podcast sits alongside employment, the profit stacks on top of his salary for rate purposes. He is nowhere near the £90,000 VAT registration threshold.

The honest verdict

Patreon at 10% is fair value for what it removes from your life. Tax collection and remittance, chargebacks, disputes, fraud and the whole Stripe setup are real work and real risk, and a creator running their own checkout carries all of it.

But you cannot plan an income from the pricing page, because it gives you one number and then names three more fees without printing a rate for any of them. Treat 10% as the floor, find your true rate in your own payout statements after a few months, and rebuild your plan on that.

The decision that actually moves your income is tier design. Fixed per transaction costs mean 60 members at £10 keeps more than 200 members at £3 on identical revenue, and is a quarter of the work. Skip the £1 and £2 tiers, make the cheap option free, and put your effort into making the middle tier worth paying more for.

And do not treat an overseas audience as free growth. Currency conversion is a named, recurring fee, so international members cost more to keep than British ones. Worth having, worth knowing.

Frequently asked

What does Patreon actually charge? Its pricing page states 10% of the income you earn on Patreon, plus payment processing, currency conversion and payout fees, and applicable taxes. Patreon does not publish a figure for that second group on that page, so check your own payout statements for your real rate.

Is Patreon better than Substack for a UK creator? They both headline at 10%, but Patreon says it handles tax collection and remittance and absorbs chargebacks and fraud, while a Substack publication runs on your own connected Stripe account. If you dislike administration, that difference matters more than the percentages.

Do I pay UK tax on Patreon income? If your gross trading income across all side activities exceeds £1,000 in a tax year you must tell HMRC, normally through Self Assessment. Gross is measured before Patreon's fees, and the fees are then deductible as an expense.

Should I offer an annual membership? It removes eleven transactions and eleven chances of a failed payment per member per year, in exchange for whatever discount you give. If you do not know your average membership length, annual is the safer option, because the discount is certain and long retention is not.
