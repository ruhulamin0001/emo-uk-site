Your Bank Is About to Start Telling You What People Like You Do With Their Money

Targeted support went live on 6 April 2026. It lets authorised firms make specific suggestions to groups of customers who look alike, without the cost of full advice. The FCA is unusually blunt that this is not a personal recommendation, and the difference is the whole story.

The short version

  • Targeted support went live on 6 April 2026. The rules are in FCA policy statement PS25/22, and the authorisation gateway for firms opened on 2 March 2026.
  • The FCA expects at least 18 million people to be offered extra help over the next decade.
  • It is not advice. In the FCA's words, recommendations will not be based on a full, in-depth individual assessment.
  • It works by grouping. Firms suggest what is suitable for a set of consumers who share needs or characteristics, then place you in one of those sets.

  • Only firms authorised for it can offer it. Anything similar from an unauthorised source is a different thing entirely.

1. What targeted support actually is

Until this year there was a cliff edge in UK financial services. On one side, guidance: general information that could not tell you what to do. On the other, regulated advice: a personal recommendation based on your full circumstances, which costs money and which fewer than one in ten people ever buy.

Targeted support sits between them. An authorised firm can put you into a group of consumers who share similar needs, objectives or characteristics, and then suggest what that group should consider doing. Your pension provider can say something like: people your age, with your pot size, in your situation, typically do this.

The timeline, from the FCA's own material: the policy statement PS25/22 was published in December 2025, the FCA Board made the final rules on 26 February 2026, the authorisation gateway for firms opened on 2 March 2026, and the regime went live on 6 April 2026.

The whole thing is underpinned by the Consumer Duty. Firms must make sure the suggestions are suitable for the group, and the FCA says they should only be offered where doing so puts consumers in a better position.

A suggestion for people like you is not the same as a suggestion for you. Everything useful and everything risky about this reform sits in that gap.

2. The four things it is not

It is not a personal recommendation. The FCA states plainly that consumers will receive recommendations, but they will not be based on a full, in-depth individual assessment. Nobody is checking your debts, your partner's pension, your health or your actual plans.

It is not free of commercial interest. The firm making the suggestion usually sells the products. That is not a scandal, it is the design, but it is a reason to treat a suggestion as a starting point rather than a verdict.

It is not the same as advice for the purposes of what happens when it goes wrong. The FCA published a joint statement with the Financial Ombudsman Service specifically to clarify the approach to consumer complaints and redress under this regime. The fact that a joint statement was needed tells you the position is different from full advice.

It is not something every firm can do. A firm needs permission through the authorisation gateway. If something that looks like targeted support arrives from a source you cannot identify, check the firm on the Financial Services Register first.

3. Why the FCA did it

The numbers behind the reform are worth reading because they describe most people you know.

  • Around 7 million UK adults hold £10,000 or more in cash savings and could be missing out on the benefits of investing over their lives.
  • Over half, 54%, of unadvised people with £10,000 in cash say they have not really thought about investing, or had not thought about it at all.
  • Fewer than 1 in 10 obtain regulated financial advice, while nearly 1 in 5 investors turn to social media for research or to keep up to date.
  • 75% of defined contribution pension holders aged 45 or over do not have a clear plan for how to take their money, and only 22% say they have a good understanding of their access options.

Sarah Pritchard, the FCA's deputy chief executive, called it gamechanging and said the regulator hopes it builds confidence to invest, while adding that investing will not be right for everyone. That last clause is the one to hold on to.

Worked example: Yvonne Beckett in Stockport

Yvonne is 52, works in local government, and has £14,000 sitting in her current account earning nothing. She also has an old defined contribution pot from a previous employer that she has not looked at in six years.

What she is losing on the cash alone: at an easy access rate of 3.75%, which is what NS&I's Direct Saver has paid since 18 August 2026, £14,000 would earn about £525 a year. In a current account paying nothing, that is £525 forgone annually for no reason at all.

What targeted support might now tell her: that people in her group typically move a cash buffer into an easy access account and consider investing the rest over a longer horizon. That is a useful nudge she was never going to get before.

What it will not tell her: whether she should be doing something about the old pension pot instead, whether her employer's scheme has a better home for the money, or whether she is on track for the State Pension. None of that is in the group assessment.

The sensible order: take the free, obvious win first. Moving idle cash to a paying account is £525 a year with no risk and no decision to regret. Then treat any investment suggestion as a question to research, not an instruction.

4. How to use it without getting burned

Ask which group you were put in and why. If a firm cannot tell you the characteristics that placed you in a segment, you cannot judge whether the segment fits.

Do the free things first. Idle cash earning nothing, an unclaimed employer pension match, and unused ISA allowance are wins that need no risk appetite and no product decision.

Check the firm on the Financial Services Register before acting on anything, and treat unsolicited approaches with more suspicion, not less, now that this kind of contact is legitimate for some firms.

For anything irreversible, pay for advice. Taking money out of a pension, transferring a defined benefit scheme, or anything with a tax consequence you cannot undo is exactly where a full individual assessment earns its fee.

Keep the paperwork. If a suggestion later turns out to have been unsuitable for your group, what the firm said and when is the whole of your case.

5. The honest verdict

The gap this fills is real. Fewer than one in ten people buy advice, and three quarters of over 45s with a defined contribution pension have no plan. Something between a leaflet and a £2,000 advice fee was overdue.

The risk is equally real and it is not fraud, it is misreading. A suggestion built for a group of people who look like you on three or four data points will be right on average and wrong for a decent minority. If your situation has anything unusual in it, a debt, an illness, a divorce, a second pot, you are in that minority.

Use it for the obvious wins, ignore it for the irreversible decisions, and remember that the firm making the suggestion also sells the product. That is not cynicism. It is just reading the label.

Frequently asked

Do I have to pay for targeted support? The regime is designed to be delivered at low cost to consumers, but charging is a commercial decision for each firm. Ask before you engage.

Can I complain if a suggestion turns out badly? You can complain to the firm and then to the Financial Ombudsman Service. The FCA and the Ombudsman published a joint statement on how complaints and redress work under this regime, and it is linked below.

Is this the same as robo-advice? No. Robo-advice is usually automated regulated advice with an individual assessment. Targeted support is explicitly not a full individual assessment.

Why is my provider suddenly emailing me about this? The FCA published a joint statement with the Information Commissioner's Office on how existing direct marketing rules apply here, so firms are working out what contact is permitted. You can still opt out of marketing.
