The Rooflight Costs £400. The Hole in Your Roof Costs the Rest

People shop for roof windows by comparing units, which is the wrong comparison, because the unit is a fifth of the job. The rest is a structural opening, a weatherproof flashing and a plastered reveal. Here is the bill itemised, and the heat loss priced properly at the October 2026 cap.

The short version

  • A fitted roof window commonly runs £1,200 to £2,500. The window itself is often £350 to £700 of that.
  • Approved Document L sets a limiting U-value of 2.2 W/m²K for rooflights in existing dwellings, measured in the horizontal plane, against 1.4 for replacement windows.
  • The extra heat loss from one roof window against insulated roof is roughly 74 kWh a year, which is about £5.93 a year at the October 2026 capped gas rate of 7.97p per kWh.

  • Fitting between existing rafters is the cheap route. The moment you cut a rafter you need a trimmer, and on a trussed roof you may need a structural engineer.

1. The install, priced line by line

Here is where the money goes on a straightforward roof window into a pitched tiled or slated roof, fitted from outside with a tower or light scaffold.

  • The window. A standard centre-pivot unit in a common size, typically £350 to £700. Top-hung, electric opening, solar blinds and triple glazing all push upward, and none of them change the structure.
  • Flashing kit. Sized to the roof covering, because slate, plain tile and profiled concrete tile each need a different one. £90 to £200, and getting the wrong one is the commonest cause of a leak.
  • Forming the opening. Stripping the covering back, cutting the felt and battens, trimming, and setting the frame square. Labour for two people for most of a day.
  • Structural trimming. Only if a rafter is cut. See the next section, because this is the line that moves.
  • Access. Tower or scaffold, from around £150 for a simple two-storey rear to several hundred for anything higher or awkward.
  • The internal reveal. Insulating, boarding, plastering and decorating the splayed opening inside. Frequently £300 to £600, and routinely left out of the first quote so that it appears later as an extra.
  • Making good outside. Replacing the tiles or slates around the flashing, and disposing of the broken ones.

Ask specifically whether the plastered and decorated reveal is included. That single question resolves most of the apparent price difference between quotes, because one firm has priced a finished room and the other has priced a hole with a window in it.

2. The one decision that doubles the price

Standard roof windows come in widths designed to fit between common rafter spacings. Choose one of those, position it in an existing gap, and no structure is disturbed. The job is a day, and the price sits at the bottom of the range.

Choose a wider unit, or insist on a position that does not line up with the rafters, and one or more rafters must be cut. That requires trimmers, which are timbers running across the opening to carry the loads from the cut members into the neighbouring rafters. On a traditional cut roof a competent carpenter does this routinely. On a trussed rafter roof, which is nearly every house built since the mid-1960s, cutting a truss member is a structural alteration and needs a designed solution, usually from a structural engineer, and sometimes steelwork.

The practical advice is short. Take the width that fits between your rafters unless there is a compelling reason not to. Measure the spacing in the loft before you go shopping, because it decides your choice more than any brochure will.

3. The heat loss, calculated at the October 2026 cap

This is the number that surprises people, and it is worth doing in full.

Figures use the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: gas at 7.97p per kWh including VAT at 5% and electricity at 26.32p per kWh with no VAT, because the zero rate on electricity runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027.

Approved Document L sets the limiting U-value for a rooflight in an existing dwelling at 2.2 W/m²K in the horizontal plane. An insulated pitched roof will be around 0.16 W/m²K. So putting a window into the roof replaces a very good insulator with a much worse one.

Take a common centre-pivot size of roughly 0.76 m² of glazed area. The difference in U-value is 2.04 W/m²K. Using an approximate UK heating season of 2,000 degree days:

2.04 multiplied by 0.76, multiplied by 2,000, multiplied by 24, divided by 1,000, gives roughly 74 kWh a year of extra heat demand.

At 7.97p per kWh, that is £5.93 a year.

Six pounds. Now compare two glazing specifications. Upgrading from a 1.4 unit to a 1.0 unit saves about 0.4 W/m²K, which on the same maths is roughly 14.6 kWh, or £1.16 a year. If that upgrade costs £250, the payback is over two hundred years.

The honest conclusion is that glazing specification on a single rooflight is not a money decision at all. Choose it for comfort, condensation resistance and noise, which are real and immediate, and stop pretending the energy saving is the reason.

Worked example: the Hardcastle household, Halifax

Converted attic room in a 1930s semi with a traditional cut roof, rafters at 400mm centres, one small gable window and a permanently gloomy interior.

The first quote, £2,750, was for a wide unit needing one rafter cut and trimmed, triple glazed, with the reveal priced separately at a further £480.

The second approach. Measured the rafter spacing, chose a 550mm wide unit that dropped straight into an existing gap, standard double glazing at a 1.4 U-value. Fitted, including tower, flashing kit for plain tile, and a plastered and painted reveal: £1,610.

The difference in daylight between the two units was noticeable but not transformative, since the room already had the gable window. The difference in price was £1,620.

The difference in running cost between the triple and the double glazed unit: about £1.16 a year. Over the twenty-year life of the window that is £23, against a several-hundred-pound premium.

They spent £400 of the saving on a blackout blind, which improved the room considerably more than either upgrade would have done.

5. The problem nobody warns you about, which is summer

Everything above concerns heat leaving the house. The complaint people actually make about roof windows concerns heat arriving.

A roof window faces the sky, and in a loft room the glazing is close to the occupants and close to the ceiling where hot air collects. South and west facing rooflights in an attic bedroom can make the room genuinely unusable on a July afternoon, and the effect is far more noticeable than the six pounds a year of winter heat loss.

Three remedies, in order of effectiveness. External awning blinds stop the solar gain before it passes through the glass and are dramatically more effective than anything fitted inside, typically cutting solar heat gain by around three quarters. Solar control glazing works permanently and without moving parts, at the cost of slightly reduced daylight. Internal blackout blinds control light beautifully and heat poorly, because by the time the energy has crossed the glass it is already in the room.

If you are specifying a roof window on a south or west slope, budget the external blind at the same time. Retrofitting one later means access equipment again, which is the expensive part of the whole exercise.

6. The honest verdict

Measure your rafter spacing before you look at a single brochure, and buy the widest unit that fits between them. That one constraint keeps the job at the cheap end and out of the structural engineer's diary.

Get the plastered reveal written into the quote. It is the most commonly omitted line and the one that turns a finished price into a surprise.

And do not buy glazing upgrades for the energy saving. Six pounds a year is the entire heat loss of the window, so the upgrade is worth having only if you want the comfort, the quiet or the reduced condensation, and it is worth nothing at all if you are chasing a payback.

Frequently asked

Do I need planning permission for a roof window? Usually not on a rear or side roof slope, under permitted development, provided it does not project more than 150mm beyond the plane of the roof and is not above the highest part of the roof. Front elevations, conservation areas, listed buildings and flats are the exceptions.

Does it need building regulations approval? Yes if it involves structural alteration such as cutting a rafter, and the glazing must meet the limiting U-value. A straightforward fit between existing rafters is still subject to the thermal and structural requirements, so the installer should confirm how it is being certified.

Will it leak? A correctly flashed unit in the right kit for the roof covering does not leak. The overwhelming majority of leaks trace to the wrong flashing kit, a unit fitted at too shallow a pitch for its specification, or an installer trying to make a generic kit work on a profile it was not made for.

Is a sun tunnel as good as a window? For daylight into a windowless space, it is excellent and much cheaper. It gives no view and no ventilation, so it is a substitute only when daylight is genuinely the whole requirement.
