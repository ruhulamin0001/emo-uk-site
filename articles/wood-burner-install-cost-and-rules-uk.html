A Wood Burner Is Cheap to Buy and Expensive to Make Legal

The stove in the showroom is perhaps a quarter of what you will spend, and the rules about what you may put in it have tightened twice since 2020. This is the installation priced line by line, and the fuel law that decides whether the thing is useful in your postcode at all.

The short version

  • A typical fitted installation runs £2,500 to £5,000, of which the stove itself is often under £1,200. The flue liner and the builder's work are the bulk.
  • The Air Quality (Domestic Solid Fuels Standards) (England) Regulations 2020, SI 2020/1095, restrict what can be sold. Wood in units under two cubic metres must be under 20% moisture and carry the Ready to Burn mark.
  • In a smoke control area you can only burn authorised fuel, or any fuel in an exempt appliance. Most of urban England is a smoke control area, and many people do not know theirs is.
  • A HETAS registered installer can self-certify to building control. Using a non-registered fitter means a building notice and a council inspection, which is slower and not free.
  • Running cost is a separate question and we have covered it elsewhere. This article is about what it costs to put one in lawfully.

1. The installation, priced line by line

The showroom price is the stove. Everything below is what turns a stove into a legal, working, insurable appliance, and it is worth seeing it itemised because quotes rarely are.

  • The stove. A competent 5kW Ecodesign-compliant unit sits in the mid hundreds to around £1,500. Above that you are buying brand, materials and looks rather than heat.
  • Flue liner. Stainless steel, 904 grade for wood or multi-fuel, sized to the stove and dropped down the existing chimney. Materials plus labour are commonly £800 to £1,600 depending on stack height and access.
  • Register plate or closure plate, and insulation. Seals the old opening around the liner so the chimney is not doing convection for free. A few hundred pounds.
  • Hearth. Building Regulations Approved Document J sets minimum dimensions and non-combustibility. A slate or granite hearth is typically £200 to £600.
  • Opening works. Enlarging a fireplace, forming a lintel, fitting a fireproof board surround and making good. This is the wild card, from nothing to well over a thousand pounds.
  • Air supply. Stoves rated above 5kW generally require a dedicated air vent. Coring an external wall is a few hundred pounds, and the vent is a permanent draught, which is worth knowing before you choose a bigger stove than you need.
  • Carbon monoxide alarm. Required in the room. Tens of pounds and non-negotiable.
  • Scaffold or tower access. Where the pot is not reachable safely, add several hundred.
  • Sign-off. Included in a HETAS installer's price. A separate building notice if not.

Add those up honestly on an average job and the total lands between £2,500 and £5,000. A quote materially below that range usually has no liner in it, and a chimney without a liner is the single most common reason a stove installation fails, smokes back, or invalidates a house insurance policy.

2. Why the flue liner is the number that moves

Old chimneys were built for open fires, which push a large volume of hot, dilute gas up a wide flue. A modern stove is far more efficient, which means the gas leaving it is cooler and much smaller in volume. Put that into an unlined Victorian stack and it cools further as it climbs, condenses on the brickwork, and deposits acidic tar. The results are a poor draw, staining through into bedrooms, and a genuine chimney fire risk.

So the liner is not an upsell, it is the thing that makes the installation work. What varies is the price, and three factors drive it: stack height, the number of bends, and access to the pot. A two-storey terrace with a straight run and ladder access is the cheap end. A three-storey Victorian with two offsets and no safe roof access needs scaffold before anyone starts.

Ask any quote to state the liner grade and length. 904 grade is the correct choice for wood and multi-fuel and carries a longer guarantee than 316. That one question separates a professional quote from an optimistic one.

3. What you are allowed to burn, and where

Two separate sets of rules apply, and people conflate them constantly.

What can be sold. SI 2020/1095 restricts the retail sale of solid fuels in England. Wood sold in units under two cubic metres must have a moisture content of 20% or less and carry the Ready to Burn certification mark. Traditional bituminous house coal has been phased out of retail sale, and manufactured solid fuels face sulphur and smoke emission limits. Selling wet wood in small volumes is the offence; burning wood you seasoned yourself is not, though seasoning below 20% takes most homeowners far longer than they expect.

What can be burned where. Smoke control areas are a separate and older regime, administered by local authorities. Inside one, you may burn only authorised fuels in an ordinary appliance, or any suitable fuel in an exempt appliance that DEFRA has approved. Most modern Ecodesign stoves are exempt appliances, which is the practical reason to buy one, but you must confirm the specific model is on the list rather than assuming.

Check your own address on your council's website before you buy anything. A surprising number of suburban and even semi-rural addresses sit inside a smoke control area, and finding out afterwards is an expensive way to learn.

Worked example: the Kerrigan household, Kendal

1930s semi, blocked-up fireplace in the front room, existing brick stack in sound condition. Not in a smoke control area, which they checked first.

The quote, itemised on request. Stove, 5kW Ecodesign, £1,050. 904 grade liner, 8 metres, fitted with scaffold, £1,480. Opening out, lintel and fireproof board, £620. Slate hearth, £340. Register plate and insulation, £180. Carbon monoxide alarm and commissioning, £60. HETAS certificate included. Total £3,730.

The 5kW decision saved them twice. Under the 5kW threshold no dedicated air vent was needed, which removed a core drill through the front elevation at about £300 and, more usefully, removed a permanent hole in the wall of the room they were trying to keep warm.

What they did not do. A neighbour had been quoted £1,900 with no liner. The saving was £1,830 on paper. Their chimney sweep found tar staining on the bedroom chimney breast within two winters, and the eventual liner cost more than it would have done at the start, because the stove had to come out first.

5. Why almost everyone buys a stove that is too big

Showrooms sell heat output, and heat output is the specification most likely to be wrong on the ticket you buy.

The rough working rule used across the trade is to take the room volume in cubic metres and divide by fourteen for a reasonably insulated room. A 4m by 4m room with a 2.4m ceiling is 38.4 cubic metres, which comes out at 2.7kW. Most people in that room buy a 5kW stove, and a good many buy 8kW.

An oversized stove has three costs. It is run throttled down to stop the room becoming unbearable, and a throttled stove burns dirty, tars the liner and fails to reach the temperatures its Ecodesign rating assumes. It pushes you over the 5kW threshold where a permanent air vent is required, which is a hole in the wall of the room you are heating. And it costs more to buy.

Under-sizing slightly is the safer error. A small stove run hot is efficient, clean and pleasant. A large stove run cool is none of those, and it is the usual explanation when someone says their stove smokes, blackens the glass, or needs sweeping twice as often as the neighbours.

6. The honest verdict

Buy the smallest stove that heats the room, use a HETAS registered installer, and insist on a 904 grade liner with the length and grade written into the quote. That combination is the whole of the advice.

Check your address for a smoke control area before you choose a model, and if you are in one, buy a DEFRA exempt appliance so you are not restricted to authorised fuels for the next twenty years.

And if the attraction is lower heating bills rather than the fire itself, read the running cost comparison first. The install cost above has to be recovered before a single pound is saved, and on a modern well-insulated house it frequently is not.

Frequently asked

Do I need planning permission for a wood burner? Normally no, because the works are internal and a flue terminal within the permitted limits is usually permitted development. Listed buildings and conservation areas are the exceptions and need checking before anything is ordered.

Can I fit a stove myself? Legally you can, but the work is notifiable under Building Regulations Part J. Without a registered installer you must submit a building notice to your local authority and have the work inspected, which costs money and time and is often refused sign-off if the detail is wrong.

Is a multi-fuel stove better than a wood-only one? It is more flexible, but with house coal withdrawn from retail sale the practical fuel choice narrows to wood and manufactured smokeless fuels. Multi-fuel grates also run wood slightly less efficiently than a dedicated wood stove.

How often does the chimney need sweeping? For wood, generally twice a year when in regular use, and at least annually otherwise. Many insurers require a sweep certificate, and the cost of the sweep is trivial against the cost of a chimney fire.
