HP Instant Ink Charges 9p a Page Whether You Print a Word or a Photo. The Plans, the Rollover Cap, and the Cartridges That Stop Working When You Cancel

Instant Ink turns printer ink from a thing you buy into a thing you rent, and HP's pitch is that you never run out. The pricing model underneath is simple and it is the whole story: you pay per page, not per drop. That makes it a bargain for one kind of household and a quietly expensive habit for another. Here are the UK plans as HP lists them, the rules that matter, and the arithmetic for both households.

1. The plans, and the price per page

HP's UK Instant Ink page lists five monthly plans, and the prices on 5 September 2026 were: 10 pages a month for £1.79, 50 pages for £4.49, 100 pages for £6.49, 300 pages for £13.49 and 700 pages for £28.99. HP labels them light, occasional, home, frequent and small business. The printer has to be an Instant Ink compatible HP model, connected to the internet, and enrolled through the HP app or website; the ink then arrives by post before the printer runs out, and you are billed monthly whether you print or not.

Divide the price by the pages and the per-page cost falls steeply as the plan grows: 17.9p a page on the 10 page plan, 9p on 50, 6.5p on 100, 4.5p on 300 and 4.1p on 700, on our arithmetic. The plan you pick is the cost you pay, and the temptation to pick the small one and stay under it is exactly what the overage pricing in section 3 is designed around.

HP runs promotional trials for new enrolments, typically a number of free months with a code, and the figure varies by month and printer. Those are real but they are introductory; the plan price is what you pay afterwards, and it is the plan price this article uses.

2. The rule that decides everything: a page is a page

Instant Ink counts pages, not ink. A page of black text with 5% coverage counts as one page. A full-page colour photograph that soaks the paper counts as one page. HP's own marketing puts it as the freedom to print in colour without worrying about the cost, and that is true, because the person printing the photo pays the same 9p as the person printing a list.

This is the single fact that decides whether the subscription is good value for you, and most reviews bury it. A page of text uses a small fraction of a cartridge, so the cartridge price spread over hundreds of text pages comes to a few pence a page. A colour photo can use dozens of times as much ink, so the same cartridge spread over photos costs tens of pence a page. Instant Ink flattens both to the plan rate. Heavy colour users win, light text users lose, and the printer does not care which you are.

The second consequence is that the printer must count. It reports page counts and ink levels to HP over the internet, which is how HP knows when to send ink and how to bill. A printer that is offline for an extended period cannot report, and HP's terms allow printing to be suspended until it reconnects. If your printer lives somewhere without reliable wifi, the subscription is a poor fit for that reason alone.

3. Rollover, overage and the cancel trap

Pages you do not use roll over into your account, up to three times your monthly plan allowance. HP's own example: on the 50 page plan you can bank up to 150 rollover pages. Beyond the cap the oldest pages are lost. So a light month is not wasted, but a run of light months eventually is, and a plan chosen for a busy December will be paying for pages that expire in March.

Print more than the plan and the rollover, and HP charges for additional sets. On the UK page the extra sets are 10 to 15 pages for £1.50, with the pages per set varying by plan. That is 10p to 15p a page, more than the 9p of the 50 page plan and more than double the 4.5p of the 300 page plan. A household that regularly goes over should be on the next plan up, and HP lets you change plan at any time, which is worth doing rather than buying sets.

Then the rule HP states plainly under the question of what happens when you cancel. Instant Ink cartridges will not work after the end of the current billing cycle in which you cancel, and HP tells you to make sure you have standard replacement cartridges ready to use. The ink physically in your printer is rented, not owned. Cancelling without a spare set of ordinary cartridges to hand means a printer that stops printing on the day the cycle ends, which for many people is the first time they understand what they signed up to. There is no cancellation fee and no contract; the cost is the cartridge set you have to buy to carry on.

4. How it compares with buying cartridges

The honest comparison depends on your printer's cartridges, their page yield and their price on the day, none of which is fixed, so we will not hand-type prices here. The method is what matters. Take the manufacturer's stated page yield for a black cartridge, which is measured at about 5% coverage, the industry standard for a page of text. Divide the cartridge price by the yield and you have your per-page cost for text. Do the same for the colour cartridge or the individual colours at their yields, and you have your per-page cost for colour at that same modest coverage; a photo uses far more and the true per-page cost rises accordingly.

Now set those against the plan you would need. If your text per-page cost from cartridges is lower than the plan rate, which for small home printers it very often is at 100 pages a month or fewer, Instant Ink is charging you for convenience. If your colour and photo per-page cost is higher than the plan rate, which it almost always is, Instant Ink is subsidising you. The break-even is not a page count. It is the mix of what you print.

Two further items on the cartridge side. Third-party compatible cartridges are cheaper still, and some HP printers refuse them with firmware updates, which is its own long story. And an ordinary cartridge that sits unused for months can dry out, so a household that prints twenty pages a year can end up paying for a new cartridge every time it prints, which is the one case where the 10 page plan at £21.48 a year can be the cheaper option.

5. A worked example in Aberdeen

Sandra in Aberdeen runs a small craft business from home and prints about 120 pages a month, roughly half of them colour labels and product photos. On Instant Ink she would need the 300 page plan at £13.49, because the 100 page plan would leave her buying extra sets most months. That is £161.88 a year on our arithmetic, for up to 3,600 pages and never touching a cartridge.

Her colour printing on ordinary cartridges is expensive per page, because photos and labels use far more ink than the yield figure assumes. When she worked out the previous year's cartridge spend from her bank statements it came to more than £161.88, and she had run out twice mid-order. For her the subscription is cheaper, and the running out problem, which cost her a rush delivery each time, goes away. The rollover cap is generous at her volume: 900 pages banked on the 300 plan.

Her neighbour Iain prints a handful of black text pages a week, perhaps 25 a month, and never in colour. The 50 page plan is £4.49 a month, £53.88 a year, which is 18p a page at his actual usage because he never uses the full 50. A single black cartridge for his printer, at the yield on the box, costs him a few pence a page and lasts him most of a year. For Iain the subscription is a bad deal at any plan, and the only thing it would buy him is not having to think about ink, at a premium of several times his real cost. He stays on cartridges, keeps one spare in the drawer, and prints his test page once a month so the heads do not dry.

6. The honest verdict

Instant Ink is per-page pricing: 4.1p to 17.9p depending on plan, and the same for a photo as for a line of text. That rule, not the page count, decides whether it pays.

Colour and photo households win, often by a lot. Light black-and-white households pay several times their real ink cost for convenience. Work out your mix before your volume.

Know the two traps before you enrol: rollover caps at three months of plan, and the cartridges in the printer stop working at the end of the cycle you cancel in. Keep a standard set in the drawer if you might leave.

Does a colour photo count as more than one page? No. Instant Ink counts pages, not ink. A full colour photo and a page of text each count as one page, which is why colour-heavy households get the best value and text-only households the worst.
