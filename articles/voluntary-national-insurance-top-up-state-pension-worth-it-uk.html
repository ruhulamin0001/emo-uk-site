One Missing National Insurance Year Costs £956.80 to Buy and Pays £358.50 a Year for Life. Who Should Buy, and Who Is Wasting Money

Buying back a gap in your National Insurance record is one of the few deals in personal finance where the government is on your side of the arithmetic. It is also one where thousands of people pay for years that add nothing. Here is what a year costs in 2026/27, what it adds, how quickly it pays back, and the check you must do before sending HMRC a penny.

1. What a year costs in 2026/27

Voluntary Class 3 National Insurance is the contribution anyone can pay to fill a gap in their record. The rate for the 2026 to 2027 tax year is £18.40 a week, which makes a full 52 week year £956.80. If you are paying for the previous two tax years you pay the rate that applied in those years, which is slightly less; for anything older than that you pay the current 2026 to 2027 rate. So the cost of an old gap rises each April, which is one reason not to let a decision drift.

Self-employed people have a cheaper route. Class 2 voluntary contributions are £3.65 a week, or £189.80 for a year, and count towards the State Pension in exactly the same way. If you were self-employed in the gap year, or you can register as such for it, Class 2 is the rate to ask HMRC about before you pay Class 3. The same £358.50 a year of pension for a fifth of the price is the single biggest saving in this whole subject, and it is routinely missed by people who were self-employed for a year or two and never thought of it.

A partial year can be topped up for a proportionate amount: if you have 30 weeks of contributions in a year, you buy the missing 22 weeks. HMRC's letter or your online record will state the exact figure.

2. What a year adds, and the one 35th rule

The full new State Pension is £241.30 a week in 2026 to 2027, and anyone whose National Insurance record started after April 2016 needs 35 qualifying years to get it. Each qualifying year is therefore worth one 35th of the full amount: £241.30 divided by 35 is £6.894 a week, which is £358.50 a year on our arithmetic. That is what one bought year adds, every year, for the rest of your life, and it rises each April with the triple lock along with the rest of the pension.

You need at least 10 qualifying years to get anything at all. Someone with 9 years and no prospect of more gets nothing; buying one year takes them to 10 and unlocks 10 35ths, about £3,585 a year. That is the extreme case, and it is real: it applies to people who spent most of their working life abroad or outside the system.

For people whose record started before April 2016 the picture is more complicated, because a starting amount was calculated in 2016 under both the old and new rules and the higher one was kept. If you were contracted out of the additional State Pension through a workplace scheme, your starting amount was reduced and you may need more than 35 years, or a bought year may add less than a 35th, or in some cases nothing. GOV.UK says so plainly. This is why the forecast check in section 4 is not optional.

3. The payback arithmetic

Take the clean case: a year that definitely adds a full 35th. You pay £956.80 once. You receive £358.50 a year from State Pension age. Divide one by the other and the year has paid for itself after 2.67 years, before tax. If your pension income is taxed at the basic rate of 20%, the net gain is £286.80 a year and the payback stretches to 3.34 years. Either way, anyone who lives more than about three and a half years past State Pension age is ahead, and the average is far longer than that.

Over 20 years of retirement the same £956.80 returns £7,170 gross on our arithmetic, and that ignores the annual uprating, which under the triple lock has never been below 2.5%. No savings account, bond or ISA gets near it, which is why every independent adviser and MoneySavingExpert have described it in the same terms for years. The catch is entirely in whether the year counts.

Class 2 changes the sums again. £189.80 for the same £358.50 a year pays back in about seven months. If you qualify for Class 2, there is no serious argument against it.

4. The forecast check that decides everything

Before paying anything, get your State Pension forecast on GOV.UK and look at three numbers: the qualifying years you have now, the years you are forecast to reach by State Pension age if you keep working, and the weekly amount you are on course for. Then look at your National Insurance record for the specific years showing as gaps.

The forecast answers the only question that matters. If it says you will reach the full £241.30 anyway, because you have enough working years ahead of you, a bought year adds nothing and HMRC will not refund it later. If it says you will fall short, each bought year lifts the forecast, and the record page shows exactly which years can be filled and for how much.

For anyone whose record started before 2016 or who was ever contracted out, GOV.UK's guidance is to contact the Future Pension Centre before paying, because the effect of a year is not always a straight 35th. The call is free and it is the single most useful ten minutes in this process. Money bought for the wrong year cannot be moved to a right one.

5. A worked example in Dundee

Gordon is 58, lives in Dundee, and his forecast shows 31 qualifying years with four gap years, 2020 to 2021 through 2023 to 2024, from a spell of low-paid part-time work during and after the pandemic. He plans to retire at 62 and stop paying National Insurance then. At 62 he will have 35 years if he keeps working until then, so the gaps do not matter. If he stops at 60, he will have 33 years and be two short.

He decides to retire at 60. He can buy two of the four gap years, but which two? All four are older than the previous two tax years, so each costs £956.80 at the 2026 to 2027 rate, and the oldest, 2020 to 2021, drops off the six year window on 5 April 2027. He buys the two oldest before they expire, for £1,913.60 in total. Each adds £358.50 a year, so his pension at 67 rises by £717 a year on our arithmetic, from £227.51 a week (33 35ths) to £241.30. Payback after 2.7 years; by 80 the two years have returned about £9,300 for £1,913.60.

His wife Morag, also 58, has 36 qualifying years from unbroken employment. She has three gap years from a period abroad in her twenties and receives the same HMRC letter. For her the answer is different: she is already over 35, and buying any of the three adds nothing. Same household, same letter, opposite decisions. The forecast, not the letter, is what told them apart.

6. Deadlines, Class 2 and free credits

You can normally only fill gaps for the past six years, and each year's deadline is 5 April. GOV.UK's own example: you have until 5 April 2032 to fill the 2025 to 2026 year. Working the other way, the 2020 to 2021 year can only be bought until 5 April 2027, and after that it is gone. The extended window that allowed people to go back to 2006 closed on 5 April 2025 and has not been reopened.

Before buying, check whether the gap should have been covered by free National Insurance credits. Years spent receiving Child Benefit for a child under 12, claiming Jobseeker's Allowance or Universal Credit, receiving Carer's Allowance, or on Statutory Sick Pay often carry credits automatically, and some, such as Child Benefit years where the claim was in a partner's name, can be transferred or claimed late. A credit costs nothing and does the same job as £956.80.

Payment is by bank transfer to HMRC using the 18 digit reference it gives you, and the record can take several weeks to update. Keep the confirmation. Once paid, Class 3 contributions are not refundable except in narrow circumstances, which is the final reason to do the forecast first.

7. The honest verdict

If your forecast shows you will fall short of 35 years, buy the missing ones. £956.80 for £358.50 a year for life pays back in under three years and beats anything a bank will offer.

If your forecast shows you will reach 35 years anyway, or you already have, do not. The year adds nothing and the money is not refundable. Same letter, opposite answer.

Ask about Class 2 if you were ever self-employed, and about credits before you pay for anything. £189.80 or nothing at all buys the same pension as £956.80.

Frequently asked

How much does a voluntary National Insurance year cost in 2026/27? Class 3 is £18.40 a week, or £956.80 for a full year. Class 2, for people who were self-employed in the gap year, is £3.65 a week, or £189.80.

How much State Pension does one qualifying year add? One 35th of the full new State Pension. At the 2026/27 rate of £241.30 a week that is £6.89 a week, about £358.50 a year, rising each April.

Is it ever a waste of money? Yes. If you already have 35 qualifying years, or will reach 35 by State Pension age through work or credits, an extra year adds nothing. People contracted out before 2016 may also gain less than a full 35th. Check the forecast and, if in doubt, call the Future Pension Centre.

How far back can I go? Six years, with a 5 April deadline for each. The 2020 to 2021 year expires on 5 April 2027. The temporary window back to 2006 closed on 5 April 2025.
