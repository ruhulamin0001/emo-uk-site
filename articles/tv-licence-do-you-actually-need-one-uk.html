You Do Not Need a TV Licence to Own a TV. Here Is What £180 a Year Actually Buys, and Who Can Legally Stop Paying

The licence fee went up to £180 on 1 April 2026, and every year the rise brings the same question: do I still need one? The answer depends on what you watch, not what you own. Here is the rule in plain terms, the list of things that are genuinely licence-free, what happens when you cancel, and the sums for a household that only streams.

1. The rule: it is about live TV and iPlayer, not the set

The law has not required a licence to own a television for a long time, and the official wording on GOV.UK is precise. You need a TV Licence if you watch or record live TV on any channel or service, or if you use BBC iPlayer. That is the whole test. The device does not matter: a smart TV, a laptop, a phone, a games console, a Fire TV stick or a Sky box are all the same in law. The channel does not matter either: live ITV, live Channel 4, a live international channel, or Sky News streamed live on YouTube all count.

Live means as it is being shown. TV Licensing's own guidance says this includes recording a programme to watch later, watching on a delay, +1 channels, repeats shown as live, and Red Button. It also includes live TV inside a streaming service: if Amazon Prime Video or YouTube carries a channel or an event as it happens, watching it needs a licence, even though those services are otherwise licence-free.

BBC iPlayer is the exception to the on-demand rule. Anything on iPlayer, live, catch-up or box set, needs a licence. That has been the case since 1 September 2016. The one carve-out TV Licensing lists is S4C programmes on demand, which do not need a licence.

One licence covers a household, meaning everyone living at the address under a joint tenancy or as a family. People in a shared house with separate tenancy agreements each need their own if they watch in their own room. Students in halls need one for their room, but a device running on its own battery, not plugged in and not connected to an aerial, is covered by the licence at their home address outside term time.

2. What is legally licence-free

GOV.UK lists what you do not need a licence for, and it is worth quoting because the list is where most of the saving lives. You do not need a TV Licence to watch streaming services like Netflix and Disney Plus, on-demand TV through services like All 4 (now Channel 4) and Amazon Prime Video, videos on websites like YouTube, or videos and DVDs.

Put concretely, a household that watches only these things is outside the rule: Netflix, Disney Plus, Apple TV Plus, Prime Video's library, Now's on-demand content, catch-up on ITVX, Channel 4 and My5 after the programme has aired, YouTube videos that are not a live broadcast, Blu-rays and DVDs, and games. It can own three televisions and a projector. It does not need a licence, and it can tell TV Licensing so.

The line is thin, though. ITVX carries live channels alongside catch-up, and watching one live episode of a soap on it is watching live TV. Channel 4's app streams its channels live. YouTube hosts live news channels. Now includes live Sky Sports. The same app can be licence-free one minute and in scope the next, and the responsibility for knowing which is yours.

3. What it costs, and why it rises every April

The colour licence is £180 a year from 1 April 2026, confirmed by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport on 6 February 2026. That is £5.50 more than the £174.50 charged from April 2025, or about 46p a month more. The black and white licence is £60.50. Businesses pay the same £180 per premises.

The rise is not discretionary. The 2022 licence fee settlement froze the fee at £159 for two years and then linked it to CPI inflation for the rest of the BBC's Charter period, which ends on 31 December 2027. So there is another inflation-linked rise due on 1 April 2027, and after that the funding model is whatever comes out of the Charter Review consultation the government is running. Nothing has been decided about what replaces the fee, and anyone telling you it is being scrapped is guessing.

Paying monthly by Direct Debit does not cost extra, but it does not save anything either: twelve payments add up to £180. The Simple Payment Plan, for people in financial difficulty, spreads the cost fortnightly or monthly with no premium. There is no cheaper licence for a second home, a caravan or a student room; each is a full licence unless it is covered by another one under the battery rule.

4. Cancelling: the refund, the declaration and the visit

If you genuinely stop watching live TV and stop using iPlayer, you can cancel. TV Licensing will refund any complete unused months left on the licence, and you can apply up to 14 days before the date you stop needing it. Refunds are processed within about 21 days by cheque or BACS. The condition is that you will not need the licence again before it would have expired, so cancelling for a summer and restarting in the autumn is not the deal on offer.

You then complete a No Licence Needed declaration, which stops the reminder letters. TV Licensing says openly that it may visit to confirm the declaration, and that when it does, it finds one in fourteen people who said they did not need a licence actually do, as at September 2025. Enquiry officers have no right of entry without a warrant, but if they establish that live TV is being watched, the offence carries a fine of up to £1,000 plus legal costs.

Do not cancel the Direct Debit and leave it there. TV Licensing's own guidance says cancelling the Direct Debit does not cancel the licence, and the address will keep receiving letters as if unlicensed. Cancel the licence itself, and let them cancel the Direct Debit.

5. A worked example in Swindon

Dev and Hannah live in Swindon with one child. They pay for Netflix and Disney Plus, have Prime Video through a Prime membership, and watch YouTube on the television. They have not switched on Freeview in two years. The one thing they do use is iPlayer, for a Sunday evening drama and the children's programmes on weekday mornings.

On our arithmetic the licence is £180 a year, or £15 a month. The Sunday drama runs for six weeks twice a year; the children's programmes are on most weekdays. Against that, CBeebies and CBBC content is only on iPlayer, so giving up the licence means giving up the children's viewing they actually use. They decide the £180 is buying something.

Their neighbour Marek is different. He lives alone, watches Netflix and YouTube, listens to radio, and has never opened iPlayer. He cancels in November with five complete months left on a licence that ran from April. TV Licensing refunds five twelfths of £180, which is £75 on our arithmetic, and he saves £180 a year from then on. He fills in the declaration. Eight months later he is offered a visit, confirms the position, and hears nothing more.

The difference between the two households is not the number of televisions or the size of the streaming bill. It is a single app. That is where the honest check sits: open your smart TV's recently used list and see whether iPlayer or a live channel is on it.

6. Who gets it free or half price

People aged 75 or over who receive Pension Credit are entitled to a free licence, and TV Licensing says you can apply from 74. Since August 2020 the age alone is not enough; Pension Credit is the test. If you or someone you live with is over 75 and on Pension Credit, apply rather than cancel: the free licence covers the household.

People who are blind or severely sight impaired pay half price, and the concession applies to the whole household if the licence is in the blind person's name. Residents of care homes and some sheltered accommodation can qualify for a £7.50 Accommodation for Residential Care licence through the home's administrator. There is no student discount, no low income discount and no discount for not watching the BBC.

7. The honest verdict

You need a licence for live TV on anything, and for BBC iPlayer for anything. You do not need one for owning a television, for Netflix, Disney Plus, Prime Video's library, catch-up on ITVX or Channel 4, or YouTube clips.

A household that can honestly say it never watches live and never opens iPlayer saves £180 a year, gets a refund for unused complete months, and should expect a visit at some point.

Most households cannot say that. One live match, one news bulletin, one iPlayer box set puts you in scope, and the fine is up to £1,000. Check your recently used apps before you decide, not after.

Frequently asked

Do I need a TV Licence for Netflix? No. Netflix, Disney Plus and other on-demand services are licence-free. You need a licence if you watch live TV on any service, or use BBC iPlayer.

Do I need a TV Licence to watch ITVX or Channel 4 catch-up? Not for catch-up programmes after they have aired. You do need one if you watch a channel live through those apps.

Can I get a refund if I cancel my TV Licence? Yes, for each complete month left on the licence, provided you will not need it again before it expires. Apply up to 14 days before you stop needing it. Refunds take about 21 days.

What is the fine for watching TV without a licence? Up to £1,000 plus costs on conviction in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and up to £2,000 in Guernsey.
