Your Broadband Deal Has Two Price Rises Already Written Into It. Here Is What It Actually Costs

Ofcom banned inflation-linked broadband price rises to protect households from uncertainty. It worked: the rises are now certain. It also, on the arithmetic, made them bigger for almost everyone. Here is the number nobody publishes, and the total you should be comparing instead of the headline.

The short version

  • Since 17 January 2025 providers cannot write inflation-linked price rises into new contracts. They must state any rise in pounds and pence, upfront, at the point of sale. That is an Ofcom rule, announced 19 July 2024.
  • The 2026 replacement rises were £2 to £4 a month on fixed broadband. BT, EE, Plusnet, Virgin Media and TalkTalk applied £4, Sky £3, Vodafone £3.50.
  • The number nobody publishes. A flat £4 rise only equals the banned CPI plus 3.9% formula at a monthly price of £57.97. At £3 the break-even is £43.48. Below those prices the new rule costs you more than the old one would have.
  • On our worked 24-month contract the "protection" costs £42.23 more than the banned formula, and the advertised price understates the real average monthly cost by 12.3%.
  • You cannot walk away. Because the rise is stated upfront, there is no penalty-free exit. That right only exists when a provider raises a price it did not disclose.
  • The rise is not where your money is. Out-of-contract customers pay £7 to £9 a month more than in-contract ones, and 28% of broadband customers are sitting there. That is up to £108 a year, and it dwarfs the £4.

1. What the pounds and pence rule actually changed

For years most large UK providers wrote an annual increase into your contract linked to a future inflation figure, usually CPI plus 3.9%, applied every March or April. Ofcom estimated that as of April 2024, around six in ten broadband and mobile customers were on contracts of that kind.

Ofcom's own research found people did not understand them. More than half of broadband customers, 55%, could not say what inflation measures like CPI and RPI actually measure. Among customers of providers using inflation-linked rises, only 16% were both aware there would be a rise and able to identify that it was inflation-linked with an extra percentage on top.

So Ofcom banned the practice. From 17 January 2025, providers cannot put inflation-linked or percentage-based rise terms into new contracts. Any rise must be set out in pounds and pence, prominently, before you sign, with the dates it applies.

That is a genuine improvement in one specific way and it should be said clearly: the future cost of your contract is now knowable on the day you sign it. Ofcom's own worked illustration shows exactly this, a price of £30.00 until a stated date, then £31.50, then £33.00, all printed next to the headline.

What it did not do is make the rises smaller. In 2026 the announced in-contract increases ran from £2 to £4 a month on fixed broadband, and £1.80 to £2.50 on mobile. BT, EE, Plusnet, Virgin Media and TalkTalk went to £4. Sky went to £3. Vodafone went to £3.50 for contracts taken from 12 November 2025.

A flat increase is the same cash for every customer. That means it is a far larger percentage for the cheapest deals, which are the ones bought by the households with the least room to absorb it.

2. The break-even price nobody publishes

Every guide to this subject lists the £3 and £4 figures. Almost none of them does the obvious comparison, which is to ask at what monthly price the flat rise would have been the same as the formula it replaced.

CPI for the twelve months to January 2026 was 3.0%, published by the ONS on 18 February 2026. January is the print providers historically used for a spring rise. Add the customary 3.9% and the old formula would have produced 6.9%.

Our arithmetic, and it is only division. A flat rise equals a 6.9% rise at the point where the flat amount is 6.9% of the monthly price.

£3.00 ÷ 0.069 = £43.48 a month
£3.50 ÷ 0.069 = £50.72 a month
£4.00 ÷ 0.069 = £57.97 a month

Below those prices, the flat pounds and pence rise takes more money off you than the inflation-linked term Ofcom banned would have done at January 2026 inflation.

Very few UK households pay £58 a month for broadband alone. Ofcom's own historical series put the average superfast bundle list price at £42.28 in 2023, and promoted full fibre deals routinely sit in the mid twenties. So for the ordinary customer, the rule that was introduced to protect them produced a larger cash increase than the practice it outlawed.

This is not a claim that Ofcom was wrong. Its stated objective was certainty and comparability, not a lower bill, and on certainty it delivered. But the trade was real, it was paid for by customers on cheaper tariffs, and it deserves to be stated with a number attached rather than left as a feeling.

3. Worked example: the Rahman household in St Albans

The Rahmans sign a 24-month full fibre contract in August 2026 at £27 a month. The contract discloses, as it must, a £4 rise on 31 March 2027 and another £4 on 31 March 2028.

What they actually pay.
August 2026 to March 2027, 8 months at £27 = £216.00
April 2027 to March 2028, 12 months at £31 = £372.00
April 2028 to July 2028, 4 months at £35 = £140.00
Total over 24 months: £728.00. True average: £30.33 a month.

The advertised price was £27. The real average is £3.33 a month higher, an understatement of 12.3%.

The same contract under the banned formula, at 6.9%: £27.00, then £28.86, then £30.85.
8 × £27.00 + 12 × £28.86 + 4 × £30.85 = £685.77.

The pounds and pence rule costs the Rahmans £42.23 over the contract.

Two honest caveats on that comparison. It assumes inflation stays near its January 2026 level for the whole term, which nobody can promise, and that is precisely the uncertainty Ofcom objected to. If CPI had spiked to 8%, the old formula would have been far worse. The point is not that flat rises are always dearer. It is that at the inflation rate we actually have, they are, and the gap has a number.

4. Comparing two deals: the rise matters less than you think

Once you know both rises are printed on the contract, the temptation is to shop on the size of the rise. That is mostly a mistake, and the arithmetic says so plainly.

Deal A: £25 a month, two £4 rises. 24 months from August = £680.00, an average of £28.33.
Deal B: £27 a month, two £3 rises. 24 months from August = £708.00, an average of £29.50.

Deal A wins by £28, despite having the bigger rises.

The reason is structural. Over a 24-month term signed in late summer, a £1 difference in the annual rise is worth about £20 in total, because the first rise only applies for the last 16 months and the second only for the last four. A £2 difference in the headline price is worth £48, because it applies to all 24. The headline price usually still dominates.

What genuinely changes the answer is when in the year you sign. Sign in February and the first rise lands after a single month, so you carry both rises for almost the whole term. Sign in April and you get a clear eleven months at the price you agreed. If your current deal ends in winter, it is worth asking whether a short extension into April is cheaper over the following two years than signing in February. Do that sum with your own two figures, not ours.

There is also a group of providers that sidesteps the question entirely. Zen Internet, Hyperoptic, Community Fibre, Cuckoo, Toob, Fibrus and Rebel Internet all advertise no mid-contract price rises for the minimum term. Most are regional or full fibre only networks, so availability at your postcode is the first thing to check, not the price.

5. Where the real money is, and the £12 tariff almost nobody claims

Everything above concerns £20 to £42 over two years. Worth knowing, not worth losing an evening over. Two other numbers are much larger.

Being out of contract. Ofcom's pricing research finds in-contract customers spend £7 to £9 a month less than out-of-contract customers. That is £84 to £108 a year, and Ofcom data indicates 28% of broadband customers are currently out of contract. If that is you, one phone call to your existing provider's retentions team is worth more than every price rise decision in this article combined. You do not even have to switch. The same research notes most out-of-contract customers could save simply by re-signing with the provider they already have.

Social tariffs. If anyone in the household receives Universal Credit, Pension Credit, Jobseeker's Allowance, Income Support or Employment and Support Allowance, you may be eligible for a social tariff. Hyperoptic's Fair Fibre Plan is £12 a month for 50Mbps, Virgin Media Essential is £12.50, BT Home Essentials is £15 for 36Mbps. Take-up reached 532,000 customers by June 2025, which Ofcom puts at just 8.6% of eligible households, against an eligible population it estimates at four to eight million. Around 70% of eligible households do not know these tariffs exist. Exact eligibility lists vary by provider, so check the provider's own page.

The honest verdict

The pounds and pence rule did what it said. You can now read your contract and know the total. Use that. Add up all 24 months and compare totals, not headline prices, because the headline understates the truth by roughly 12% on a typical deal.

Do not shop on the size of the rise. A £1 difference in the annual increase is worth about £20 over two years, while £2 off the monthly price is worth £48. The headline still wins most of the time.

And be clear about scale. If you are out of contract, that is £84 to £108 a year, and a fifteen minute call fixes it. If someone in the house is on a qualifying benefit, a social tariff at £12 to £15 a month may halve the bill. Both of those are worth more than every price rise argument in this article. Everything else here is worth knowing, but it is not where your money is.

Frequently asked

Can I leave without paying an exit fee when the price goes up? Almost certainly not. Ofcom's rules give you one month's notice and a penalty-free exit only when a provider raises a price that was not set out in your contract from the start. Because the pounds and pence rise is disclosed upfront, it is part of the deal you agreed, and the right to exit does not apply.

Are broadband prices actually rising, then? In real terms, no. Ofcom reported average broadband prices in the market fell 6% in real terms over the past year. What rises is the price on an individual in-contract customer's bill. Those two things are compatible, because new customers are being offered cheaper deals than existing ones. That is exactly why re-signing matters.

Does the rule cover mobile and pay TV too? Yes. The ban applies to phone, broadband and pay TV contracts. The 2026 mobile increases announced were smaller, in the £1.80 to £2.50 a month range.

What if my contract started before 17 January 2025? Older contracts can still carry the inflation-linked term, because the ban applies to new contracts from that date. Two regimes are running side by side. Check your own contract terms rather than assuming which one you are on.
