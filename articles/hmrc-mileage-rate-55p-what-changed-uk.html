The 45p Mileage Rate Died in June. Plenty of People Are Still Claiming It

For fifteen years the approved mileage rate sat at 45p and nobody had to think about it. On 5 June 2026 it moved to 55p, and because it moved mid tax year rather than in April, a lot of expense policies and a lot of self assessment habits have not caught up. If yours has not, the gap is real money.

The short version

  • 55p per business mile for the first 10,000 miles in a tax year, then 25p for every mile after that. Cars and vans.
  • For National Insurance the rate is 55p on all business miles, with no 10,000 mile step down. Tax and NI genuinely differ here.
  • Motorcycles 24p, bicycles 20p, for both tax and NI, on all business miles.
  • The change took effect on 5 June 2026, not 6 April, which is why so many policies missed it.
  • If your employer still pays 45p you are not stuck. You can claim tax relief on the 10p shortfall through Mileage Allowance Relief.
  • Relief is worth your tax rate, not the full shortfall. A 10p gap is worth 2p a mile to a basic rate taxpayer, 4p to a higher rate one.

1. The new rates in full

These are mileage allowance payments, which is the amount an employer can pay an employee for using their own vehicle on business without it becoming taxable pay. HMRC publishes them as approved amounts on its rates and thresholds page for employers.

Car or van. 55p for the first 10,000 business miles in the tax year, then 25p for each mile after, for tax. For National Insurance, 55p for all business miles.

Motorcycle. 24p for all business miles, both tax and NI.

Cycle. 20p for all business miles, both tax and NI.

HMRC's own update log on that page records the change precisely: on 5 June 2026 the mileage allowance payments for employees using their own car for business journeys increased from 45 pence to 55 pence per mile for the first 10,000 miles, and the rate also applies for National Insurance purposes.

The 45p figure it replaced had been unchanged since the 2011 to 2012 tax year. That is why this catches people out. A number that has not moved in fifteen years stops being one anyone checks, and it gets hard coded into expense forms and software defaults.

2. The 10,000 mile cliff, and the NI quirk

Two details do real work here and they are easy to miss.

The cliff. The 25p second tier did not move. So the gain from the change is concentrated in the first 10,000 miles and is capped at £1,000 a year in extra approved allowance, no matter how far you drive.

12,000 business miles, old rates. 10,000 at 45p is £4,500, plus 2,000 at 25p is £500. Approved amount £5,000.

12,000 business miles, new rates. 10,000 at 55p is £5,500, plus 2,000 at 25p is £500. Approved amount £6,000.

Difference: £1,000, and it would still be £1,000 at 20,000 miles.

The NI quirk. For tax the rate steps down to 25p after 10,000 miles. For National Insurance it does not: 55p applies to every business mile. On 14,000 miles, a flat 55p pays £7,700, the tax approved amount is £6,500, and the £1,200 difference is taxable but not NIable. That is not a payroll error, it is how the two rules are written.

3. What to do if your employer still pays 45p

An employer is not obliged to pay the approved rate. The approved rate is a ceiling for tax free payment, not a legal entitlement. Many employers will move to 55p, some will not, and some simply have not noticed yet.

If yours pays less than the approved amount, the shortfall is not lost. You can claim Mileage Allowance Relief on the difference between what you were paid and the approved amount. Be clear about what that is worth: you get tax relief on the shortfall, not the shortfall itself. A 10p per mile gap is worth 2p a mile to a basic rate taxpayer and 4p a mile to a higher rate taxpayer.

Three practical points:

  • Raise it at work first. Getting your employer to move to 55p is worth five times more than claiming relief on the gap.
  • Keep the mileage record. Date, journey, purpose, miles. A claim without a record is the one HMRC queries.
  • Mind the part year. The change landed on 5 June 2026, partway through the 2026 to 2027 tax year. Business miles driven before that date sit under the old approved amount. A claim that applies 55p to the whole year is overstated.

4. Worked example: Priya in Leicester

Priya, Leicester. Field based role, 9,000 business miles in her own car across the year, all of them after 5 June. Her employer pays 45p and has not updated the policy.

What she is paid. 9,000 at 45p is £4,050, tax free.

The approved amount. 9,000 at 55p is £4,950. Shortfall £900.

If she claims relief. As a basic rate taxpayer, relief on £900 at 20% is £180. As a higher rate taxpayer it would be £360.

If her employer moves to 55p instead. She receives the full £900 as an extra tax free payment. Which is why the first move is a conversation with whoever owns the expense policy, not a claim form.

5. Who this does not help

Being straight about the limits is the useful part.

  • Company car drivers. If the car is provided by your employer, this is not your rate. Yours is the advisory fuel rate, which is a different table, changes quarterly, and was last updated on 1 September 2026.
  • Commuting. Home to a permanent workplace is not a business journey and never has been. The rate change does nothing for it.
  • Self employed people using simplified expenses. The flat rate vehicle deduction for the self employed is a separate provision from employer mileage allowance payments, and the page quoted here is the employer one. Do not assume the two moved together. Check the simplified expenses guidance before changing what you claim.

The honest verdict

This is one of the rare changes that is unambiguously good for the person doing the driving, and it has been undersold because it happened in June rather than in a Budget. If you drive your own car for work, the number to have in your head is 55p, and the date to have in your head is 5 June 2026.

The realistic value is modest and capped. The most it adds to your approved amount is £1,000 a year, because the 25p tier did not move. But a lot of people will get none of it purely because a form still says 45p, and that is a fixable problem. Ask at work before you claim, because the full 10p in your pocket beats tax relief on it every time.

Frequently asked

Is my employer legally required to pay 55p? No. The approved rate is the maximum an employer can pay per business mile without it becoming taxable. It is not a minimum entitlement. If your employer pays less, the route open to you is Mileage Allowance Relief on the shortfall.

What if I drive more than 10,000 business miles? The first 10,000 miles are at 55p and everything after is at 25p for tax purposes. The 25p tier did not change, so the maximum extra approved allowance from this rise is £1,000 a year regardless of mileage. For National Insurance, 55p applies to every business mile.

I drove business miles in May 2026. Which rate applies? The increase took effect on 5 June 2026, so miles before that date fall under the previous approved amount of 45p for the first 10,000 miles. If you are making a claim covering the whole tax year, split it at that date rather than applying 55p throughout.
