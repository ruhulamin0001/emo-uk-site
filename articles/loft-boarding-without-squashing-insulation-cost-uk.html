Raised Loft Boarding Is Storage. It Is Not an Energy Improvement

The sales pitch for raised loft boarding is that it protects your insulation and saves energy. The first half is true. The second half does not survive contact with the Energy Saving Trust's own numbers, and once you see the division you cannot unsee it. That does not mean do not buy it. It means know what you are buying.

The short version

  • The Energy Saving Trust puts the top up saving at £17 a year. That is its published figure for going from 120mm to 270mm of loft insulation in Great Britain, and 120mm is roughly what a 1990s or 2000s house has.
  • Raised boarding costs £450 to £1,200 fitted. Divide one by the other and the payback is measured in decades, not years. On energy grounds alone the answer is no.
  • Squashing insulation is a real problem. The Energy Saving Trust says plainly that compressing mineral wool reduces its effectiveness. Boarding straight onto the joists does exactly that.
  • So the raised system is the right way to board a loft. It is just not an investment. It is the correct method for a thing you were going to do anyway.
  • The honest saving is the one you avoid losing. If you already have 270mm and you flatten it with chipboard, you go backwards. Raised boarding stops that. It does not create a gain.
  • Compare it to a loft ladder and a tidy, not to a heat pump. The competitor for your £750 is a storage unit, a shed, or throwing things away.

1. Why squashing insulation matters

Mineral wool does not insulate because of what it is made of. It insulates because of the air trapped in it. Squash it to half its thickness and you have not got half as much insulation, you have got considerably less than half, because you have destroyed the structure that was doing the work.

The Energy Saving Trust says it in one sentence on its loft insulation guidance: if you are using mineral wool, make sure you do not squash it, because this will reduce its effectiveness as an insulator.

Now look at a typical British loft. The joists are usually around 100mm deep. Current building regulations recommend a minimum of 270mm of mineral wool. So the recommended depth is nearly three times the depth of the timber you would normally screw boards to. If you lay chipboard flat on the joists, you are compressing 270mm of wool into 100mm of space across the whole boarded area.

Boarding straight onto the joists does not slightly reduce your insulation. Over the boarded area it largely cancels it.

A raised boarding system solves this with legs or a grid that lifts the boards above the insulation, leaving the wool at full loft and a ventilation gap above it. That is the right way to do it. Everything in this article that follows is about whether it is worth paying for, not about whether it works. It works.

Two rows in that table tell the whole story. The top up from 120mm to 270mm is worth £17 a year and costs £600. That is a payback of about thirty five years on insulation that lasts about forty. And raised boarding is not even in that trade, because boarding does not add insulation at all. It only preserves insulation you already have.

So the strongest energy claim you can honestly make for raised boarding is this: if you already have good insulation and you were going to board your loft anyway, the raised system avoids throwing away a saving you currently enjoy. That is a real and sensible thing. It is not a saving. It is the absence of a loss you were about to inflict on yourself.

One point of fairness to the Energy Saving Trust. Its £17 figure looks startlingly small and gets quoted with raised eyebrows, but it is the top up case, not the from scratch case. Going from an uninsulated loft to 270mm is a completely different proposition and is one of the best things you can do to a house. The confusion between those two cases is what most loft boarding marketing runs on.

3. What £750 buys elsewhere

If the goal is storage, board the loft. If the goal is a lower gas bill, £750 has better homes, and the comparison is not close.

  • £0 on the boiler flow temperature saves roughly £68 a year at October 2026 gas prices. See our guide to boiler flow temperature. It costs nothing and beats the insulation top up four to one.
  • £40 on draught proofing typically returns its cost inside two years. The numbers are in our draught proofing payback guide.
  • Cavity wall insulation, where the house has unfilled cavities, is in a different league again, and is covered in our cavity wall payback guide.
  • Floor insulation in a suspended timber floor house is another one where the arithmetic works, and it is set out in our floor insulation guide.

There is also a free route worth checking before you spend anything. Grant funded insulation exists and the ECO4 scheme closes on 31 December 2026, which our ECO4 guide covers. If you qualify, the top up you were going to pay £600 for may cost you nothing, which changes the arithmetic entirely.

4. If you are going to do it, do it properly

Assume you have decided you want the storage. Four things decide whether the job is good or bad, and none of them is the brand of board.

The boards must clear the insulation, with a gap. Raised legs or a grid, insulation at full depth underneath, and a small ventilation gap between the top of the wool and the underside of the board. The Energy Saving Trust is explicit that the gap is there to prevent condensation, not just to avoid compression.

Do not block the eaves. Air has to move through the loft. Insulation pushed hard into the eaves, or boards laid right to the edge, stops that airflow and is one of the commonest causes of a damp loft afterwards.

Fix the damp first if there is any. The Energy Saving Trust warns that insulating makes a loft colder, which makes condensation more likely and can worsen a problem that already exists. If there is any staining on the timbers, deal with that before anything else goes in.

Insulate the hatch and the pipes. A well insulated loft is a cold loft, and cold lofts freeze pipes and water tanks. Lagging and a jacketed tank are cheap, and our cylinder jacket and pipe lagging guide has the numbers. An insulated, draught sealed loft hatch is worth doing at the same time.

5. Worked example: the Okonkwo loft in Coventry

A 1998 three bedroom detached in Coventry. The loft has about 150mm of mineral wool, laid by the builder, and a growing pile of boxes balanced on two planks across the joists.

They get two quotes. One is £390 to lay chipboard straight onto the joists across about 18 square metres. The other is £840 for a raised system over the same area.

The cheap option. £390, and it compresses 150mm of wool into a 100mm joist depth across the whole 18 square metres. That is roughly a quarter of their loft area effectively de-insulated.

The raised option. £840, insulation untouched, ventilation gap maintained.

Energy difference between the two. The raised system preserves what they already have. Neither option creates a new saving.

The £450 difference between the quotes. This is the real decision, and it is not an energy decision. It is £450 to not make their house slightly worse.

What £840 would do instead on energy. Topping the loft up to 270mm would cost about £600 and save about £17 a year. Turning the boiler flow temperature down costs nothing and saves about £68 a year.

Their sensible answer, and the one most households land on: spend nothing on the boiler dial this weekend, take the raised boarding if they genuinely need 18 square metres of storage, and treat the £840 as the price of the storage, which is roughly four years of a self storage unit. Do not take the £390 option at all, because it is the only one of the three that actively costs them money every winter afterwards.

Frequently asked

Do loft boarding companies lie about the savings? Most do not lie, they blur two different cases. Preventing the loss of insulation performance is genuinely valuable and they are right to say so. Presenting that as an energy saving that pays back the boarding is where it stops being accurate. If a quote carries a specific annual saving figure for boarding, ask which insulation depths it is comparing, and whether it is the 0mm case or the top up case.

Is 270mm really necessary? It is the minimum recommendation for new builds under current building regulations, and it is the depth all the published savings figures are measured against. Going deeper than 270mm gives you very little extra.

Can I do it myself? Laying insulation, yes, if the loft is easy to reach, has no damp and is not a flat roof. The Energy Saving Trust says so directly. Raised boarding systems are also DIY friendly. What is not DIY is anything involving a flat roof, a lived in loft, or a loft with a damp problem.

What about spray foam on the underside of the roof? Treat any such offer with real caution. The Energy Saving Trust specifically warns against spraying foam onto a damaged or leaking roof without fixing the problem first, and spray foam has caused serious mortgage and resale difficulties for a lot of households. It is a very different product from the subject of this article.

The honest verdict

Buy raised loft boarding if you want loft storage and you can afford it. Do not buy it as an energy improvement, because it is not one, and any payback calculation offered to you on that basis will not survive the Energy Saving Trust's own £17 a year top up figure.

If you are boarding a loft, the raised system is the only correct way to do it. The cheap alternative of chipboard on the joists is worse than doing nothing, because it costs you money and heat every winter for the life of the house.

And if a lower bill is genuinely what you are after, spend nothing at all: go and turn your boiler's flow temperature down first, and check whether you qualify for grant funded insulation before the ECO4 scheme closes at the end of December.
