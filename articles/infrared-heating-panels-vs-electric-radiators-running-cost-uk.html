A 600 W Infrared Panel And A 600 W Oil-Filled Radiator Both Cost 15.8p An Hour. The Panel Is Not More Efficient. It Might Still Be The Better Buy, For One Reason.

Infrared heating is sold on efficiency, and the word is doing something dishonest. Every plug-in electric heater, panel, radiator, convector or fan, converts 100 per cent of the electricity it draws into heat, so at the same wattage they cost exactly the same to run. What differs is where the heat goes. A panel warms surfaces and people directly, so a room can feel comfortable at a lower air temperature, and that is the only mechanism by which it can ever save money. This article prices it.

The short version

  • At the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p per kWh, a 600 W heater of any kind costs 15.8p an hour flat out, £1.26 for an eight-hour day, about £189 for a 150-day winter. That is the same for an infrared panel, an oil-filled radiator, a convector or a fan heater at 600 W.
  • There is no efficient electric heater. Resistive heating is 100 per cent efficient at the point of use by definition; the label 'up to 60 per cent cheaper' on a panel compares it with running a different heater harder, not with the same watts.
  • Gas through a boiler costs about 8.9p per kWh of heat (7.97p per kWh of gas at 90 per cent efficiency), so any electric heater is about three times the price of central heating per unit of warmth. Panels do not change that ratio.
  • The one real advantage is radiant comfort. A panel warms you and the surfaces rather than the air, so a room can feel warm at 18 degrees air temperature instead of 21, and a thermostat set lower runs the panel for fewer hours. That is the mechanism, and it is worth something in a draughty or high-ceilinged room and nothing in a small sealed one.
  • Herschel's Select XLS range runs 300 W to 1,100 W, sized by the maker at 5 to 24 square metres, listed on its own site on 5 September 2026 from £238 to £539 with a five-year warranty. An oil-filled radiator of the same wattage is typically £40 to £90 with one or two years. The panel premium is £200 to £450 and it never pays back on electricity alone.
  • Buy a panel for wall space, silence, no dust and a room you use in short bursts. Buy a radiator for a room you heat all evening on a budget. Buy neither as the main heating for a home that has a gas boiler.

1. Why there is no efficient electric heater

A resistive electric heater passes current through an element that gets hot. All of the electrical energy becomes heat in the room; there is no exhaust, no flue, no losses to speak of. That is true of a £15 fan heater and a £539 infrared panel alike, and it is why the phrase energy efficient infrared heater is meaningless in the way it is usually meant. A 600 W panel draws 600 W and makes 600 W of heat. So does a 600 W oil-filled radiator.

At the Ofgem cap unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p per kWh for Direct Debit customers in Great Britain, 600 W for one hour is 0.6 kWh and costs 15.8p. Eight hours is £1.26. A 150-day heating season at eight hours a day, if the heater ran flat out the whole time, is £189. A 1,100 W panel is 29p an hour; a 2 kW fan heater is 52.6p. The shape of the heater does not appear anywhere in that sum.

The claims on panel packaging, typically that infrared is up to 50 or 60 per cent cheaper to run, come from comparing a panel on a thermostat set low with a convector heating the whole air volume of a room to a higher temperature. That comparison can be true. It is a comparison of how the two heaters are used, not of how efficiently they turn electricity into heat, and section 3 works out what it is actually worth. The heat pump is the only electric heating that produces more heat than the electricity it consumes, and that is a different article: our heat pump guide covers it.

3. Where the infrared saving actually comes from, and how big it can be

A convector or oil-filled radiator warms the air, the air warms you, and warm air rises to the ceiling and leaks out through every gap. A far-infrared panel emits radiation that warms the surfaces it faces, including your skin and clothes and the walls and floor, which then re-radiate. Two consequences follow. You can feel comfortable at a lower air temperature, typically two or three degrees lower, and the walls stay warmer and drier, which matters in a room with condensation.

The Energy Saving Trust puts one degree on a room thermostat at about £120 a year for a whole gas-heated house. Scale that to one electrically heated room and a thermostat set two to three degrees lower is worth perhaps 10 to 20 per cent fewer heating hours, so for a 600 W panel on five hours a day through winter, £118 a year, the saving against a radiator in the same room is about £20 to £35 a year. That is the whole infrared saving. It is real, it depends entirely on the user turning the thermostat down and sitting in the panel's line of sight, and it is what the payback column of the table is built on.

It is largest in rooms where air heating is wasteful: high ceilings, draughty older rooms, conservatories, a workshop or garage, or anywhere someone sits in one place, such as a desk. It is smallest in a small, sealed, well-insulated room, where the air warms in minutes anyway and stays warm. If your room is the second kind, the panel is a design choice, not a saving.

4. Wattage, sizing and the Herschel figures

Herschel's Select XLS product page, read on 5 September 2026, lists five panels: 300 W at 30 by 90 cm for 5 to 7 square metres, 400 W at 60 by 60 cm for 6 to 8, 600 W at 60 by 85 cm for 11 to 13, 800 W at 60 by 120 cm for 13 to 18, and 1,100 W at 60 by 155 cm for 15 to 24 square metres, with a five-year warranty and prices listed that day from £238 to £539. Those are the manufacturer's own sizing figures for a reasonably insulated room, and a manufacturer has an interest in the product; an older room with single glazing needs the next size up, and the maker's own wattage calculator will tell you so.

A panel that is too small runs flat out all evening and never lets the thermostat do the saving in section 3, so it costs the same as a radiator and heats the room worse. A panel that is right-sized cycles. That means the wattage is the number to get right, and the thermostat, either built in, a plug-in one or a wired one, is not optional. Wall or ceiling mounting matters too: a panel behind the sofa heats the sofa.

Against that, an oil-filled radiator of the same wattage costs a fifth of the price, carries a shorter warranty, takes floor space, is silent, and warms the air rather than the person. For a bedroom heated for an hour before sleep, or a room you heat all evening on a budget, it is the better buy. For a home office where one person sits at a desk for eight hours, a 400 W panel on the wall in front of them, set to hold 18 degrees, is where the infrared argument is strongest.

5. Worked example: Eleanor in Norwich

Works from home in a north-facing box room, 8 square metres, single-glazed sash window, gas central heating that she does not want to run for the whole house from 8 to 5

Eleanor has been running a 2 kW fan heater in bursts. She is looking at a 400 W Herschel panel on the wall behind her monitor, listed on the maker's site at £258 on 5 September 2026, against a 700 W oil-filled radiator at about £55.

The fan heater now. Roughly two hours of run time spread across the day at 2 kW: 4 kWh, £1.05 a day, about £158 over a 150-day winter, and the room is either hot or cold.

The oil-filled radiator. 700 W on a thermostat at 20 degrees, running perhaps 60 per cent of an eight-hour day: 3.4 kWh, 89p a day, £133 a winter.

The panel. 400 W directly in front of her, thermostat at 18 because the radiant heat lands on her, running perhaps 75 per cent of the day in a draughty room: 2.4 kWh, 63p a day, £95 a winter. Saving against the radiator £38 a year; against the fan heater £63.

The payback. Panel premium over the radiator £203, divided by £38, is 5.3 years; the panel has a five-year warranty. Against the fan heater she already owns, £258 divided by £63 is four years. Marginal, and it only works because she sits still in one place all day in front of it. She buys the panel, for the wall space and the silence as much as the sum, and draught-strips the sash, which on the draught proofing figures is the better investment of the two.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy an infrared panel because it is efficient. At the same wattage it costs exactly what an oil-filled radiator costs, 15.8p an hour at 600 W on the October 2026 cap rate, and both are about three times the price of gas per unit of heat. Buy it, if you buy it, because it warms a person or a surface directly and lets you hold the room two or three degrees cooler, which is worth £20 to £35 a year against a radiator in the same room and pays back the £200 to £450 premium in seven to fifteen years, or sooner in a home office where one person sits in front of it all day.

For a bedroom, a room heated all evening, or anyone on a budget, the £40 to £90 oil-filled radiator is the right buy. For a house with a gas boiler, neither is the main heating; a thermostatic valve on the radiator you already have is.

Frequently asked

Are infrared heating panels cheaper to run than electric radiators? Not per watt. Every resistive electric heater converts all of its electricity into heat, so a 600 W panel and a 600 W oil-filled radiator both cost 15.8p an hour at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p per kWh. A panel can cost less per winter only if its radiant heat lets you set the thermostat two or three degrees lower, which is worth roughly £20 to £35 a year in a typical room.

How much does an infrared panel cost to run per hour? Wattage divided by 1,000, times 26.32p. A 300 W panel is 7.9p an hour, 400 W is 10.5p, 600 W is 15.8p, 800 W is 21.1p and 1,100 W is 29p, all flat out. On a thermostat the panel cycles, so the real hourly cost is lower once the room is up to temperature.

Is infrared heating cheaper than gas central heating? No. Gas at the October 2026 cap is 7.97p per kWh, or about 8.9p per kWh of heat through a 90 per cent efficient boiler, against 26.32p for electricity. A panel costs about three times as much per unit of heat as the boiler. It only beats the boiler when heating one small room instead of the whole house, which is covered in our one-room heater article.

What size infrared panel do I need? Herschel's own sizing for its Select XLS range, read on 5 September 2026, is 300 W for 5 to 7 square metres, 400 W for 6 to 8, 600 W for 11 to 13, 800 W for 13 to 18 and 1,100 W for 15 to 24, for a reasonably insulated room. A draughty or single-glazed room needs the next size up. An undersized panel runs flat out and saves nothing.
