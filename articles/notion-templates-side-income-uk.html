1. What one template sale actually nets you

Notion does not sell your templates for you. You build the template in Notion and sell access to it somewhere else, and that somewhere else is what determines your economics. Most UK sellers end up on Gumroad, so start there.

Gumroad's published pricing is 10% plus 50 cents per transaction on direct sales, with no monthly fee, and 30% on sales that come through its Discover marketplace. That second number is the one people discover late. If a buyer finds you through Gumroad's own marketplace rather than through your link, your cut drops by a fifth of the sale price.

On a £15 template sold through your own link, the 10% is £1.50 and the fixed part is 50 cents, so call it roughly £1.90 all in, depending on the rate on the day. You net about £13.10. The fixed 50 cents is set in dollars, which matters more than it sounds: it does not move when you change your price, so it is 3.3% of a £15 template and 13% of a £3 one. Cheap templates are punished by the fixed fee far harder than the percentage suggests.

The same sale through Discover costs £4.50 in commission, netting about £10.50. Discover brings buyers you would not otherwise reach, so it is not simply worse, but you should know that a Discover sale is worth roughly 80% of a direct one before you decide how hard to push your own link.

Etsy is the other common route, and its published fees have a different shape: a 6.5% transaction fee, a listing fee of 0.20 US dollars charged when you list and again every four months until the item sells, payment processing that varies by country, and Offsite Ads. That last one has a detail worth reading before you sign up. Offsite Ads fees are 15% of an attributed order while your shop is under 10,000 US dollars of sales over the previous 365 days, and you can opt out at that stage. Once you cross 10,000 dollars, participation becomes compulsory and the rate drops to 12% for the lifetime of the shop, even if your sales fall back below the threshold afterwards. Etsy also prohibits moving a transaction off the platform to avoid its fees.

2. The two thresholds, and why they arrive in the wrong order

This is the part that generates the most panic and the least accurate advice.

Threshold one: being reported. Under rules that started on 1 January 2024, digital platforms collect your name, address, date of birth and National Insurance number, and report your income to HMRC. GOV.UK sets out the exclusion: your details will not be reported if you make fewer than 30 sales of goods in a calendar year and receive less than 2,000 euros, about £1,700, for those sales. Sell 30 templates at £15 and you are at £450 of income, nowhere near anything, and still reported.

Threshold two: owing tax. The trading allowance is £1,000. If your total income from all trading activity in the tax year is more than that, you need to tell HMRC. At £15 a template, £1,000 is 67 sales.

So the report arrives first, at roughly half the sales volume that triggers any obligation. That is why the sequence feels wrong. The platform also has to give you a copy of what it reported, showing total earnings for the calendar year less fees, broken into quarters.

The trading allowance is measured on gross income, not profit. This catches people out constantly. If you take £1,100 and Gumroad kept £140 of it, your income for the allowance test is £1,100, not £960. You are over.

The two thresholds also use different calendars. Platforms report the calendar year, 1 January to 31 December. HMRC wants your income for the tax year, 6 April to 5 April. GOV.UK tells you explicitly that if your platform shows calendar year figures, you have to work out the tax year figures yourself. Download your Gumroad sales data monthly and this takes ten minutes. Leave it to January and it is an evening.

One honest caveat on the 30 sale figure: GOV.UK words that exclusion around sales of goods. Whether a digital template counts as goods or as something else is not spelled out on that page, so treat 30 as the number to plan around rather than a guarantee, and keep your records either way.

And the reassurance GOV.UK gives in its own words: a platform reporting your details to HMRC does not automatically mean you owe tax. It means HMRC has a number it can compare to your return. If your return is right, nothing happens.

3. The platform choice that decides whether VAT is your problem

Almost every comparison of these platforms stops at the percentages. The percentages are the small difference. The large one is who is legally selling to your customer.

Gumroad states that it is the merchant of record and has been since 1 January 2025, and that it handles sales tax collection and remittance worldwide, at no extra cost, including VAT and GST on international sales. In practical terms that means when a buyer in Germany buys your template, Gumroad is the one with the VAT obligation, not you.

Etsy does something similar for digital items, and states it directly: where local law requires, Etsy collects and remits VAT to the relevant authorities when you sell a digital item delivered by automatic download. It displays the higher amount to the buyer and pays the VAT over itself.

Selling direct from your own site with your own payment processor is where this changes. Then you are the seller of record, and digital services sold to consumers in other countries follow their rules rather than yours. There is no UK style threshold protecting you on EU sales of digital services to consumers. The UK VAT registration threshold of £90,000 on a rolling twelve months governs your UK obligations, not your obligations elsewhere.

This is the honest reason a beginner should use a platform even though the percentage looks high. You are not paying 10% for a checkout page. You are paying 10% for somebody else to carry the international VAT problem. Once you are selling enough that 10% is a large number, that is the point to get proper advice about doing it yourself, not before.

This is general information rather than tax advice, and VAT on digital services is one of the areas where the detail genuinely matters. If you are near any threshold, speak to an accountant.

4. How many templates you actually need to sell

Work in units rather than in ambitions. At £15 a template sold direct on Gumroad, netting about £13.10:

  • 30 sales, £450 gross. You get reported. You owe nothing. Net in your pocket roughly £393.
  • 67 sales, £1,005 gross. You cross the trading allowance and have to tell HMRC. Net roughly £878.
  • 115 sales, £1,725 gross. You cross the 2,000 euro reporting figure as well.
  • 382 sales, £5,730 gross. Roughly £5,000 net, which is the level at which this stops being pocket money.

Now the part the income guides skip. Those are annual figures, and template sales are not evenly spread. A template sells in a burst when it is new or when something links to it, then trails. Sixty seven sales in a year is not five or six a month, it is thirty in launch month and two or three a month afterwards. Plan your tax position on the annual number and your motivation on the shape.

The lever that matters most is price, not volume, because the fixed 50 cents does not scale. Selling one template at £30 nets about £26.50. Selling two at £15 nets about £26.20, and takes twice as many buyers. If you can justify £30, the arithmetic strongly prefers it.

The second lever is bundling, for the same reason. A bundle is one transaction with one fixed fee, not four.

5. Worked example: Priya in Norwich, 140 sales in a year

Priya works in operations in Norwich and builds Notion systems for her own team. She packages three of them, prices them at £18, and sells through Gumroad with her own links plus Discover switched on.

The year. 140 sales, of which 95 come through her own link and 45 through Discover. Gross income: 140 at £18 is £2,520.

The fees. On the 95 direct sales, 10% of £1,710 is £171, plus 95 fixed fees of 50 cents, roughly £36. On the 45 Discover sales, 30% of £810 is £243, with the fixed fee included in that rate. Total fees roughly £450, so she nets about £2,070.

Note what Discover did. It was 32% of her sales but 54% of her fees. That is worth knowing, and it is still not an argument for turning it off, because those 45 buyers found her through Gumroad rather than through her.

What she has to do. Her gross trading income is £2,520, over the £1,000 trading allowance, so she has to tell HMRC. She registers for Self Assessment. Because she is over £1,000 she can choose between deducting the trading allowance, so tax on £2,520 minus £1,000, or deducting her actual allowable expenses, which here are the £450 of fees plus her Notion subscription. The allowance is larger, so she takes the allowance. You pick one, not both.

What she owes. As a basic rate taxpayer through her main job, the £1,520 she is taxed on attracts 20%, so £304, leaving her about £1,766 from the year. There may be Class 4 National Insurance depending on her profit level, which is worth checking on GOV.UK for the current year.

The bit that arrives in January. Gumroad reports her calendar year figures to HMRC and sends her a copy. Those figures will not match her tax return, because her return covers 6 April to 5 April. That mismatch is normal and expected. Keeping a monthly sales export is what turns it from an awkward conversation into a five minute reconciliation.

The honest verdict

Selling Notion templates is a real but small income for almost everyone who does it, and the honest version of the maths is that at £15 a template you need 67 sales in a tax year before HMRC is interested, and about 380 before it is worth structuring around.

The two thresholds are the thing to get straight. Thirty sales gets your details sent to HMRC. One thousand pounds of gross income creates an obligation. They are unrelated, the first arrives well before the second, and a platform reporting you does not mean you owe anything. Keep monthly records, because the platform counts calendar years and HMRC counts tax years.

On platforms, do not choose on percentage. Choose on who carries the VAT. Gumroad says it is merchant of record and handles sales tax worldwide; Etsy collects and remits VAT on digital downloads. Selling direct from your own checkout hands that problem to you, and for a side income it is not a problem worth owning.

And if you are pricing at £3 to shift volume, stop. The fixed fee eats 13% of a £3 sale and 3% of a £15 one. Fewer, better, dearer templates is not marketing advice here, it is arithmetic.

Frequently asked

Does Notion take a cut of template sales? Not on the sale itself. Notion is where the template lives; the money goes through whichever platform you sell on, and that is where the fees are. Budget for Notion as a subscription cost, not a commission.

Do I have to register as a business to sell templates? Not to start. If your gross trading income across all side activities goes over £1,000 in a tax year you need to tell HMRC, which usually means registering for Self Assessment. Under £1,000 the trading allowance generally covers you, though you should still keep records.

What if I already sold more than 30 templates and nobody told me about reporting? Nothing has gone wrong. The platform reports and sends you a copy; GOV.UK says being reported does not automatically mean you owe tax. If your income was over £1,000 gross for the tax year, tell HMRC. If it was not, keep the paperwork and carry on.

Is it better to sell on Gumroad or Etsy? For a UK seller starting out, the deciding factor is usually not the percentage but that both act on VAT for you, Gumroad as merchant of record and Etsy on digital auto downloads. Etsy brings its own search traffic and charges listing fees whether you sell or not. Gumroad charges nothing until you sell but sends you no buyers unless you use Discover at 30%.
