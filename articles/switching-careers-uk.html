One in Three UK Workers Wants Out. The Ones Who Go Do the Payback Sum First

Wanting a different job is close to universal. Actually moving is rare, and the reasons people give for staying are usually money and skills. Both are real. Both can be worked out on paper before you commit to anything.

1. Who actually changes, and when

33% of UK workers say they want to change career completely, and among 25 to 34 year olds that rises to 45%. The most common age to actually make the switch is 31, and for people moving into the trades it is typically 32 to 40.

Two barriers dominate. 29% say financial insecurity stops them and 69% believe they lack the necessary skills. There is also a belief problem: one in three UK workers over 30 thinks it is too late, which the ONS data contradicts, since career changes after 40 have been rising steadily, particularly into health, education, self employment and the trades.

Being 42 is not the obstacle. Not having done the arithmetic is.

2. The payback sum

Before any course, any application, any conversation, work out this one number.

Payback period = (cost of retraining + earnings lost while retraining) divided by the annual pay uplift.

ScenarioCostLost earningsAnnual upliftPayback
Evening course, keep working£1,200£0£4,000Under 4 months
Skills Bootcamp, keep working£0£0£3,000Immediate
Full time year of study£9,250£26,000£8,000About 4.4 years
Trade retraining, part time£6,000£2,000£5,000About 1.6 years
The often quoted bad case£15,000Not counted£5,0003 years, before opportunity cost

Two rules fall out of that table. Anything you can do while still earning has a dramatically shorter payback, because the lost earnings column is usually larger than the course fee. And a payback longer than about five years needs a reason beyond money, which is a perfectly good reason, but it should be a decision rather than a surprise.

The course fee is rarely the expensive part. The year you did not get paid is the expensive part, and most career change advice leaves it out of the sum entirely.

3. The skills gap that is smaller than it feels

69% of would be career changers say they lack the necessary skills. Meanwhile a 2025 CIPD study found 83% of UK employers now prioritise skills over formal qualifications. Those two findings sit awkwardly together, and the resolution is usually that people are comparing themselves to a job advert rather than to the person currently doing the job.

What actually transfers, and is undervalued by the person who has it:

  • Managing people. Transfers to every sector and is scarce everywhere.
  • Dealing with angry customers. Retail and hospitality staff have this. Most office workers do not.
  • Working to a hard deadline with consequences. Rarer than employers expect.
  • Regulated environments. Care, finance and aviation experience carries directly into other regulated sectors.
  • Spreadsheets and systems. Genuinely capable spreadsheet users are still uncommon.

The practical exercise: find five job adverts for the role you want, list every requirement, and mark each one as have it, could learn in three months, or genuinely need training. Most people find the third list is two or three items, not fifteen.

4. Free and cheap funding routes

Paying full price for retraining is often unnecessary, and this is the part most people do not check before they hand over a card.

  • Skills Bootcamps. Free courses in digital, technical and management skills, typically up to 16 weeks, with a guaranteed interview at the end. Free to the learner. This is the strongest option available and it is underused.
  • Advanced Learner Loan. Covers Level 3 to 6 qualifications, repaid income contingently like a student loan, so you repay nothing until your income passes the threshold.
  • Apprenticeships at any age. There is no upper age limit in England. If you are 21 or over you get the apprentice rate for the first year only, then the full National Living Wage of £12.71. You earn while you train and pay nothing for the training.
  • Your current employer. Many will fund training that is even loosely relevant, and most people never ask. The worst outcome is no.
  • Free foundational learning. The Open University's OpenLearn, and free tiers from the major software vendors, cover enough to establish whether you like the subject before you spend anything.

Check the funded routes before the paid ones. A bootcamp that costs nothing and a course that costs £6,000 often lead to the same first job.

5. Bridging instead of jumping

The mental image of career change is a leap: quit, retrain, emerge transformed. That version has the worst payback and the highest risk. The version that works is usually a bridge.

  1. Move sideways inside your current employer. Cheapest and fastest. You keep your salary, your pension and your service, and you are a known quantity rather than a stranger with no track record.
  2. Move to the target sector in your current role. An accountant moving into a tech company is one change, not two. Then move roles a year later from inside.
  3. Do the new work at the edges first. Evenings, weekends, one paying client. This tests whether you actually enjoy it, which is the failure mode nobody plans for: people retrain into work they discover they dislike.
  4. Take the pay cut with your eyes open, and time limit it. "I will accept £4,000 less for eighteen months" is a plan. "It will work out" is not.

6. A worked change, with the numbers

Michelle, 38, retail supervisor in Stoke on £24,500. Wants to move into IT support.

The naive plan she nearly took. A £7,500 twelve month full time course. Cost £7,500 plus roughly £24,500 of lost earnings, so £32,000. First IT support salary about £26,000, an uplift of £1,500. Payback: over twenty years. The maths kills it instantly, which is exactly what the maths is for.

What she did instead. A free Skills Bootcamp in IT support, part time over 14 weeks, while still working. Cost £0, lost earnings £0. Guaranteed interview at the end.

The bridge. Before finishing, she applied internally for a role supporting her retailer's till and stock systems. She got it at £25,800, a small uplift, but now with IT support on her CV and a company that already trusted her.

Eighteen months later. She moved to a dedicated IT support role at £31,000. Total spend on the change: £0. Total lost earnings: £0. Uplift: £6,500 a year.

The comparison that matters: the leap version cost £32,000 for a £1,500 uplift. The bridge version cost nothing for a £6,500 uplift and took eighteen months instead of twelve. The extra six months bought her the entire £32,000.

7. Traps in career change advice

  • Courses sold with salary figures from the top of the range. Ask what the median first job pays, not what is possible. If they will not say, that is the answer.
  • "Follow your passion" as a plan. Passion tells you the direction. It does not tell you whether the payback works, and plenty of enjoyable sectors pay badly for entry level work.
  • Ignoring the pension. Leaving employment mid year can interrupt contributions and employer matching. Check before you resign, not after.
  • Quitting before testing. Do the work at the edges first. The most expensive career change is the one you reverse in year two.
  • Treating age as the barrier. Career changes after 40 are rising in the UK. The barrier is almost always the payback sum, not the birth certificate.
  • Not asking your employer. Free training and an internal sideways move is the cheapest version of every plan in this article.

The honest verdict

Do the payback sum before anything else, and put lost earnings in it. That single calculation eliminates most bad career change plans in about ten minutes and tells you which version of your idea is affordable.

Then look for the funded route. Skills Bootcamps are free, apprenticeships pay you while you train at any age, and the Advanced Learner Loan is income contingent. Full price self funded retraining should be your last option, not your first.

And bridge rather than jump wherever you can. Sideways inside your employer, or same role in the target sector, keeps your income while you make the change. It feels less decisive and it works far more often.

Frequently asked

Am I too old to change career at 45? No. ONS data shows a sustained rise in career changes after 40, particularly into health, education, self employment and the trades. What changes with age is that the payback period matters more, since you have fewer working years to recover the cost.

Do I need a degree to change career? Often not. A 2025 CIPD study found 83% of UK employers prioritise skills over formal qualifications. Check five real adverts for the role you want before assuming a degree is required.

Should I tell my employer I am looking? Not usually, unless you are pursuing an internal move, in which case the conversation is the whole strategy.

What if I take a pay cut and regret it? Set the limit in advance: how much less, for how long, and what has to be true by then. A time limited cut is a decision. An open ended one becomes your new normal.
