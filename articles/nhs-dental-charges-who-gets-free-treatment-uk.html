The NHS Page That Tells You What a Dentist Costs Quotes Two Different Prices for the Same Treatment

Three bands, one urgent rate, and a two month rule that quietly makes a lot of follow-up appointments free. All of it is published clearly, except for one paragraph where the NHS is still using last year's numbers against its own current headings. If you have ever been quoted a figure that did not match what you read, this is probably why.

The short version

  • Band 1 is £27.90. Examination, X-rays if needed, fluoride, simple scaling if clinically needed, moulds, minor denture adjustments.
  • Band 2 is £76.60. Everything in Band 1 plus fillings, root canal, extractions, sealants and extensive gum disease management.
  • Band 3 is £332.10. Everything in Bands 1 and 2 plus crowns, dentures, bridges and orthodontics.
  • Urgent treatment is £27.90, the same as Band 1, and covers a surprising amount including draining an abscess and removing up to 2 teeth.

  • Some things are free for everyone: having stitches removed, having bleeding stopped, and denture repairs.
  • The NHS page contradicts itself in one paragraph, quoting Band 1 at £27.40 and Band 2 at £75.30 for gum treatment, against its own headings of £27.90 and £76.60.

1. The three bands, and what is in each

NHS dental treatment in England is priced in three bands. You pay the band, not the item, and the dental professional should tell you the cost before starting.

Band 1: £27.90. Examination, assessment and advice. X-rays if clinically needed. Fluoride applied to the teeth. Simple management of gum disease such as scaling, if clinically needed. Moulds of your teeth. Minimal adjustments to dentures or braces.

Band 2: £76.60. Everything in Band 1, plus fillings, root canal treatment, extractions and other oral surgery, sealants, additions and extensive adjustments to dentures, a bite-raising appliance that is not laboratory made, and extensive management of gum disease.

Band 3: £332.10. Everything in Bands 1 and 2, plus crowns, inlays and onlays, dentures in plastic or metal, bridges, orthodontic treatment such as braces, and other custom-made appliances not including sports guards.

Urgent treatment: £27.90.

The urgent band is the underrated one. For the same £27.90 as a check-up, urgent treatment covers examination, X-rays, temporary fillings, emergency partial root canal, management of a knocked-out tooth, refixing crowns and bridges, removing up to 2 teeth, draining an abscess and treating the infection, and one urgent filling. That is a substantial amount of dentistry at the lowest price on the list. You may need further treatment afterwards, which can cost more.

Two charging rules do a lot of work. If you need a mix of treatments from different bands, you pay only the highest band. And if you need a mix from the same band, you pay the band charge once. So three fillings in one course of treatment is £76.60, not three times £76.60.

2. The paragraph where the NHS disagrees with itself

Under the Band 1 heading there is a subsection on gum treatment and scaling. Its wording, on the page as it stands, is that scaling is "usually included in Band 1 (£27.40), but extensive treatment, or treatment for complex gum problems may be charged as a Band 2 (£75.30) treatment".

Those two figures do not match the headings a few centimetres above them, which say Band 1: £27.90 and Band 2: £76.60. The page itself records that it was modified on 1 April 2026, which is when dental charges are uprated each year.

Which is right? The headings. £27.40 and £75.30 are the previous year's Band 1 and Band 2 charges. What has happened is an annual price update that changed the headings and the tables but missed the numbers written inside a paragraph. It is an editing oversight rather than a second pricing regime.

The gap is 50p on Band 1 and £1.30 on Band 2, so nobody is going to be ruined by it. It is worth knowing for two reasons. If your practice quotes £27.90 for a scale and polish and you have read £27.40 online, the dentist is not overcharging you. And more generally: on a subject where the price changes every 1 April, the safest habit is to check the headline figure rather than a number embedded in a paragraph, on any site including this one.

3. Who pays nothing

In England you get free NHS dental appointments and treatment if:

  • you are aged under 18, or under 19 and in full-time education
  • you are pregnant or have had a baby in the last 12 months
  • you have had a stillbirth in the past 12 months
  • you are being treated in an NHS hospital by a hospital dentist, although you may still pay for dentures or bridges
  • you receive War Pension Scheme payments, or Armed Forces Compensation Scheme payments and the treatment is for your accepted disability

You also qualify if you or your partner receive certain benefits, including Income-related Employment and Support Allowance, Pension Credit Guarantee Credit, Pension Credit Guarantee Credit with Savings Credit, and Universal Credit, but only if your income is below a certain amount.

The part people miss: if you or your partner get one of those qualifying benefits, your dependants aged under 20 also get free dental treatment. That reaches 18 and 19 year olds who are not in full-time education and would otherwise pay.

If you do not qualify outright and you are on a low income, the NHS Low Income Scheme can pay part of the cost. You get an HC3 certificate showing how much you will pay, and you show it to the dentist. A full exemption on low income comes through an HC2 certificate.

Proof matters. Depending on the reason you will need to show something: an HC2 certificate, a birth certificate, a maternity exemption certificate, a MatB1, a notification of birth form or your baby's birth certificate, or a stillbirth certificate. War Pension and Armed Forces Compensation claimants usually have to pay first and claim back from Veterans UK.

And if you paid when you should not have, you may be able to get a refund. The NHS Business Services Authority publishes the route.

4. Worked example: Cerys in Chesterfield and the two month rule

Cerys goes for a check-up in October. The dentist finds she needs a scale and polish and, on a different tooth, a filling.

Naive expectation: £27.90 for the check-up, plus a scale, plus £76.60 for the filling. Three charges.

What actually happens: the check-up and the scaling are Band 1, the filling is Band 2, and where treatments mix across bands you pay the highest band only. So the whole course is £76.60. The examination and the scaling cost her nothing extra.

Three weeks later a second tooth needs a filling. This is further treatment within 2 months, and a filling is the same band as the treatment she already paid for. So it is free.

In December, five weeks after that, she chips a tooth and needs a crown. A crown is Band 3, which is a higher band, so she pays the difference in the sense that the higher band fee applies: £332.10.

What she has paid across three months: £76.60 + £0 + £332.10 = £408.70. Charged item by item rather than by band and by the two month rule, the same work would have come to considerably more.

The behaviour that saves money here is not shopping around. It is going back inside two months. If something is bothering you six weeks after a course of treatment, the visit is free where the further treatment is in the same or a lower band. Wait until week ten and it is a new charge.

5. The rules that decide what you are charged

Highest band wins on a mixed course. Different bands in one course of treatment, you pay the highest one.

Same band is charged once. Four fillings is one Band 2 charge.

Two months of protection. Further treatment within 2 months in the same or a lower band is free. In a higher band you pay the higher band fee. After 2 months, you pay again.

Three things are free for everyone, regardless of exemption: having stitches removed, having your dentist stop bleeding from your mouth, and having dentures repaired. If dentures cannot be repaired you pay for new ones.

Clinically necessary is the gate. Scaling on the NHS depends on the dental professional saying it is clinically needed. If they say it is not, you pay privately. The same logic runs through white fillings, orthodontics and wisdom teeth: available on the NHS when clinically necessary, private otherwise.

Private is a separate conversation. If you are eligible for free NHS treatment, that only covers the NHS treatment. Any private work you choose, you pay for.

And this is England. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run their own charging systems with different bands and different figures.

The honest verdict

NHS dental charging is better value than its reputation and worse explained than almost any other price list in public services. £76.60 for a course that includes an examination, a scale and as many fillings as you need in one go is not expensive. The two month rule is genuinely generous and is the single most useful thing on this page, because it turns a follow-up into a free visit if you go back promptly.

Band 3 at £332.10 is the one that hurts, and there is no way to soften it. A crown, a denture or a bridge is a serious bill for a household on a tight month, which makes the exemption list worth reading properly rather than assuming it is not you. Pregnancy and the twelve months after a birth catch a lot of people who never check. So does the rule that dependants under 20 are covered when a parent gets a qualifying benefit.

On the contradiction: it is small, it is 50p and £1.30, and it is not a scandal. Treat it as a reminder rather than a grievance. Dental charges change every 1 April, and the number in the heading is the one that has been updated.

Frequently asked

Is a check-up really £27.90? Yes, that is Band 1, which covers examination, assessment and advice, plus X-rays and simple scaling where clinically needed. If you need anything from a higher band in the same course, you pay that higher band instead, not both.

Why have I seen £27.40 quoted? That figure appears in the gum treatment paragraph on the NHS page alongside £75.30 for Band 2. Those are the previous year's charges and they contradict the current headings of £27.90 and £76.60 on the same page. The headings are the current figures.

Do I get free treatment on Universal Credit? Only if your income is below a certain amount. Universal Credit is not an automatic exemption in the way Pension Credit Guarantee Credit is. Check with the NHSBSA eligibility tool before claiming an exemption, because claiming one you are not entitled to leads to a penalty.

My child is 18 and has left school. Do they pay? Usually yes, unless they are in full-time education, or unless you or your partner receive a qualifying benefit, in which case dependants aged under 20 are covered.

I paid and then found out I was exempt. Can I get it back? Possibly. The NHS Business Services Authority publishes a refund process for NHS dental charges paid by someone who was entitled to free or partly paid treatment.
