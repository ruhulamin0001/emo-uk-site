Defender Scores 99.0%. The £60 One Scores 99.97%. What That 0.97% Is Actually Worth

The antivirus question is usually argued with test scores, and the test scores are now so close together that they cannot settle it. The useful question is different: what are UK households actually losing money to, and does a subscription stop it?

The short version

  • Microsoft Defender scored a perfect 18 out of 18 at AV-TEST in April 2026 and Advanced+ with 99.0% protection at AV-Comparatives across February to May 2026. It is no longer the weak option.
  • The best paid engines score marginally higher. Bitdefender 99.97%, ESET 99.95%, Kaspersky 99.94% in the March 2026 malware protection test. The gap is real but it is under one percentage point.
  • UK Finance reports £1.28 billion stolen through fraud in 2025, up 4%, across more than four million cases. That is roughly eight frauds a minute, and antivirus stops almost none of it.
  • Individuals lost £1.03 billion of that, averaging £4.6k per report. Investment scams alone rose 40% to a record £221.5 million.
  • If you are going to spend money on security, the highest value purchase is usually not antivirus. It is a password manager and turning on two factor authentication, which are free or close to it.

1. What the independent tests actually found

There are two labs whose results are worth reading, AV-TEST in Germany and AV-Comparatives in Austria. Both test against live malware rather than a static sample set, and both publish their methodology.

Two things stand out. First, Defender is now scoring alongside products that cost £40 to £80 a year. Second, a free third party product, Avast Free, also scored perfectly, so "free" and "weak" are no longer the same word.

One point in Defender's favour that rarely gets mentioned: its performance impact is the lowest of any consumer engine. Paid suites are heavier, which on an older laptop is a real cost you pay every day rather than a theoretical one.

The argument used to be free versus protected. It is now 99.0% versus 99.97%, which is a very different argument, and a much smaller one.

2. What the 0.97% gap means in practice

Be fair to the paid products: the gap is genuine. Across thousands of malware samples, 99.97% and 99.0% are meaningfully different numbers, and if you are unlucky enough to meet the sample in that gap, the difference is total.

But translate it. These tests deliberately expose the software to live malware repeatedly. A typical UK home user does not encounter thousands of live samples a year. They encounter a handful of risky moments: a dodgy download, an email attachment, a fake update prompt.

So the practical question is not "which is better in a lab" but "does my behaviour put me in the gap". If you download cracked software, install browser extensions casually, or use a shared family PC where somebody else does, you are meeting more samples and the extra percentage point starts to matter. If your machine is used for email, banking, shopping and streaming, you are not near the gap.

3. What UK households are really losing money to

This is the part that reframes the whole purchase.

UK Finance's annual fraud report puts £1.28 billion stolen through fraud in 2025, up 4% on 2024, across more than four million cases. That works out at roughly eight frauds every minute, with case volume up 11%.

  • Individuals accounted for £1.03 billion of the losses, at an average of £4.6k per report.
  • Investment scams rose 40% to a record £221.5 million.
  • Authorised push payment fraud rose 19% to £576.4 million. That is fraud where the victim is persuaded to send the money themselves.
  • Cyber dependent crime, the malware and hacking category, was 22.6% of cases. The clear majority was not.

Read that last pair together. The bulk of UK fraud loss is people being persuaded, not machines being infected. Antivirus does not intervene when you willingly transfer £4,600 to someone impersonating your bank. No subscription protects against that.

4. What Defender does and does not include

Microsoft Defender is built into Windows, on by default, and updates through Windows Update. If you have never installed anything, you already have it running.

Included: real time malware scanning, cloud delivered protection, ransomware protection through Controlled Folder Access which is worth switching on manually, a firewall, SmartScreen reputation checking in Edge, and parental controls through Family Safety.

Not included: a VPN, a password manager, dark web monitoring, identity theft insurance, or a cross platform licence covering your phone and your partner's Mac. Those extras are most of what a paid suite is actually selling you at renewal.

Worth knowing separately: Windows 10 stopped receiving security updates on 14 October 2025. If you are still on Windows 10, no antivirus fixes that, because the gap is in the operating system beneath it. Upgrading to Windows 11, or moving to a supported OS, matters more than any security subscription.

5. When paid antivirus is genuinely worth it

There are real cases, and it is not honest to pretend otherwise.

  • You want one licence across several devices and people. A family plan covering five devices including Android and macOS is a genuine convenience Defender does not offer.
  • You are buying the bundle, not the engine. If a suite includes a VPN and a password manager you would otherwise pay for separately, compare the bundle price against buying those two alone. Sometimes it wins.
  • You share the machine with someone who clicks anything. A teenager downloading game mods genuinely does encounter more samples. The extra percentage point earns its place.
  • You want somebody to phone. Paid products come with support. For a less confident user, or one you are supporting remotely, that has value that does not appear in a test score.

When it is not worth it: a single modern Windows 11 machine, one careful adult, used for ordinary things. In that case you are paying £40 to £80 a year for under one percentage point of lab difference and a slower computer.

6. Where the same £60 does more

Two households, both spending £60 on security this year.

Household A buys a premium antivirus suite. Protection moves from about 99.0% to about 99.97% against malware samples. Everything else is unchanged. Their passwords are still reused across sites, and their email account still has no second factor.

Household B keeps Defender, which is free, and spends the £60 differently: about £30 a year on a password manager for the family, and nothing at all on turning on two factor authentication for email and banking, because that is free. They spend an evening changing the reused password that was on their main email account.

What each is protected against. Household A is better protected against a malware sample in the 0.97% gap. Household B is protected against credential stuffing, where a password leaked from one breached website is tried against their email, and against account takeover, which is the front door to most of that £1.03 billion of individual losses.

Neither is wrong. But the risks they bought protection from are very different sizes.

7. Traps in antivirus pricing

  • The renewal jump. Year one at £19.99, year two at £79.99, automatically, on the card you gave them. Diarise the renewal date the day you buy.
  • Auto renewal you cannot cancel online. New UK subscription rules requiring easy online cancellation do not take effect until spring 2027, so check the cancellation route before you subscribe.
  • Scareware in the free version. Some free products exist to generate alarming pop ups about "issues found" that are resolved by upgrading. Defender does not do this, which is a real advantage.
  • Bundled VPNs with limits. The included VPN is often capped at a low monthly data allowance, which is not a VPN you can actually use.
  • Paying for a scan you already have. Never pay a phone caller or a pop up to "clean" your PC. That call is itself the fraud.

The honest verdict

For a single, up to date Windows 11 machine used by a reasonably careful adult, Microsoft Defender is enough. It scores 18 out of 18 at AV-TEST, 99.0% at AV-Comparatives, costs nothing, and slows your machine less than anything you could buy.

Buy a paid suite if you want multi device family cover, if you genuinely want the bundled VPN and password manager, or if somebody in the house downloads things they should not. Those are good reasons. "Free antivirus is not real protection" is not one, and has not been for a while.

And keep the £1.28 billion in view. The money UK households lose is overwhelmingly lost to persuasion, not infection. Two factor authentication on your email, unique passwords, and the habit of never acting on an urgent message about your money will protect you far more than the difference between 99.0% and 99.97%.

Frequently asked

Do I need antivirus on a Mac? macOS has its own built in protections and the consumer malware volume is lower, though not zero. The same logic applies: behaviour and account security matter more than the product.

Do I need antivirus on Android? Install from the Play Store, keep the OS updated, and check app permissions. That covers most of it. Paid mobile antivirus is largely selling features rather than protection.

Is Windows Defender the same as Microsoft Defender? Yes, the consumer product was renamed. Older articles calling "Windows Defender" weak are usually describing a version from several years ago, and the test scores have moved a long way since.

Should I run two antivirus products at once? No. They interfere with each other and can flag each other as threats. Run one, and let Defender step aside automatically if you install another.
