HMRC Has Been Collecting Your Crypto Data Since January. The £300 Fine Everyone Quotes Is the Wrong One

Since 1 January 2026 every UK crypto platform has been legally required to hold your name, date of birth and National Insurance number, and to log every disposal you make. The first report reaches HMRC by 31 May 2027. Almost every write up of this mentions a £300 fine, and almost all of them point it at the wrong person.

The short version

  • Collection started 1 January 2026, not next year. UK platforms must already hold your name, date of birth, home address, country of residence and your National Insurance number or UTR, plus the value, asset, type and number of units for every transaction (HMRC guidance, updated 1 January 2026).
  • The first report covers the whole of 2026, retrospectively. Providers file between 1 January and 31 May 2027, covering 1 January to 31 December 2026. There is no clean slate from today.
  • The £300 appears twice in the rules, aimed at two different people. Regulation 11(2) fines the platform up to £300 per user. Regulation 13 fines you up to £300, but only where the failure is deliberate or careless (SI 2025/744).
  • Your tax did not change. HMRC's own policy paper says the requirement is "for the RCASPs ... and not the individual cryptoasset user", and scores the Exchequer impact as negligible in every year to 2030-31.
  • The mismatch nobody mentions: CARF reports a calendar year, UK tax runs on a tax year, so the 2026 report straddles two of your tax years and lands roughly eight months before the return covering it is even due.

1. What is being collected, and since when

The rules are called the Cryptoasset Reporting Framework, or CARF. They sit in The Reporting Cryptoasset Service Providers (Due Diligence and Reporting Requirements) Regulations 2025, made on 25 June 2025, and every operative regulation came into force on 1 January 2026.

If you hold crypto through a UK provider, that provider has been under a legal duty since New Year's Day to collect and verify, for each individual user:

  • name, date of birth and home address
  • country of residence
  • National Insurance number or Unique Taxpayer Reference for UK residents, or a tax identification number and issuing country for everyone else

And for every transaction: the value, the type of cryptoasset, the type of transaction and the number of units. That covers Bitcoin, stablecoins and NFTs alike.

Providers submit their first report to HMRC in a window running from 1 January 2027 to 31 May 2027, covering 1 January to 31 December 2026. If you have been waiting to see whether this applies to you, it has applied since January and the trades are already logged.

2. The £300 fine everyone quotes is the wrong one

Search this topic and you will be told, repeatedly, that crypto investors face a £300 fine. That is a garbled version of something real, and the garbling matters, because it turns an administrative tidy up into a threat. The £300 figure appears in two separate regulations, pointing at two separate people:

Regulation 11(2). Where a UK provider fails to obtain a valid self-certification from a user, the provider is liable to a penalty not exceeding £300 for each user affected.

Regulation 13. Where a self-certification provider, meaning you, fails to give a valid self-certification, you are liable to a penalty not exceeding £300, but only where the failure is deliberate or is due to a failure to take reasonable care.

So there is a user facing penalty. It is not a myth. But it is capped at £300 in total, not per transaction or per platform, it is conditional rather than automatic, and it is triggered by neglecting to hand over your details, not by how much you traded or whether you made money.

The penalty is for ignoring an email asking for your National Insurance number. It is not a tax, and it is not a fine for owning crypto.

For completeness, the other penalties in the same regulations all land on the platform, not on you: up to £100 per user for a due diligence failure, up to £5,000 a year for poor record keeping, up to £5,000 plus £600 a day for a late report, up to £100 per user for an inaccurate or incomplete one, and up to £1,000 plus £300 a day for failing to register with HMRC. Every one of those figures is a maximum, written as "not exceeding".

3. The UK went further than the international rule required

This is the part that explains why you are affected at all. The OECD framework the UK signed up to is about cross border information. On its own terms it does not require a UK provider to report on a UK resident, because there is no other country to send that data to. The UK chose to add domestic reporting on top: announced at Autumn Budget 2024, legislated in June 2025, with Finance Bill 2025-26 amending regulation 6 to bring UK resident users into scope.

The result, in HMRC's own words in its policy paper of 26 November 2025, is that it will hold CARF data "on all UK taxpayers using both UK based and non-UK based RCASPs".

Two figures from that same paper are worth knowing, because they are the honest counterweight to the crackdown framing. HMRC scores the Exchequer impact of the domestic extension as negligible in every year from 2026-27 through to 2030-31, and estimates it affects roughly 50 businesses. The department also states plainly that the requirement is "for the RCASPs ... and not the individual cryptoasset user", and that the measure "doesn't change tax obligations".

One more line from the equalities section, which is rarely quoted and says something real about who this lands on. HMRC's commissioned research estimates that people aged 16 to 44 make up 76% of UK crypto owners against 46% of UK adults, men 69% against 51%, and those from an Asian or Asian British background 11% against 5%.

4. Worked example: Imran in Luton

Imran earns £34,000 in a salaried job. He bought £4,000 of Bitcoin across 2021 and 2022 and sold the lot in June 2026 for £11,500. The arithmetic below is ours.

Gain: £11,500 less £4,000 = £7,500.

Less the annual exempt amount of £3,000 for 2026/27 = £4,500 taxable.

Band check: his £34,000 salary leaves about £16,270 of basic rate band, so the whole gain is taxed at the basic rate of 18%.

Capital Gains Tax due: £810.

Imran owed that £810 before CARF existed and he owes exactly £810 now. What changed is that HMRC will be handed the disposal rather than having to find it.

Now the timing, which is the part that catches people. The June 2026 sale falls in the 2026/27 tax year, so Imran's return is not due until 31 January 2028. But the CARF report covering it is a calendar year report, filed by 31 May 2027. HMRC sees the trade roughly eight months before the return declaring it is due, and the same report straddles two UK tax years, because January to 5 April 2026 sat in 2025/26.

5. What is actually worth doing

Short list, and it is genuinely short.

  • Answer the email. If a platform has asked for your NI number, date of birth or address, give it. That single act removes any regulation 13 exposure entirely, because the penalty requires a deliberate or careless failure.
  • Pull your 2026 transaction history now. Exchanges close, restrict UK users and lose old data. Export a CSV for the full calendar year and keep it, because HMRC expects you to evidence acquisition costs.
  • Check whether you are over the line. The annual exempt amount is £3,000, so gains below it with no other disposals mean no CGT. Rates are 18% within the basic rate band and 24% above.
  • Do not close accounts to hide. The 2026 data is already collected. Closing an account in December does not remove the year that has already happened.

The honest verdict

If you have been declaring your crypto gains, CARF changes nothing for you at all. Answer the identity request, keep your records, carry on. That is the whole job, and it is free.

If you have not been declaring them, be clear eyed: this is not a new tax and not a new penalty regime aimed at you, it is a data pipe. The exposure that should concern you is the ordinary Capital Gains Tax you already owed, plus interest and penalties for not declaring it, and that is a much larger number than £300.

There is nothing to buy here. No app and no accountant is required to type your National Insurance number into a form. If your affairs are genuinely complicated, with staking, DeFi or hundreds of disposals, paid help is reasonable. For one exchange account and a handful of trades it is not.

Frequently asked

Will I get a £300 fine just for holding crypto? No. The user facing penalty in regulation 13 is capped at £300 and applies only where you deliberately or carelessly fail to provide a valid self-certification, which in practice means ignoring your platform's request for your details. Providing them ends the matter.

Is crypto taxed differently from shares now? No. Both sit at 18% within the basic rate band and 24% above it, with the same £3,000 annual exempt amount and the same pooling rules. Those rates have applied to all assets since 30 October 2024.

Does using an overseas exchange keep me out of it? Not reliably. More than 50 jurisdictions have agreed to exchange this data, so an overseas provider reports to its own tax authority, which passes UK resident details to HMRC. First international exchanges are due in 2027.

What if I made losses? Losses can be set against gains, but you generally have to claim them. Registering a capital loss with HMRC preserves it for future years, and doing so is worth more than most people assume when a later gain arrives.
