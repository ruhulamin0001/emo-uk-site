1. Who this actually applies to

You need a hot water cylinder. That is a tank, usually in an airing cupboard, usually about the size of a dustbin, that stores hot water heated by your boiler or an immersion heater.

If your boiler heats water on demand as you open the tap, you have a combi and you do not have a cylinder. There is nothing on this page for you. That is worth saying plainly at the top rather than three sections down, because a lot of guidance on this subject wastes the reader's time before admitting it.

If you do have a cylinder, there are three states it can be in. It may be bare copper, which is rare now and the best case for this measure. It may have a thin layer of foam bonded to it at the factory, often around 25mm, which is the case the Energy Saving Trust's £45 figure is built on. Or it may already be wrapped in a thick jacket or have modern factory insulation, in which case you are done.

Our page on the running cost of an immersion heater against a gas boiler covers which of those is cheaper to heat the tank with. This page is only about stopping the heat leaving once it is in there.

2. The arithmetic, and why it is so much better than everything else

The Energy Saving Trust's guidance on insulating tanks, pipes and radiators, last updated 3 November 2025, states that a hot water cylinder jacket costs about £30 in Great Britain and £30 in Northern Ireland. It states that adding an 80mm British Standard jacket to a tank with 25mm foam built in can save £45 a year in Great Britain and £35 a year in Northern Ireland. Its own summary of that is that it is more than enough to cover the cost of the jacket.

£30 divided by £45 a year is 0.67 years. Eight months.

Set that against the rest of the measures we have costed on this site this week. Cavity wall insulation pays back in about ten to eleven years. Solid wall insulation does not pay back on bills at all. Floor insulation runs to fourteen years and beyond at professional prices. A full set of heating controls is six years.

Eight months is a different category of decision. At that speed the question is not whether it is worth doing but whether you can be bothered to walk to the airing cupboard, and the honest answer for most people is that they have never opened it.

The reason the return is so extreme is that a hot water cylinder is a large, hot object sitting in an unheated cupboard, losing heat continuously, twenty-four hours a day, whether or not anyone is using hot water. It is one of the few things in a house that wastes energy while you sleep and while you are out.

The £6 for pipe insulation is small enough that it is not really a payback calculation, it is a rounding error that happens to be positive. Do it while you are in the cupboard with the jacket, not as a separate trip.

There is a second reason to lag pipes that has nothing to do with the bill. The Energy Saving Trust points out that pipes exposed to cold, particularly outdoors or in a loft, basement or garage, are at risk of freezing below zero, and that insulating them reduces that risk. A burst pipe costs considerably more than £6.

4. The five minute check to do before you spend anything

Open the airing cupboard and look at the tank. Then work through this in order.

Is there a cylinder at all? If not, stop. You have a combi.

Is it bare copper? If yes, buy the jacket. This is the best case and the £45 is if anything conservative.

Is there a red or green quilted jacket already strapped round it? Measure its thickness by pinching it. The Energy Saving Trust's rule is that it should be at least 80mm. Old jackets fitted decades ago were often 25mm or 50mm and sag over time. If yours is thinner than 80mm, replacing it is worth doing.

Is there a hard, bonded foam layer instead of a quilted jacket? That is factory insulation. If it is around 25mm you are exactly the case the £45 figure describes, and you can fit a jacket over the top. Modern cylinders are often manufactured with considerably thicker foam, and those are already done.

Are the pipes coming out of the top of the tank bare? The first metre or so off the top of a cylinder is where most of the pipe loss happens, because that is the hottest water. Lag that first, if you only lag one thing.

5. The cylinder thermostat, which is free

While you are in the cupboard, look for a small dial strapped to the side of the tank about a third of the way up. That is the cylinder thermostat, and it sets how hot the tank is kept.

The Energy Saving Trust's heating controls guidance puts the right setting at 60 to 65 degrees Celsius. Below 60 you risk legionella bacteria surviving in the stored water, which is why the guidance does not go lower. Above 65 you are storing water hotter than anyone uses it, paying to keep it there, and increasing the loss through the tank walls that the jacket is there to reduce.

A lot of cylinder thermostats have been sitting at maximum since installation because nobody ever looked. Turning it down to 60 costs nothing, takes ten seconds and is the only free item on this page.

It also compounds with the jacket. A cooler tank loses less heat through its walls in the first place, so the jacket has less to hold in.

6. Worked example: Bernadette in Hull

Bernadette has a 1990s cylinder in the airing cupboard with a bonded foam layer she measures at about 25mm, and the first metre of copper pipe above it is bare. Her cylinder thermostat is set to just under 70.

Jacket: she buys an 80mm British Standard jacket for about £30 and straps it over the existing foam. On the Energy Saving Trust's figures that saves her £45 a year. Payback: eight months.

Pipes: she lags the exposed pipework with foam sleeving for a few pounds. That is £6 a year.

Thermostat: she turns it from just under 70 down to 60. That costs nothing and saves an amount the Energy Saving Trust does not put a national figure on, so we are not going to invent one. It is not zero.

Her total spend is about £35 and her measurable saving is about £51 a year. She is in profit before next summer, and the jacket will still be there in fifteen years.

Compare that with the other things she was considering. New double glazing, floor insulation, a smart thermostat. All of those are worth reading about, and all of them are years away from paying for themselves. She should do this one first, this weekend, and think about the rest afterwards.

7. What the jacket does not fix, and when to replace the cylinder instead

A jacket slows heat leaving the tank. It does not do three other things, and it is worth knowing which is which before you decide this is the end of your hot water spending.

It does not make the water cheaper to heat. That is set by whether you heat the tank with gas from the boiler or with an immersion heater on electricity, and at the October 2026 cap those two are far apart: 7.97p a kWh for gas against 26.32p for electricity. If your tank is heated by immersion because the boiler is broken or the timer is wrong, fixing that is worth far more than any jacket.

It does not stop losses from the pipework beyond the first metre. Long runs of uninsulated hot pipe under floors are a real loss and pipe foam only reaches the parts you can get to.

It does not help a cylinder that is failing. If the tank is scaled up, or the coil inside it is furred, it takes longer to heat and holds less usable water. A jacket wrapped round a bad cylinder is still a good £30 spend, but it is not the answer to slow hot water.

There is a point at which replacing the cylinder beats insulating it. A modern factory-insulated cylinder comes with thick bonded foam and loses considerably less than an old tank plus a jacket. But that is a job with a plumber attached and a bill in the high hundreds or more, and the payback against a £30 jacket is long. The sensible order is: jacket now, thermostat down now, and replace the cylinder only when it fails or when you are already having the system worked on for another reason.

One further check that costs nothing. Look at whether the tank is heated at times nobody is home. Many cylinder systems run on a timer that was set once, years ago, and heats a full tank every morning whether or not anyone is going to use it. Reheating a tank you then leave to go cold all day is the most expensive way to own one, and a jacket only slows the loss, it does not stop you paying for it in the first place. Our page on heating controls and their payback covers the programmer side of that.

8. The honest verdict

If you have a hot water cylinder with bare copper or thin factory foam, buy an 80mm British Standard jacket. About £30 for £45 a year on the Energy Saving Trust's figures is an eight month payback, which is the fastest return of anything we have costed in this series, and the jacket will outlast the boiler. Lag the first metre of pipe off the top of the tank while you are in there for another £6 a year, and turn the cylinder thermostat down to 60 degrees, which is free.

Do not buy anything if you have a combi boiler, because you have no cylinder for it to go on. Do not buy a second jacket to go over an existing 80mm one. And measure the tank before you order, because a jacket that leaves the top uncovered misses the hottest part of it.

Frequently asked

How do I know whether I have a combi or a cylinder? If there is a large insulated tank in a cupboard, you have a cylinder. If your boiler is a single wall-mounted unit with no tank anywhere and hot water comes through instantly with no chance of running out, you have a combi.

Can I fit a jacket over existing factory foam? Yes. That is exactly the case the Energy Saving Trust's £45 figure describes: adding an 80mm British Standard jacket to a tank with 25mm foam built in. Strap it over the top.

Is there a risk in insulating a cylinder too well? No, but do not cover the cylinder thermostat or block access to the immersion heater element and its wiring. Jackets are made in segments precisely so you can leave those accessible.

What if my tank is in a warm room rather than a cupboard? The heat it loses is not entirely wasted in winter, because it warms the room. In summer it is entirely wasted, and it is also making the room uncomfortable. The jacket is still worth fitting.
