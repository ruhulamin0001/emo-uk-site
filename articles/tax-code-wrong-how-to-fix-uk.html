Four Letters On Your Payslip Decide Whether You Are Overpaying Tax

If your tax code ends in W1, M1, X or NONCUM, you are on an emergency code and being taxed as though this month's pay repeats every month of the year. That is often temporary and correct. Knowing which of those two you are looking at takes about ninety seconds, and GOV.UK gives you a number to measure it against.

The short version

  • 1257L is the code most people with one job or pension have. The numbers are your tax free allowance with the last digit removed, so 1257L means £12,570 of tax free pay.
  • W1, M1, X or NONCUM at the end means emergency. HMRC works your tax out on that week or month alone, as if you earned that amount every period of the year.
  • HMRC allows up to 35 days from starting a job to issue the right code. GOV.UK's own trigger for acting is that you started more than 35 days ago and the code is still wrong.
  • BR means every pound from that job is taxed at basic rate with no allowance. Normal for a second job. Wrong, and expensive, if it is your only one.
  • Sometimes an emergency code is correct and you should leave it. Start getting company benefits or the State Pension and you may be on one until the end of the tax year by design.
  • You do not have to claim most refunds. If you are employed or on a pension, HMRC issues a P800 or Simple Assessment automatically, sent out between June and March of the following tax year.

1. How to read your code in ninety seconds

Your tax code is the instruction HMRC gives your employer about how much tax to take. Get it wrong in one direction and you are lending the government money interest free. Get it wrong in the other and you owe a bill you did not budget for.

You can find it on your payslip, in the HMRC app, by checking your tax code for the current year online, or on a Tax Code Notice letter. Start there rather than guessing.

The numbers. HMRC takes your tax free Personal Allowance, subtracts anything you owe tax on that has not been taxed, such as untaxed interest or a company benefit, and then removes the last digit. So 1257L means £12,570 of tax free pay across the year. The Personal Allowance is frozen at £12,570, which is why 1257L has been the standard code for several years running and why more people drift into paying tax as wages rise.

GOV.UK gives a clean example. You are entitled to the standard £12,570, but your employer provides medical insurance worth £1,570. That benefit comes off your allowance, leaving £11,000 of tax free pay, and your code becomes 1100L.

The letters. These describe your situation. The ones worth knowing:

  • L you get the standard Personal Allowance. This is the normal one.
  • BR every pound from this job or pension is taxed at basic rate, with no allowance applied here.
  • D0 and D1 everything from this source is taxed at higher rate and additional rate respectively.
  • 0T your allowance is used up, or you have started a job and your employer does not yet have what they need.
  • K you have untaxed income worth more than your Personal Allowance, so the calculation runs the other way.
  • M and N Marriage Allowance, received and given respectively.
  • NT no tax on this income at all.
  • S at the front means Scottish rates, C means Welsh rates.

HMRC also runs a free tool where you type your code in and it tells you what it means and what you may need to do next. If you only do one thing after reading this, do that.

2. The emergency codes, and the 35 day line

You are on an emergency code if your code ends in W1, M1 or X. W1 is used when you are paid weekly, M1 when you are paid monthly, X when your pay dates vary. Depending on your employer's payroll software you might see NONCUM instead. If it does not end in one of those four, you are not on an emergency code, whatever anyone has told you.

What the emergency code actually does is the part people find surprising. Normally your tax is worked out on your total income so far in the tax year, so a quiet month evens out a busy one. On an emergency code, HMRC works out your tax on that week or month alone, taxing you as though you are paid that amount every single period of the year. If your first payslip at a new job includes a part month, or a bonus, or overtime, that one figure gets treated as your annual run rate. That is how people end up staring at a first payslip that looks badly wrong.

The 35 day number is the one to hold on to. When you start a job, your new employer tells HMRC. If they do not have your previous income and tax details, you get paid on an emergency code. HMRC updates it once the details come in from both employers, and GOV.UK says plainly that this can take up to 35 days from when you start your job. GOV.UK's own next step is headed “if you started a job more than 35 days ago”, which is as close as government guidance gets to telling you when to stop waiting.

The single most effective thing you can do to shorten it is give your new employer the P45 from your last job. If your previous employer did not give you one, ask. Without a P45 there is a starter checklist to fill in instead, and filling it in properly is the difference between three weeks of emergency tax and three months.

Now the case where the emergency code is correct and you should leave it alone. If you have just started receiving company benefits or the State Pension, GOV.UK says you might be put on an emergency code, that this is normal, and that it helps you pay the right amount of tax for that year. You will stay on it until the end of the tax year and get a normal code in the new one. Check online that the benefit or the State Pension is actually reflected in the code. If it is, there is nothing to fix and ringing HMRC will not speed anything up.

3. The codes that usually mean something is actually wrong

Emergency codes correct themselves. These ones often do not.

BR on your only job. BR taxes every pound at basic rate with no Personal Allowance applied. That is exactly right on a second job, where the allowance is already being used by the first. On a single job it means you are paying tax on the first £12,570 that should be tax free. At basic rate that is £2,514 over a full year. This is the most expensive common error on this list and it is the easiest to spot, because BR does not end in W1, M1 or X, so nothing will nudge it loose on its own.

0T where you expected a normal code. 0T also gives no allowance. It appears when your allowance is genuinely used up, and also when a new employer does not have the details they need. The second case is a paperwork problem wearing the costume of a tax decision.

A code that quietly shrank. HMRC reduces your allowance for untaxed income and company benefits. If you gave up the company car, stopped the private medical cover, or paid off an old underpayment, the deduction should come out again, and it does not always. Compare this year's code with last year's, which you can see in the online service, and ask what changed.

Two jobs where the wrong one has the allowance. If your allowance sits against the job paying £4,000 a year and BR sits against the one paying £20,000, you will not use the whole allowance and you will overpay until it is sorted. HMRC can move it.

Left over from a job you no longer have. A code attached to an ended employment can leave your allowance stranded. This is common after a short contract that overlapped with a permanent role.

4. How to fix it, and how the money comes back

The route is the same in every case and it does not require a phone call as the first step. Check your tax code for the current year in the online service or the HMRC app. If the information HMRC holds about your income, benefits or employments is wrong, update it there. If you cannot resolve it in the service, contact HMRC about Income Tax. There is no fee and no form to buy.

Ignore anyone charging you for this. Refund agents take a percentage of money you were going to receive anyway, and some use assignments that route your refund through them. Everything in this article is free to do yourself in under half an hour.

Once the code is right, the money comes back in one of two ways.

Within the same tax year, a corrected cumulative code usually sorts itself on your next payslip. Your employer recalculates the year to date and the overpayment comes back as a smaller deduction, or occasionally a refund, in that pay run. No claim needed.

After the tax year ends, HMRC reconciles automatically. If you are employed or receiving a pension and you have paid too much or too little, you get either a tax calculation letter, called a P800, or a Simple Assessment letter. GOV.UK says these are sent out between June and March of the following tax year, so a code problem in the year to April 2026 might not produce a letter until well into the following winter. You will get a Simple Assessment rather than a P800 if the tax cannot be collected from your income, if you owe more than £3,000, or if you owe tax on your State Pension.

One important exception: if you are registered for Self Assessment, none of this applies. Your bill is adjusted through your return and you will not get either letter. And if you think you have overpaid but no letter has arrived, there is a separate route on GOV.UK to claim a refund rather than waiting indefinitely.

5. Worked example: Declan in Coventry, two jobs and a BR code

Declan works four days a week at a warehouse in Coventry earning £21,000 a year, and does Saturdays at a garden centre for £4,800 a year. He has been on 1257L at the garden centre and BR at the warehouse since he picked up the Saturday work eighteen months ago, because the garden centre job came first in HMRC's records.

What is happening. His £12,570 allowance is sitting against the garden centre job, which only pays £4,800. So £4,800 of allowance gets used and £7,770 of it is doing nothing. Meanwhile the warehouse job is on BR, taxing all £21,000 at basic rate with no allowance at all.

What it costs. Unused allowance of £7,770, taxed at the 20% basic rate it should have sheltered him from, is £1,554 a year of tax he should not be paying. Over the eighteen months it has been running, roughly £2,330.

What he does. He signs in to check his tax code for the current year, sees the allowance sitting on the wrong employment, and asks HMRC to move it to the warehouse job. Because his total income is above the allowance either way, the fix is not extra allowance, it is putting the allowance where there is enough pay to absorb it. The garden centre then moves to BR, which is correct for a second job.

What comes back. The current year corrects through payroll once the new codes reach both employers. The earlier year does not correct itself through payroll, because it has closed. That is what the P800 is for, and if one has not arrived he can claim the refund directly rather than waiting.

Worth noting what this example is not. Declan was never on an emergency code. Nothing on his payslip ended in W1, M1 or X. If he had been waiting for an emergency code to resolve itself, he would still be waiting, and he would still be £1,554 a year down. That is the case for actually reading the letters rather than assuming the system corrects everything.

The honest verdict

Most tax codes are right, and most of the ones that are wrong fix themselves. The useful skill is not suspicion, it is telling the two apart, and the letters at the end of the code do almost all of that work for you.

If it ends in W1, M1, X or NONCUM, you are on an emergency code. Give your employer your P45, and use HMRC's 35 day figure as the line between waiting and acting. If it is an emergency code because you just started getting company benefits or the State Pension, that is deliberate and you should leave it alone until the new tax year.

If it says BR or 0T and this is your only job, that is the one worth acting on today. A full year on BR with a single job costs £2,514 at basic rate, and nothing in the system will nudge it loose on its own.

Everything here is free. Check the code online, correct the information HMRC holds, and let the P800 or the payroll correction do the rest. Anyone charging you a percentage is charging for a form you can fill in yourself.

Frequently asked

How do I know if I am on an emergency tax code? Look at the end of the code on your payslip. W1, M1, X or NONCUM means emergency. Anything else does not, including BR and 0T, which are not emergency codes even though they often produce the same feeling when you open the payslip.

How long should I wait before contacting HMRC about a new job? GOV.UK says HMRC can take up to 35 days from when you start to issue the right code, and its own guidance treats more than 35 days as the point to act. Before that, giving your employer your P45 is more useful than chasing.

Can I get back tax from previous years? Yes. Tax years are reconciled separately and there is a claim route on GOV.UK if no P800 has been sent. Check the current time limit for the year in question on GOV.UK, because it is a fixed number of years back and an old year will eventually close.

Is it worth paying a refund company? For a straightforward wrong tax code, no. They take a percentage of a refund you would have received anyway, and HMRC issues most of these automatically. Check what you are signing, because some agreements cover future refunds as well as the one you asked about.
