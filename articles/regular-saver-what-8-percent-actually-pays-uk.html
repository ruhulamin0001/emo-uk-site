An eight per cent regular saver pays about four, and it is not a trick

Regular savers carry the biggest numbers on the savings market and the smallest balances. The gap between the two is not sharp practice, it is arithmetic, and it is worth understanding before the Bank of England meets on 17 September.

The short version

  • Bank Rate is 3.75 per cent, held on 30 July 2026. Next decision 17 September 2026.
  • The headline rate is roughly halved because your first payment earns for twelve months and your last earns for one.
  • £250 a month at 8 per cent earns about £130 over a year on £3,000 paid in. That is 4.33 per cent of what you saved.
  • If you already hold the lump sum, drip feeding it in is worth about £65 more than leaving it at 4 per cent, not £120 more.
  • Tax rarely bites. The Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic rate, £500 for higher rate, nothing for additional rate.

1. Why the headline rate is roughly halved

A regular saver only lets you pay in a set amount each month. That is the whole mechanism and the whole explanation. The payment you make in month one sits there for twelve months, the one you make in month twelve for a single month. On average your money has been in the account for about half the term, so it earns about half of what the headline suggests.

Work it as fractions of a year. Month one earns 12/12, month two 11/12, down to 1/12. Those add up to 78/12, which is 6.5. So the interest is the monthly payment times the rate times 6.5, not 12. That is the standard approximation and ignores monthly compounding, which here moves the answer by pennies.

Nobody is hiding this. Every savings account advertises a rate on the balance. The trouble is we read savings rates as if they apply to the total we deposited, and on a regular saver they never do.

2. The arithmetic, with a worked example

Take 8 per cent as an illustration, at the top of what the market has advertised this month, used purely as a round number and not as a recommendation.

Ravi, Watford. He can put away £250 a month.

  • Paid in over twelve months: £3,000.
  • Interest: £250 x 0.08 x 6.5 = £130.
  • As a percentage of what he actually saved: 130 divided by 3,000 = 4.33 per cent.

Not £240. The 8 per cent headline and the 4.33 per cent outcome are both true statements about the same account.

Now the case where people genuinely lose money by getting this wrong. Suppose Ravi already holds the £3,000 in easy access at 4 per cent, and drip feeds it in at £250 a month.

  • Regular saver interest: £130, as above.
  • The money still waiting in easy access also earns. The first £250 leaves immediately, the second after one month, and so on, giving 66/12 = 5.5 year fractions at 4 per cent: £250 x 0.04 x 5.5 = £55.
  • Total: £185.
  • If he had simply left the whole £3,000 at 4 per cent: £120.

So the 8 per cent account is worth £65 more over a year, not £120 more. Real money, and worth twelve standing orders. But a long way from doubling anything, and if the easy access rate he gave up was higher, the gap narrows further.

3. Where the leftover sits, and when to bother

On a regular saver most of your money is somewhere else for most of the year, so the rate on that somewhere else matters more than the headline. Do not leave it in a current account. The £55 above is more than 40 per cent of what the regular saver itself paid. At nought per cent the £65 advantage becomes £10.

Watch the tax, but do not be paralysed by it. Per GOV.UK the Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic rate, £500 for higher rate and nothing for additional rate, with a starting rate for savings of up to £5,000 that shrinks as other income rises. The £185 above is nowhere near any of those. A basic rate taxpayer needs roughly £25,000 in easy access at 4 per cent to trouble the allowance, and at that point an ISA becomes the conversation.

None of this makes regular savers bad. It makes them a specific tool. They are genuinely good when you are saving out of income, because no lump sum sits idle and 4.33 per cent on money you would otherwise have spent is a good outcome. They are also a useful behavioural device. They fit badly when you already hold the cash, when the monthly cap makes the exercise worth tens of pounds, or when missing a month drops the rate. Check that last one before you open anything.

Context for the next few weeks: Bank Rate was held at 3.75 per cent on 30 July 2026, the next decision is due 17 September 2026, and the Bank's page showed inflation at 2.9 per cent against a 2 per cent target when this was written. A twelve month regular saver fixes your rate for the year, worth something if rates fall and costly if they rise.

The verdict

Halve the headline rate in your head. That one habit gets you within a few pence of the truth on any twelve month regular saver.

If you are saving from income, open one and set the standing order. If you already hold the lump sum, work out the real difference first: on £3,000 it was £65. And make sure the money waiting its turn is not sitting somewhere paying nothing, because that is where most of your money spends most of the year.

Frequently asked

Is the 8 per cent figure a real account? It is an illustration at the top end of what regular savers have been advertising. This site deliberately does not name live rates, because they change weekly and a stale number is worse than none. Check a current comparison table for the rate available to you.

Why 6.5 and not 6? Because the twelve monthly fractions from 12/12 down to 1/12 add up to 78/12, which is 6.5. Using 6 would understate the interest slightly.

What happens if I miss a month? It depends entirely on the account. Some allow it, some drop you to a much lower rate, some close the account. This is the single most important term to read before opening one.
