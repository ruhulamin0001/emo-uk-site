Electricity Is Three Times The Price Of Gas Per Unit Of Heat. So When Does A Plug-In Heater Actually Win?

"Just heat the room you are in" is the most repeated piece of winter money advice in Britain, and it is half right. A 2 kW fan heater costs 52.6p an hour flat out at the October 2026 cap. A gas boiler delivers the same heat for about a sixth of that. The heater only saves money when it stops the boiler running at all, and there is a simple number that tells you whether it will.

The short version

  • Electricity is 26.32p per kWh and gas is 7.97p per kWh from 1 October 2026 under the Ofgem cap. A plug-in heater turns electricity into heat at 100%; a condensing gas boiler is around 90% efficient, so gas heat costs about 8.9p per kWh.
  • That is a ratio of about 3 to 1. Every kWh of warmth from the socket costs three times the same warmth from the radiator.
  • A 2 kW fan heater costs 52.6p an hour flat out, a 1.5 kW oil-filled radiator 39.5p, and both cycle on a thermostat, so real use is often half that.
  • The crossover is about 4.5 kW of boiler output. If running the boiler for the whole house would burn less than 4.5 kW of gas-fired heat while you run a 1.5 kW heater in one room, the boiler is cheaper. Most homes burn more than that, so the heater wins only when the boiler would otherwise be on.
  • If the boiler is on anyway for other people, a plug-in heater is pure extra cost, £1.50 to £2 a day in a home office.
  • Turning down the radiators in unused rooms with their own valves costs nothing and gets most of the benefit.

1. The 3 to 1 ratio, from the two Ofgem rates

The Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 sets a Direct Debit unit rate of 26.32p per kWh for electricity and 7.97p per kWh for gas, GB average. Those are the only two figures in this article we did not calculate ourselves.

Heat is heat. A kilowatt-hour of warmth in a room is the same whether it came from a socket or a radiator, so the only thing that matters is what each route costs per kWh of heat delivered.

RouteFuel price per kWhEfficiencyCost per kWh of heat in the room
Plug-in electric heater, any type26.32p100%26.32p
Modern condensing gas boiler7.97p90% (our assumption)8.86p
Older non-condensing boiler7.97p70% (our assumption)11.39p

Every plug-in heater, fan, oil-filled, halogen, ceramic, "eco" or otherwise, converts electricity to heat at 100%. There is nothing to be efficient about; a resistor cannot lose energy anywhere except as heat. So an advert claiming one plug-in heater is more efficient than another is describing how the heat is spread around the room, not how much heat you get per penny. That is fixed by physics at 26.32p per kWh.

Gas heat costs between 8.9p and 11.4p per kWh depending on how good your boiler is. Call the ratio three to one. It has been around that level for years, and it is the single fact that decides this whole question.

2. What each heater costs an hour

The running cost of a plug-in heater is its plate rating multiplied by the unit rate, then reduced by however much of the time the thermostat has it switched off. The rating is printed on the heater, on the box, and on the listing, so use yours rather than ours.

HeaterPlate ratingCost per hour flat outCost per hour cycling 50% on a thermostat
Fan heater2,000 W52.6p26.3p
Oil-filled radiator1,500 W39.5p19.7p
Small panel or ceramic heater1,000 W26.3p13.2p
Halogen heater, one bar400 W10.5pNot thermostatic, 10.5p
Electric throw or blanket100 W2.6pAbout 1.5p

Our arithmetic, at 26.32p. The 50% column is an assumption: a heater in a small room with the door shut will reach the thermostat setting and start cycling, and half on, half off is a reasonable middle. In a large, draughty room the same heater might run flat out all evening and never reach temperature, and then the flat-out column is the true one.

The last row is the one that matters most, and it is the one the fan heater adverts hope you do not notice. Heating the person rather than the air is a hundred watts, not two thousand, which is why the electric throw is the only plug-in option that is cheap in absolute terms rather than merely cheaper than the alternative.

3. The crossover: when one electric room beats a gas house

Now the actual question. You are alone in the house for eight hours. Option A: run the boiler and heat everything. Option B: leave the boiler off and run a 1.5 kW heater in the one room you are in.

Option B costs 39.5p an hour flat out, or about 19.7p cycling. Option A costs whatever the boiler burns, which depends on the house. A well-insulated flat might need 3 kW of heat to hold temperature on a cold day; a detached 1930s house with solid walls might need 10 kW or more.

The crossover is the boiler output at which the two cost the same. Take the heater flat out at 39.5p an hour, and divide by 8.86p per kWh of gas heat: 4.46 kW. If your whole house needs less than about 4.5 kW of heat to stay warm, running the boiler is cheaper than a 1.5 kW heater in one room. If it needs more, the heater is cheaper.

Two things follow. First, for most houses the heater does win against heating the whole house, because most houses on a cold day need more than 4.5 kW. Second, and this is the bit that gets left out, the heater only wins if the boiler is actually off. If someone else is home, or the heating is on a timer for the evening anyway, the boiler runs regardless and the heater is 20p to 40p an hour of pure addition.

The other silent assumption is that you will be comfortable in one warm room with the rest of the house cold. Cold rooms in winter mean condensation on cold surfaces, and condensation means mould. Heating one room electrically for an entire winter while the rest of the house sits at 12 degrees can end up costing more in damp treatment than it saved in gas.

4. The free version of the same idea

Most of the benefit of "heat one room" is available without buying anything. If your radiators have thermostatic valves, the ones with a numbered dial, turn the valves down to 1 or the frost setting in the rooms you are not using, shut the doors, and run the boiler as normal. The boiler then heats one or two rooms at 8.9p per kWh instead of the whole house, which is cheaper than any plug-in heater can ever be, because it uses the cheaper fuel for the smaller job.

The main room thermostat needs to be in a room that is still being heated, or the boiler will run flat out trying to warm a room whose radiator you have turned off. If your thermostat is in the hall and you have turned the hall radiator down, that is exactly what will happen.

If you do not have thermostatic valves, fitting them is a plumbing job rather than a plug-in one, and we have covered smart versions of them separately. For the purposes of this article the point is simpler: the cheapest way to heat one room is still the boiler, just with fewer radiators open.

5. Worked example: Priya in Reading, working from a box room

Three-bed semi, works from home three days a week, partner out all day

Priya works from the smallest bedroom, a box room with one radiator, from 9 to 5 on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. The house has a condensing combi boiler and thermostatic valves on every radiator except the hall. On a typical January day we assume the whole house needs an average of 5 kW of heat over those eight hours to hold 20 degrees, which is our assumption for a 1990s semi with cavity insulation, not a measurement.

Option A, boiler on all day for the whole house. 5 kW for 8 hours is 40 kWh of heat. At 90% efficiency that is 44.4 kWh of gas at 7.97p: £3.54 a day.

Option B, boiler off, 1.5 kW oil-filled radiator in the box room, cycling 60% of the time. 1.5 kW times 0.6 times 8 hours is 7.2 kWh at 26.32p: £1.90 a day. Saving against option A: £1.64 a day. Over roughly 85 working-from-home days in a November to March heating season that is about £140, so a £60 heater pays for itself in the first winter, with the rest of the house left cold.

Option C, boiler on, every radiator except the box room and hall turned to frost setting. The boiler now heats perhaps 1.5 kW of demand plus some leakage into the cold rooms, call it 2 kW for 8 hours: 16 kWh of heat, 17.8 kWh of gas, £1.42 a day. Cheaper than the electric heater, the room is heated by the radiator she already owns, and nothing was bought.

The trap. On the two days her partner is home with the heating on anyway, the electric heater is £1.90 a day added to a boiler bill that does not fall. Over a winter that is about £60 of pure waste, which is exactly what the heater cost.

6. The honest verdict

Buy a plug-in heater to save money only if you will genuinely leave the boiler off while you use it, and only if turning down the other radiators is not available to you. In that narrow case a £40 to £80 heater pays back inside one winter, and an oil-filled radiator with a proper thermostat is the sensible type.

If your radiators have thermostatic valves, do not buy a heater at all. Turn the valves down in the rooms you are not using and let the boiler heat one room at a third of the price the socket would charge you.

If you want the cheapest possible warmth while sitting still, the answer is not a heater. It is an electric throw at about 2.6p an hour, and a jumper.

Frequently asked

Is it cheaper to heat one room with an electric heater than to use central heating? Only when the alternative is heating the whole house and the house needs more than about 4.5 kW of heat to stay warm, which most do on a cold day. If the boiler runs anyway, the heater is an extra cost, not a saving.

Which type of electric heater is cheapest to run? They all cost the same per kWh, 26.32p at the October 2026 cap. The differences are in wattage and whether there is a thermostat. A lower wattage with a thermostat costs less per hour; nothing is more efficient.

How much does a 2 kW heater cost per hour? 52.6p flat out at 26.32p per kWh. With a thermostat cycling it half the time, about 26p.

What about an electric heater in a house with no gas? Then the comparison is between electric options only, and the answer changes completely. Storage heaters on an off-peak tariff and heat pumps are the relevant comparison there, not this one.
