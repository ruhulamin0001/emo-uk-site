1. The two questions, and why they decide the whole thing

A shower head that restricts flow saves you two separate things: the water itself, and the energy used to heat that water. Whether either of those is worth money to you depends on how your house is plumbed and billed.

Question one: are you on a water meter? If you are, every litre you do not use is a litre you do not pay for, twice over, because most companies charge for water in and used water out on the same measured volume. If you are on rateable value, your bill is fixed by the property and using less water changes it by exactly nothing. The water half of the saving disappears.

Question two: what heats the shower water? If it is a mixer shower fed from a combi boiler or a hot water cylinder, then using less hot water means burning less gas, and that is a real saving. If it is an electric shower, the wall-mounted box with its own pull cord, the logic breaks. That unit draws its rated power, commonly 8.5 to 10.5 kilowatts, for as long as it is switched on. Reducing the flow through it does not reduce the power it draws. It just makes the water hotter.

So there are four households here, and the answer ranges from buy one today to do not buy one at all. Find yours in the table in section three.

2. What one litre of hot water actually costs

Everything below is built from three published numbers, all checked this month.

Water. Severn Trent's published metered charges for 2026/27 are £2.5503 per cubic metre for water supply and £1.6255 per cubic metre for used water. Together that is £4.1758 per cubic metre, and since a cubic metre is a thousand litres, one litre costs 0.4176p. Other companies differ, roughly between £3.30 and £4.20 combined, so take this as a middle-of-the-road figure and substitute your own.

Energy. Ofgem's price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 sets gas at 7.97p per kWh and electricity at 26.32p per kWh.

Physics. Raising one litre of water from a mains temperature of 12 degrees to a comfortable shower temperature of 40 degrees takes 0.0326 kWh of heat. A gas boiler running at about 90 per cent efficiency therefore burns 0.0362 kWh of gas to deliver it, which costs 0.2883p. The same heat from electricity costs 0.8570p, roughly three times as much.

Add the water to the gas and one litre of hot shower water costs 0.7059p on a metered gas-heated supply. That is the number the whole article turns on, and forty litres of it is 28.24p.

The difference is assumptions, not arithmetic. A national average has to include the many households that are not metered, that already have a restricted head, or that take shorter showers than eight minutes. If you are metered, gas-heated, and currently running an unrestricted head, the larger figure is the one that applies to you. If you are not, the smaller one is closer.

4. Why an electric shower breaks the usual logic

This is the part that trips up almost every article on the subject, so it is worth being precise.

An electric shower is a heater with a water pipe through it. Turn it on and it draws its rated power, say 9.5 kilowatts, continuously. Over an eight minute shower that is 1.267 kWh, or 33.34p, and that figure does not change when you fit a restricting head. What changes is the temperature: the same heat going into less water makes the water hotter, so most people respond by turning the temperature dial down, and the electricity used stays exactly where it was.

So on an electric shower, a low flow head saves water and only water. On a meter that is still £122 a year and well worth doing. Off a meter it is nothing.

There is a related trap worth naming. Some electric showers are sensitive to flow restriction and will trip out or pulse if you starve them, because their safety cut-outs are set for a minimum flow rate. If you have an electric shower and you want to save water, the manufacturer's own lower-flow spray plate is a safer route than a generic restricting head. Check the manual before buying anything.

If your hot water comes from a cylinder rather than a combi, there is a second saving sitting next to this one. A cylinder loses heat all day whether you shower or not, which is why a jacket and pipe lagging pays back faster than almost anything else in the house. Do that at the same time.

5. Worked example: Aoife in Shrewsbury

Aoife lives with her partner in a metered two-bedroom flat with a combi boiler and a mixer shower. She measures the flow the sensible way, with a two litre jug and a stopwatch, and finds the jug fills in 9.5 seconds. That is 12.6 litres a minute.

They shower once each daily, about seven minutes apiece. Current use is 88.2 litres per shower, so 2 × 365 × 88.2 = 64,386 litres a year.

At 0.7059p a litre for metered water plus gas heating, that shower habit costs them £454.51 a year. It is comfortably the most expensive thing either of them does with a tap.

She fits a £22 aerated head and re-measures: the jug now fills in 16.8 seconds, so 7.1 litres a minute. New use is 49.7 litres per shower, or 36,281 litres a year, costing £256.11.

Saving: £198.40 a year. £22 divided by £198.40 is 0.111 years, which is about six weeks.

Then she checks her water company's website and finds it offers a free water saving pack including a shower head to any customer who asks. She could have had it for nothing. She keeps the one she bought for the ensuite and orders the free one for the main bathroom, which is the correct response to discovering you have overpaid by twenty-two pounds.

The part she did not expect: the bathroom stopped steaming up so badly. Less hot water in the air means less condensation, which is the same mechanism described in our page on PIV units against dehumidifiers. The saving is money. The damp reduction is free on top.

6. The honest verdict

If you are on a water meter with a mixer shower, buy one today, and check first whether your water company will send you one free. At roughly 28p a shower saved it is the fastest payback on this site, measured in weeks rather than years.

If you are on a water meter with an electric shower, it is still worth it at about £122 a year, but buy the manufacturer's own low flow spray plate rather than a generic restrictor.

If you are unmetered with an electric shower, do not buy one. It will save you nothing at all. Spend the twenty-five pounds on something in the house that does something.

And whatever your situation, one minute less in the shower is worth about £62 a year and costs nothing.

If you are buying

Ask your water company first, because a large number of them post these out free. If you are buying, the number that matters is the stated flow rate in litres per minute, not the finish or the number of spray patterns. Anything at 8 litres a minute or below is doing the job.

Frequently asked

How do I measure my flow rate without any tools? Take a container you know the volume of, a two litre jug or a four pint milk bottle, hold it under the running shower at your normal temperature and time how long it takes to fill. Divide the volume in litres by the seconds, then multiply by 60. Anything over 10 litres a minute has room to improve.

Will a low flow head feel weak? An aerated head should not, because it mixes air into the stream and keeps the perceived pressure up while moving less water. A plain restrictor disc pushed into the hose will feel weaker. That is the difference worth paying for, and it is why the cheapest option in this category is often the one people take back off again.

Does this work with a gravity fed system? Be careful. Older systems fed by a tank in the loft run at low pressure by design, and some aerated heads need a minimum pressure to work properly. Look for a head specifically stated as suitable for low pressure or gravity systems, or you will fit it and find the shower has become a dribble.

Should I get a water meter to make this worthwhile? Possibly, and it is a separate decision worth doing properly. The rough rule is that if you have more bedrooms than people, a meter usually saves money. Most companies in England and Wales let you switch and change back within a set period, so it is a low risk thing to try.
