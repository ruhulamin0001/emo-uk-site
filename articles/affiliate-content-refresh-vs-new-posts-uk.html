Your Next Free Hour: Fix the Old Post or Write a New One?

This is the most common scheduling question in affiliate publishing and it is usually answered with instinct. It does not have to be. Google's own documentation rules out two of the popular answers explicitly, which narrows the decision to something you can make with your own data in about ten minutes.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Changing the date to look fresh is a named warning sign. Google's content documentation asks: are you changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed? Answering yes means reconsidering your approach.
  • Publishing volume to seem fresh does not work, and Google says so. On adding a lot of new content mainly because you think it makes the site seem fresh, the documentation adds the answer in brackets: No, it won't.
  • Neither does writing to a word count. Same page, same format: are you writing to a particular word count because you have heard Google has a preferred one? (No, we don't.)
  • Volume for its own sake is also flagged. Producing lots of content on many different topics in the hope some of it performs is listed as a warning sign, not a strategy.
  • What survives is substantial change. The word doing the work in the date clause is substantially. A refresh that changes what the page actually tells a reader is a different act from a date bump.
  • Trust outranks the rest. Google's own summary of E-E-A-T says of experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust that trust is most important.

1. Two answers Google rules out

Before deciding between refreshing and publishing, it is worth removing the two versions of each that do not work, because both are extremely common and both are named in Google's documentation on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content, last updated 10 December 2025.

The refresh that is only a date. Under the heading of warning signs that you should reevaluate how you are creating content, the page asks: "Are you changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed?"

The publishing plan that is only volume. On the same list: "Are you adding a lot of new content or removing a lot of older content primarily because you believe it will help your search rankings overall by somehow making your site seem 'fresh?'" Google then answers its own question in brackets: "(No, it won't)".

There is a third on the same list worth quoting because it kills a common piece of advice: "Are you writing to a particular word count because you've heard or read that Google has a preferred word count? (No, we don't.)"

And a fourth: "Are you producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results?"

Put those together and the shape of the answer appears. Google is not saying refreshing is bad or that publishing is bad. It is saying that the versions of both that are performed for the search engine rather than the reader do not work, and in one case it says so in brackets rather than leaving room for interpretation.

2. The word that decides it: substantially

The date clause hinges on four words: "has not substantially changed". That is the whole test, and it is a test about the reader, not the file.

A substantial change is one where a reader who read the old version and reads the new one would now do something different. Concretely, on an affiliate page, that is:

  • A price, rate or fee that has moved, where the old number would now send a reader to the wrong conclusion.
  • A recommendation that is no longer correct, because the product is discontinued, the terms changed or a better option exists.
  • A rule that changed. Anything on this site touching tax, benefits or programme terms falls here.
  • A question the page raised and never answered, which you can now answer.

Things that are not substantial changes, however long they take: rewording the introduction, swapping synonyms, adding a paragraph that restates the page, adding a new image, and adding the current year to the title.

That distinction is also the honest one, which matters more than the ranking argument. If you would not tell a reader "this changed, come back and look", the date should not say so either.

3. A ten minute test for any given page

Here is a sequence that resolves the question with your own data rather than a rule of thumb. It needs Search Console, which is free.

  1. Is the page getting impressions? If it is getting none at all, refreshing it is very unlikely to help, because nothing is being shown to anyone to improve on. That page is a candidate for deletion, consolidation or a genuine rewrite, not a refresh.
  2. Impressions but almost no clicks? The page is being shown and passed over. That is a title and snippet problem, and it is the single cheapest fix on this list, usually twenty minutes.
  3. Clicks but nobody goes further? The page is answering the search and then stopping. That is a content gap, and it is a genuine refresh: add the thing readers are still missing.
  4. Does the page contain a number that has moved? If yes, this is not a choice. Fix it today. A wrong figure on a money page is worse than any ranking question.
  5. None of the above apply and the page is fine? Then your hour goes on the new post, because there is nothing substantial to change here and a date bump is the thing Google names.

Notice that four of those five outcomes point at the existing page, and they do so for reasons that have nothing to do with freshness signals. They point there because the page has a specific, identifiable defect.

4. The hidden cost of the new post

The argument for always publishing new is that it is the only thing that adds surface area. That is true, and it is why the answer is never "refresh forever".

The cost that gets left out is that a new page starts with no inbound links from anywhere, including your own site, unless you deliberately give it some. A page that nothing links to is a page that crawlers reach late, if at all, and that is a mechanical problem rather than a quality one.

So the fair comparison is not "one hour refreshing" against "one hour writing". It is one hour refreshing against one hour writing plus fifteen minutes wiring the new page into the pages that already exist. If you are not spending that extra fifteen minutes, the new post is worth less than it looks on the publishing schedule.

This is also the point where the two options stop competing. The best use of a refresh is often to add a genuinely useful link from a strong old page into a weak new one, which improves the old page for the reader and solves the new page's biggest problem at the same time. Worked example: Priya in Leicester

Priya has 60 pages and one free hour on a Sunday. Her instinct is to write number 61.

She runs the test instead. Search Console shows three pages with impressions above 400 a month and click-through under 1%, and one page with a fee table she knows changed in April.

The fee table wins outright, and not on ranking grounds. It is currently telling readers something untrue, which is a different category of problem from everything else on the list.

That takes 25 minutes. With the remaining 35 she rewrites two of the three low click-through titles, because a title rewrite on a page already getting 400 impressions is the highest ratio of result to effort available to her that afternoon.

What she did not do is touch the other 56 pages or change any dates. Fifty-six of her pages had nothing substantially wrong with them, so there was nothing to refresh, and bumping their dates is the exact behaviour Google names.

Next Sunday she writes number 61, and spends the last fifteen minutes linking to it from three of the 60. The choice was never permanent. It was just this week's.

The honest verdict

Google has settled half of this argument directly. Changing dates without substantially changing content is a named warning sign, and adding content mainly to make a site look fresh is answered with "(No, it won't)". Writing to a word count gets the same treatment.

What is left is not a doctrine but a weekly triage. Fix anything that is factually wrong today, regardless. Then rewrite titles on pages that have impressions and no clicks, because that is the cheapest real gain available. Then, if nothing on the site has a specific defect, write the new page, and spend the last fifteen minutes linking to it from pages that already exist.

Where this does not help you: if your pages are getting no impressions at all, none of this triage applies, because there is no signal to work with. That is an indexing and linking problem and it needs solving before any refresh-or-publish question is worth asking.

Frequently asked

Does updating a post actually improve its ranking? Google's documentation does not promise that, and it explicitly rejects the version of it that is only a date change or only added volume. What it consistently describes as working is content that is genuinely more useful to a reader than it was. If your edit meets that bar, it is worth doing on its own merits.

Should I change the published date when I update a page? Only where the content has substantially changed. That is the test the documentation sets, and it is also the honest one. Many sites handle this by keeping the original published date and showing a separate last updated date, which tells a reader the truth about both.

How long should an affiliate post be? There is no target. Google's own list of warning signs includes writing to a particular word count because you have heard Google has a preferred one, followed by the answer (No, we don't). Length should be whatever answers the question and then stops.

Is it better to delete a weak old post or refresh it? Google lists removing a lot of older content primarily to make the site seem fresh as a warning sign, so deletion as a tactic is in the same category as publishing as a tactic. Delete a page because it is wrong, duplicated or genuinely unhelpful, not because removing pages is supposed to help.
