Fire and Rehire Protections Slipped to January 2027: What Changed, What Did Not, and What Still Lands in October

For most of this year the line was that fire and rehire would be all but banned from October 2026. It will not be. The government's updated Employment Rights Act timetable, published on 25 August 2026, lists fire and rehire protections under January 2027 instead. Here is what that means if your employer is changing your terms this autumn, what October still delivers, and the protection that already exists today and is worth more than most people realise.

The short version

  • The date moved. GOV.UK's "Plan to Make Work Pay and Employment Rights Act: timeline update", published 25 August 2026, lists "fire and rehire protections" under "Measures that will take effect in January 2027". They are not in the October 2026 lists.
  • October 2026 is not empty. From 1 October the Employment Tribunal time limit rises from 3 to 6 months. From 30 October employers must take all reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment, third-party harassment protection returns, and workers must be told of their right to join a union.
  • January 2027 now carries the big three together: fire and rehire protections, the unfair dismissal qualifying period falling from two years to six months for dismissals from 1 January 2027, and the removal of the cap on compensatory awards.
  • What the January rule does NOT do: it does not stop employers proposing changes, it does not stop you agreeing to them, and the Act keeps an exception for employers in real financial difficulty. It targets dismissal for refusing a change, not the change itself.
  • What already protects you today: the statutory Code of Practice on dismissal and re-engagement has applied since 18 July 2024. A tribunal can raise your compensation by up to 25% if an employer ignores it. Your existing unfair dismissal rights, with the two-year qualifying period, still apply this autumn.
  • Our verdict: if you are handed a new contract between now and Christmas, do not assume a ban protects you. Read the consultation paperwork, put your objection in writing, and check your start date against 1 January 2027.

1. What moved, and where GOV.UK says so

The Employment Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent in December 2025 and is being switched on in phases. The earlier roadmap grouped fire and rehire protections with the October 2026 measures, and most guides still carry that version. On 25 August 2026 the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade published a "timeline update" on GOV.UK. The October sections list the tribunal time limit, the union measures and the harassment duties. Fire and rehire is not there. It appears under "Measures that will take effect in January 2027", alongside the six-month unfair dismissal qualifying period.

The page says all future dates "remain subject to parliamentary processes and may change", meaning the regulations have not been laid yet, so January 2027 could slip again. We covered the October package, and the withdrawn tipping code, in our October 2026 employment law guide.

2. What October 2026 still delivers

The tribunal time limit is the sleeper. If a change is imposed in November and you want to argue it was a dismissal in all but name, you now have six months rather than three to claim. No new right, but it removes the commonest reason claims fail: running out of time while negotiating.

3. What the January 2027 protection will and will not do

What the Act does, once commenced, is make it automatically unfair to dismiss an employee because they refused to agree to a variation of their contract, or to dismiss and replace them with someone on the varied terms. Automatically unfair matters because it removes the employer's usual "some other substantial reason" defence, the one that has made fire and rehire lawful for decades. During the Bill's passage the scope was narrowed to what the Act calls restricted variations, broadly pay, hours, pension, holiday and shift patterns, rather than every clause in a contract.

Now the part the headlines leave out. The Act keeps an exception where the employer can show the variation was needed to eliminate, reduce or mitigate financial difficulties that were affecting its ability to carry on the business as a going concern, and that it could not reasonably have avoided the need to make the change. A business that is genuinely near the edge will still be able to use the process, provided it consults properly. The protection is aimed at profitable employers using dismissal as a bargaining chip, which is the P&O Ferries pattern that drove the legislation.

Three more things it does not do. It does not stop an employer proposing a change and asking you to agree. It does not apply retrospectively, so a dismissal in November 2026 is judged under current law even if heard in 2027. And it does nothing if no dismissal happens: an employer who simply imposes the change has committed a breach of contract, a different claim with different remedies.

4. What protects you today, this autumn

The statutory Code of Practice on dismissal and re-engagement has been in force since 18 July 2024. It requires an employer contemplating fire and rehire to consult for as long as reasonably possible, to share information, to treat dismissal as a last resort and not to use the threat of it as a negotiating tactic. It does not ban the practice, but a tribunal can increase compensation in a related claim by up to 25% where the employer unreasonably failed to follow it. Ordinary unfair dismissal law also still applies, with the current two-year qualifying period until the January change, and a dismissal for refusing new terms has to be procedurally fair and within the range of reasonable responses.

Practically, four things. Put your objection in writing, with reasons. Ask for the business case in writing; the Code expects it. Check the collective position, because 20 or more dismissals at one establishment triggers collective consultation and a protective award doubled from April 2026. And note your start date: on or after about 1 July 2026 means six months' service by 1 January 2027 and the new qualifying period covers you. Acas, Citizens Advice and your union are the free places to take it.

5. A worked example in Wolverhampton

Marcus works in a distribution warehouse in Wolverhampton on £27,500 a year, employed since March 2023. In late September 2026 his employer announces a move from fixed day shifts to a rotating pattern with two Saturdays a month, with the same annual pay, and says anyone who has not signed the new contract by 20 November will be given notice and offered re-engagement on the new terms.

Under the law this autumn, that is lawful if the employer consults properly and shows a sound business reason. The January 2027 rule would have made the dismissal automatically unfair, since shifts are a restricted variation and the firm is profitable, but notice given on 20 November is judged under the old law. What Marcus does have: three and a half years' service, so full unfair dismissal rights; the 2024 Code, with its 25% uplift if ignored; and, with 35 colleagues affected, collective consultation and a protective award of up to 180 days' pay if it is skipped. His best move is a written objection, a request for the business case and a word with the union rep. If the employer waits until January to dismiss, the calculation changes entirely, which is exactly why some will try to finish before Christmas.

6. The honest verdict

Fire and rehire is not banned this October, and it will not be banned outright in January either. What arrives in January 2027, if the timetable holds, is a rule that makes dismissing you for refusing a pay, hours or shift change automatically unfair unless the employer can prove genuine financial distress. That is a big shift in bargaining power, and it is three months later than you were told.

Until then, the tools are the ones that already exist: the 2024 Code with its 25% uplift, ordinary unfair dismissal rights if you have two years' service, collective consultation if 20 or more of you are affected, and, from 1 October, six months rather than three to bring a claim. If a new contract lands on your desk this autumn, do not sign on the day and do not assume a ban is coming to save you.

Frequently asked

Is fire and rehire banned from October 2026? No. GOV.UK's timeline update of 25 August 2026 lists fire and rehire protections under January 2027. October 2026 brings the longer tribunal time limit, the harassment duties and the union measures.

Will fire and rehire be illegal from January 2027? Not in every case. Dismissing an employee for refusing a restricted variation, such as pay, hours or shifts, becomes automatically unfair, but the Act keeps an exception for employers in genuine financial difficulty who could not reasonably avoid the change.

My employer is changing my contract now. What can I do? Object in writing, ask for the business case, check whether 20 or more colleagues are affected, and get free advice from Acas, Citizens Advice or a union. The 2024 Code of Practice already applies and a tribunal can add up to 25% to an award if it is ignored.

Does the January date affect the six-month unfair dismissal rule? They are listed together. The qualifying period drops to six months for dismissals from 1 January 2027, so anyone who started on or after roughly 1 July 2026 will be covered from day one of the new rule.
