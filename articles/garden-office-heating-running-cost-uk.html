Heating A Garden Office Costs About 89p A Working Day

Nobody selling garden rooms puts a running cost on the brochure, which is odd, because it is one of the few numbers you can work out exactly before you buy. Using one manufacturer's own published insulation figures and the October price cap, here is what a winter actually costs.

Ruhul Amin Published 11 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • A standard 4m by 3m insulated garden room loses about 28 watts for every degree of temperature difference. Heating it to 20 degrees through an English winter costs £133.76 a year, which is 89p per working day.
  • Full width bifold doors take that to £171 a year. Glazing is six times worse per square metre than the wall it replaces, and that penalty is permanent.
  • The insulation upgrade almost never pays for itself. Going from 50mm to 70mm saves £14.13 a year. On a £1,200 upgrade that is a payback of 85 years. Buy it for comfort, not for the bill.
  • The same 12 square metres inside the house costs about £28.78 a year to heat. The garden office is 4.6 times dearer, because it is electric and because all six of its surfaces face outdoors.
  • None of this is a reason not to buy one. It is a reason to spend the money on glazing you will actually use and a decent thermostat, rather than on insulation thickness.

1. The published spec, and why we can calculate this at all

Most garden room marketing talks about insulation in millimetres, which tells you nothing, or uses the phrase "fully insulated", which tells you less. A few manufacturers publish the actual thermal performance, and once you have that the running cost is arithmetic rather than opinion.

Warwick Buildings publishes a full set of calculated U-values on its own website, last updated in August 2026. A U-value is watts lost per square metre of surface for every degree of temperature difference, so lower is better. Their figures are:

  • Floors: 0.21 W/m²K
  • Standard walls, 50mm insulation: 0.29. Upgraded walls, 70mm: 0.21
  • Standard roof, 50mm insulation: 0.28. Upgraded roof, 70mm: 0.215
  • Glazing, using Pilkington K glass: 1.6

For context, the same page gives a Victorian solid brick wall at 2.1, an uninsulated cavity wall at 1.6 and a double glazed window at 2.8. So the walls of a decent garden room really are seven or eight times better than the walls of an older house. That part of the sales pitch is true.

The part that gets left out is that a garden room is a small box floating in cold air. Every one of its six surfaces is an external surface, where a spare bedroom has one external wall and borrows warmth from every room around it. Section five puts a number on that difference.

One legal note. A detached single storey building of 30 square metres or less with no sleeping accommodation is exempt from the Building Regulations under Schedule 2, Class 6 of the Building Regulations 2010. That is why manufacturers can specify whatever insulation they like, and why the published U-value is the only thing standing between you and a shed with a radiator in it.

2. Working out the heat loss, surface by surface

Take a common size: 4 metres by 3 metres, 12 square metres of floor, 2.4 metres internal height. The perimeter is 14 metres, so the gross wall area is 33.6 square metres. Assume a sensible glazed area of 6 square metres, which is a door and a decent window, leaving 27.6 square metres of solid wall.

Multiply each area by its U-value to get watts per degree:

  • Floor: 12 × 0.21 = 2.52 W/K
  • Roof: 12 × 0.28 = 3.36 W/K
  • Walls: 27.6 × 0.29 = 8.00 W/K
  • Glazing: 6 × 1.6 = 9.60 W/K

That is 23.48 watts per degree through the fabric. Then add ventilation. The internal volume is 28.8 cubic metres, and at a modest half an air change an hour the loss is 28.8 × 0.5 × 0.33 = 4.75 W/K. Total: 28.23 watts per degree.

Look at that list again. The glazing is 18 per cent of the surface area and 41 per cent of the fabric heat loss. It is the single largest item, larger than the walls, roof and floor put together. Hold that thought.

To turn watts per degree into pounds, you need three more assumptions, all stated openly. Inside temperature 20 degrees. Average outside temperature across the English heating season, roughly October to April, 5 degrees, so a difference of 15. And you use it for 8 hours a day, 5 days a week, for 30 weeks, which is 150 working days.

28.23 × 15 = 423.5 watts while you are sitting in it. Over 8 hours that is 3.388 kWh a day, and over 150 days it is 508 kWh a year. At the Ofgem price cap electricity rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 26.32p per kWh, that is £133.76 a year, or 89p a working day.

Upgrading the insulation saves £14.13 a year, which is nowhere near enough to justify what it costs. Which brings us to the next section.

4. Why the insulation upgrade takes 85 years to pay back

This is the payback calculation the rest of the article exists to set up, so here it is in the plainest form.

Upgrade cost, divided by annual saving, equals payback in years.

Going from 50mm to 70mm of insulation in the walls and roof is typically offered as an option costing somewhere between £800 and £1,500 on a building of this size, depending on the maker and the spec. Take the middle at £1,200.

£1,200 divided by £14.13 a year is 85 years. At the cheaper end, £800, it is 57 years. At the dearer end, £1,500, it is 106 years.

The building will not last 85 years. Timber cladding, properly maintained, is generally quoted at up to sixty. So on energy grounds alone, the insulation upgrade does not pay back, ever.

That does not make it a bad purchase. Better insulation means the room warms faster, holds temperature when the heater cycles off, and runs a lower condensation risk, which is the thing that quietly ruins garden rooms. Those are worth paying for. They are just not worth paying for on the basis of the electricity bill, and if a salesperson says the upgrade "pays for itself", you now have the arithmetic to disagree politely.

Contrast that with loft insulation and draught proofing, which pay back in single figures of years. Insulation pays when what you are replacing is terrible, not when it is already good.

5. The comparison nobody makes: the spare bedroom

If the alternative to a garden office is working at the kitchen table, the running cost is the whole extra cost and 89p a day is the number. If the alternative is converting the spare bedroom, the comparison is very different.

A 12 square metre bedroom in a typical cavity walled semi has one external wall of about 7.2 square metres, of which 1.5 is window. At a cavity wall U-value of 1.6 and a double glazed window at 2.8, plus the same ventilation allowance, that room loses about 18.07 watts per degree, against 28.23 for the garden room. The bigger difference is the fuel: the bedroom runs on gas at 7.97p per kWh against the garden room's 26.32p. Combine both effects and the spare bedroom costs roughly £28.78 a year to the garden room's £133.76.

So the garden office costs 4.6 times as much to heat as the same floor area indoors, an extra £105 a year. That is small enough that it should not decide anything. If a garden office gets you a door that closes, £105 is not the deciding factor.

If you are self employed and the room is genuinely used for work, look at what you can claim before you build, because the tax treatment of the structure and of the running costs are different and both are easy to get wrong. Our page on working from home tax relief covers who can claim what.

6. Worked example: Priya in Chesham

Priya runs a small bookkeeping practice from home in Chesham and is quoted for a 4m by 3m room. The base spec has 50mm insulation and a single glazed door with a window. The showroom pushes two upgrades: 70mm insulation at £1,150, and full width bifolds at £2,400 over the standard door.

She works out what each does to her bill. The insulation upgrade saves £14.13 a year, so on £1,150 it pays back in 81 years. The bifolds cost her £37.24 a year on top of their £2,400 price, so over ten years the doors really cost £2,772.

She takes the bifolds anyway, because she has clients in and the room needs to look like somewhere a professional works. She declines the insulation upgrade and puts £180 of the saving into a panel heater with a proper programmable thermostat instead of the basic one offered.

That last swap is the one that actually pays. Her old plan was on at 8am and off at 6pm, ten hours. With a thermostat and a timer she preheats for forty minutes and then lets it cycle, cutting her heated hours from ten to roughly eight. On the bifold build that is £213.75 a year at ten hours against £171.00 at eight. A saving of £42.75 a year on a £180 thermostat is a payback of 4.2 years, and it is the only upgrade in the quotation that pays for itself inside the life of the building. The same pattern shows up in heating controls and smart thermostats: the control is worth more than the kit.

7. The honest verdict

Budget about £135 a year to heat a standard 12 square metre garden office, or about £170 if you want the full width glass. That is 89p to £1.14 per working day, and for most people it is not a large enough number to change the decision.

Do not buy the thicker insulation to save money. It saves around £14 a year and costs around £1,200, which is a payback of roughly 85 years. Buy it for comfort, faster warm up and lower condensation risk, and be honest with yourself that those are the reasons.

Do buy the proper thermostat and timer. It is the cheapest item on the quotation and the only one with a payback inside five years.

And know that the glass is the thermal decision, not the walls. Every extra square metre of glazing adds about £6 a year to the bill forever. Choose it with your eyes open and enjoy it.

If you are buying

Ask any manufacturer for their calculated U-values for walls, roof, floor and glazing in writing before you sign. If they cannot give you four numbers, they have not done the calculation, and "fully insulated" is a description of an intention rather than a performance. The heater matters far less than the control on it.

Frequently asked

Would an air source heat pump be cheaper than a panel heater? On paper yes, because a small heat pump delivers roughly three units of heat per unit of electricity, which would cut the £133.76 to nearer £45. In practice a mini split unit plus installation for a room this size is a four figure cost, so the payback on £89 a year saved is ten to fifteen years. Worth pricing if you also want cooling in summer.

Is infrared cheaper to run than a panel heater? No, and anyone who says otherwise is selling infrared. A kilowatt hour of electricity produces a kilowatt hour of heat whatever the emitter. Infrared can feel warmer sooner because it heats you before the air, which may let you set a lower air temperature, but the unit rate is identical. See our comparison of infrared panels against electric radiators.

Will it be freezing in January? Not at this spec, but it takes longer to warm than you expect, because you are heating a cold box from scratch rather than topping up a house that never fully cools. That is why the timer is the upgrade worth buying.
