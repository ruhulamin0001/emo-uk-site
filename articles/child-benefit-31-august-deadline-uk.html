Your Child Benefit Stops on 31 August Unless You Tell HMRC, and There Is No Reminder

HMRC wrote to around 1.5 million parents this summer. As of 17 August, just over 372,000 had responded. If your teenager is starting sixth form, college or approved training in September, you have until 31 August 2026 to extend the claim or the payments stop by themselves.

The short version

  • The deadline is 31 August 2026. Payments for 16 to 19-year-olds stop automatically after that date if you have not told HMRC your teenager is staying in approved education or training.
  • Child Benefit is £27.05 a week for the eldest or only child and £17.90 a week for each additional child from 6 April 2026. That is £1,406.60 and £930.80 a year.
  • You only need to act if your teenager is starting a new course or approved training in September. If they are partway through a course HMRC already knows about, do nothing.
  • University degrees, standard apprenticeships in England, and courses that form part of a job contract do not qualify. If that is your teenager, there is nothing here for you to claim.
  • If you miss it, Child Benefit can only be backdated three months. Everything before that window is gone permanently, and no appeal recovers it.

1. What actually happens on 31 August

Child Benefit runs automatically until 31 August after a child turns 16. After that it only continues if HMRC has been told the young person is staying in full-time, non-advanced approved education or training.

The part that catches people out is the mechanism. HMRC does not chase you, does not phone, and does not run a grace period. If you have not responded by 31 August 2026, the payments simply stop. Most parents find out weeks later when the money does not land.

HMRC says it wrote to roughly 1.5 million parents this year. By 17 August, more than 372,000 had extended digitally. That leaves a very large number of households who either do not need to act or have not yet realised they do.

There is no reminder and no grace period. The system is designed to switch off by default, and the burden of proving your teenager is still studying sits entirely with you.

2. Who must act, and who genuinely does not

You need to extend if your teenager is starting a new course or approved training in September 2026 in full-time, non-advanced education. That means A levels, T levels, Scottish Highers, NVQs up to level 3, home schooling, and certain approved training schemes. Full-time means more than an average of 12 hours a week of supervised study or course-related work experience. Young people with an illness or disability may qualify on fewer hours.

You do not need to do anything if your teenager is already partway through a course you previously told HMRC about. HMRC has that on record.

This does not help you at all if your teenager is starting a university degree, a standard apprenticeship in England, or a course that forms part of a job contract. None of those qualify. There is no form to fill in and no money to chase. Close the tab.

One more obligation runs the other way. If your teenager's plans change later, for example they drop out in November or take a paid apprenticeship, you must tell HMRC straight away. If you do not, you will be overpaid and HMRC will ask for it back.

3. The number almost every article gets wrong

Nearly every write-up of this deadline says the same thing: you could lose £1,406.60 a year. That is the correct headline rate, but for most families it is not what actually goes missing, and the difference is large.

Child Benefit pays the higher rate to the eldest qualifying child. If your 16-year-old drops out of the claim, your next child does not stay on the lower rate. They move up to £27.05. So the real loss is usually the gap between the two rates, not the full headline figure.

This matters because it changes how urgent this feels for a family with three children versus a family with one. It also means single-child households have the most to lose, which is the opposite of what people assume.

Worked example: the Whitmore household

Ellie is 16 and starting A levels in September. Her brother Tom is 13. Right now the family receives £27.05 for Ellie plus £17.90 for Tom, so £44.95 a week, or £2,337.40 a year.

They miss the deadline. Ellie comes off the claim after 31 August. Tom becomes the eldest qualifying child and moves up to £27.05. The household now gets £27.05 a week.

The real loss is £17.90 a week, not £27.05. That is £930.80 a year, which is still serious money, but it is a third less than the figure in the headlines.

They notice in March 2027. They extend, and HMRC backdates three months to early December. The roughly thirteen weeks from September to early December are gone for good: 13 × £17.90 = £232.70, permanently.

A single-child household in the same position loses 13 × £27.05, which is £351.65 permanently. Five minutes of admin in August is worth several hundred pounds.

4. If you miss it: the three month wall

This is the rule that decides how expensive the mistake is. Child Benefit can be backdated a maximum of three months from the date you make or restore the claim. Anything older than that cannot be recovered.

So the cost is not fixed. It depends entirely on how quickly you notice. Miss the deadline and spot it in October, and you lose almost nothing. Miss it and spot it the following summer, and you have lost the best part of nine months of payments with no route to get them back.

Practical version: if you think you might have missed it, check now. Every week you wait past the three month window converts recoverable money into lost money.

5. If you earn over £60,000

The High Income Child Benefit Charge applies when the higher earner in the household has an adjusted net income above £60,000. You repay 1% of the Child Benefit for every £200 of income above that, so the entire award is clawed back by £80,000. Both thresholds are frozen.

What that means in practice:

  • Under £60,000. You keep the lot. Extend the claim.
  • Between £60,000 and £80,000. You keep part of it. Still worth extending, because a partial payment beats nothing.
  • Comfortably over £80,000. The charge cancels the payment out entirely. Be honest about this: for a 16 to 19-year-old there is no hidden benefit to protect either, because Child Benefit National Insurance credits only accrue while a child is under 12. If you have opted out of payments, extending changes nothing financially.

One thing worth knowing if you are just over the line. Adjusted net income is measured after pension contributions, so paying more into a workplace pension can bring you back under £60,000 and restore some or all of the benefit. 6. What to do, in about five minutes

  1. Work out whether you need to act. New course starting in September means yes. Continuing a course HMRC already knows about means no. Degree, English apprenticeship or a course tied to a job contract means no, and nothing to claim.
  2. Open the HMRC app or go to GOV.UK. The service for 16 to 19-year-olds is at gov.uk/child-benefit-16-19. If you received the letter, the QR code on it goes straight there.
  3. Have the course details to hand. The institution and what they are studying is enough.
  4. If you cannot use the app or the website, extend by post or phone using the contact details on the HMRC letter.
  5. Diarise it. If your teenager stays in approved education, this comes round again. Also set a note to tell HMRC promptly if they leave, to avoid an overpayment you have to repay.

There is nothing to buy here and no service worth paying for. Anyone charging a fee to "process" a Child Benefit extension is selling you a free government form.

Frequently asked

What is the exact deadline? 31 August 2026. Payments stop automatically after that date if HMRC has not been told the young person is continuing in approved education or training.

My teenager is retaking GCSEs at college. Does that count? Retakes at a school or college are non-advanced education, so generally yes, provided it is more than an average of 12 hours a week of supervised study. Check the approved list on GOV.UK for your specific course.

They are doing an apprenticeship. Do I get anything? Not for a standard apprenticeship in England, and not for any course that forms part of a job contract. Some approved training schemes do qualify, so check the GOV.UK list rather than assuming either way.

I already missed a deadline in a previous year. Can I still fix it? You can restore the claim, but backdating is capped at three months from the date you claim. Anything older is not recoverable.
