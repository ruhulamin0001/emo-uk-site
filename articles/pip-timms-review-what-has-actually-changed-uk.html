Nothing about your PIP has changed, and the review's own report says so

There is a great deal of frightening content about PIP in circulation and very little of it quotes the actual document. Here is what the interim report says, what the rates are today, and when anything could realistically move.

Ruhul Amin Published 12 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The rates are unchanged. GOV.UK today: daily living £76.70 or £114.60 a week, mobility £30.30 or £80 a week.
  • PIP is tax free and is not affected by your income or savings. You can get it while working.
  • The interim report is not a decision. It is a progress update from the steering group, updated 15 July 2026.
  • The call for evidence drew 38,713 responses, open from 19 March 2026 for 10 weeks, closing 28 May 2026.
  • Recommendations are due to the Secretary of State by autumn 2026, and it is then for the Secretary of State to decide what happens to them.

1. What PIP pays today

Start here, because almost nothing written about the review bothers to state it. Per GOV.UK today:

  • Daily living part: £76.70 a week at the lower rate, £114.60 at the higher rate.
  • Mobility part: £30.30 a week at the lower rate, £80 at the higher rate.

GOV.UK also confirms three things that get muddled constantly. PIP is tax free. The amount is not affected by your income or your savings. And you can get it while working, with savings, or alongside most other benefits. It is usually paid every four weeks.

Someone on both higher rates receives £194.60 a week, or £778.40 per four week payment. None of these figures has been altered by the review.

2. What the interim report is, and is not

The Timms Review published an interim report as a Department for Work and Pensions policy paper, updated 15 July 2026. Be precise about what kind of document that is.

It is a progress update from the review's steering group, written on the way to a set of recommendations. It covers the approach to co-production, emerging themes, call for evidence findings, the workplan and next steps. It contains no changed rates, no new eligibility test and no commencement date, because an interim report is not a legal instrument and cannot contain any of those things.

The evidence base is substantial. The call for evidence launched on 19 March 2026, ran for 10 weeks and closed on 28 May 2026, drawing 38,713 responses from disabled people and people with long term health conditions, carers, charities, clinicians, academics and think tanks. The report notes that almost one in four people of working age have a disability.

3. The timetable, in the review's own words

Paragraph 67 is the sentence that should be quoted in every article on this subject and almost never is. As committed to in the review's Terms of Reference, the steering group will report its recommendations to the Secretary of State by autumn 2026. It will then be for the Secretary of State to decide how those recommendations are taken forward.

Paragraph 240 adds that the primary focus during August and September was synthesising the evidence into a co-produced set of recommendations.

So the chain is: recommendations to the Secretary of State by autumn 2026, then a government decision, then, for anything that changes entitlement, legislation through Parliament. Three separate steps, each with its own timetable, and none has happened. That is why "PIP is being scrapped in November" content is not merely premature. It is describing a step that has no date because the step before it has not been taken.

4. What is actually worth doing now

Denise, Carlisle. Standard daily living rate, £76.70 a week, for three years, with an award review letter expected next spring. Her condition has worsened since the original assessment and she now needs help preparing a meal most days, which she did not then. She has spent six weeks worrying about the review and none of it on her own claim.

The arithmetic she is ignoring: enhanced daily living is £114.60 against £76.70, a difference of £37.90 a week, or £1,970.80 a year. That is larger than anything the review could plausibly do to her in the next twelve months, and it turns entirely on reporting a change she has already had.

  • Report a genuine change. GOV.UK says to tell DWP straight away if your circumstances change, or if the way your condition affects you changes. Awards turn on how difficult you find daily living and mobility tasks, not on a diagnosis.
  • Keep a record. Dates, what you could not do, who helped. A note written at the time is worth more than a recollection.
  • Use a free named service. Citizens Advice, a local welfare rights service through your council, or a condition specific charity. Do not pay anyone a percentage of your award.
  • Do not act on a rumoured rule. Nothing changes until the Secretary of State decides and, where entitlement is affected, Parliament legislates.

The verdict

As of today, the Timms Review has produced an interim report and nothing else. The rates are what they were. The eligibility rules are what they were. The review's own text commits only to recommendations reaching the Secretary of State by autumn 2026.

If you are worried about PIP, the best use of that worry is your own claim: whether the award still reflects how your condition affects you now, and whether you have told DWP about anything that has changed. That is a real decision with a real number attached to it, and it does not depend on a report nobody has read yet.

Frequently asked

Is PIP being abolished? No decision of any kind has been made. The steering group reports recommendations to the Secretary of State by autumn 2026, and any change to entitlement would then need the government to act and, where required, Parliament to legislate.

Will my existing award be reassessed because of the review? The interim report announces no reassessment exercise. Award reviews continue as normal on the dates in your own decision letter.

Does having savings affect PIP? No. GOV.UK states the amount is not affected by your income or savings, and that you can get PIP even if you are working.
