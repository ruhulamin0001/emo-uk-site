Almost every guide to earning with AI is written for Americans, and the UK reader who follows one ends up with a US client, a dollar invoice, and a choice of three ways to get paid that differ by more than GBP 50 on a single payment. This guide takes one USD 1,000 invoice through PayPal, Stripe and Wise using the fee pages each company publishes for UK users, converts at the rate HMRC publishes, and shows what reaches the bank. It ends with the rule for putting the income on a tax return.

1. Start with the exchange rate

Before any fee, a dollar payment has to become pounds, and the rate used decides more of the outcome than most of the fees do. There is no single correct rate on a given day, but there is a public one that a UK freelancer can point to: HMRC publishes a monthly exchange rate table for customs valuation, fixed on the penultimate Thursday of the previous month. For September 2026 it gives USD 1.3554 to GBP 1, published on 19 August 2026. At that rate USD 1,000 is GBP 737.79. Every provider below applies its fees to that starting point, and every one of them converts at its own rate, which is where a hidden cost lives.

Two of the three companies say plainly how they set their rate. Wise's pricing page says it only uses the mid-market rate, the one you can check on Google, and charges its fee separately. PayPal's UK fee page says that for converting a business balance the rate is 3% above the base exchange rate. Stripe's page lists 2% for conversion on top of the card fee. That is the first thing to look for on any provider's page: not the headline percentage, but the words next to the word conversion.

2. PayPal, the one most clients suggest

PayPal's UK merchant fee page, read on 5 September 2026, sets the standard rate for receiving a domestic commercial transaction at 2.9% plus a fixed fee that depends on the currency received: USD 0.30 for dollars, GBP 0.30 for pounds. For a payment from outside the UK an additional percentage applies on top: 1.29% where the sender is in the EEA, 1.99% for all other markets, which includes the United States. Then, when you convert the dollar balance to pounds, or when you accept a payment where you have agreed to bear the conversion, the rate is 3% above PayPal's base exchange rate. Withdrawing to a UK bank account is free when no conversion is involved.

On USD 1,000 from a US client: 2.9% is USD 29.00, the fixed fee is USD 0.30, and the international 1.99% is USD 19.90, so USD 49.20 comes off and USD 950.80 remains. Converting that at a rate 3% worse than the HMRC figure gives GBP 680.44. The total cost is GBP 57.35, or 7.8% of the invoice. PayPal's real base rate on the day may differ from HMRC's, so treat the last pound or two as illustrative; the structure of the fees is exactly as published.

3. Stripe, the one you control

Stripe suits a freelancer who sends invoices or sells a fixed-price service from a page, because the client pays by card and you never hold a dollar balance. Stripe's UK pricing page lists 1.5% plus 20p for standard UK cards, 2.5% plus 20p for European Economic Area cards, and 3.15% plus 20p for other international cards, each with a further 2% if currency conversion is required. There is no monthly fee and payouts to a UK bank account on the standard schedule are free.

A US client paying a USD 1,000 invoice by card is an international card with conversion: 3.15% plus 2% is 5.15%, which on GBP 737.79 is GBP 38.00, plus the 20p fixed fee, so GBP 38.20 comes off and GBP 699.59 lands. That is 5.2% of the invoice. The way to cut it is to invoice in pounds, which removes the 2% conversion line and leaves 3.15% plus 20p, or GBP 23.44 on the same value, but it moves the currency risk onto the client, and some US clients will refuse.

4. Wise, the one that costs least

A Wise account gives you local account details in several currencies, including US dollar details, so a US client can pay you the way they would pay an American supplier. Wise's UK pricing page says getting account details to receive in 22 currencies is free, receiving domestic non-wire payments in USD is free, and receiving a USD wire or Swift payment carries a fixed fee of USD 6.11. Converting money is listed as from 0.24%, varying by currency, at the mid-market rate, with the exact fee shown before you confirm.

On USD 1,000 sent as a domestic US bank payment: nothing comes off on receipt, and converting GBP 737.79 at the 0.24% floor costs GBP 1.77, leaving GBP 736.02. If the client insists on sending a wire, the USD 6.11 fee reduces that to about GBP 731.51. If the actual dollar-to-pound fee on the day is higher than the floor, say 0.5%, the figure is GBP 734.10. Whichever it is, Wise leaves more than GBP 30 a payment more than Stripe and more than GBP 50 more than PayPal, because it charges a small fee once rather than a percentage twice.

6. A worked example in Sunderland

Marcus in Sunderland edits and fact-checks blog posts for a US software company, using Claude to draft summaries and flag inconsistencies before he reads each piece. The client pays USD 1,000 a month and, like most, first offered PayPal. Through PayPal he would receive about GBP 680.44 a month, or GBP 8,165 a year. Through Stripe, invoicing in dollars, about GBP 699.59 a month, or GBP 8,395 a year. Through Wise with US account details, about GBP 736.02 a month, or GBP 8,832 a year.

The difference between the client's first suggestion and the cheapest route is GBP 667 a year, which is more than three years of the Claude Pro subscription he uses to do the work, priced in the Claude plans guide. He asks the client's accounts team whether they can pay a US bank account by ACH; they can, because to them it is a domestic payment. It takes one email.

Marcus is well above the GBP 1,000 trading allowance, so he registered for Self Assessment by the 5 October after the tax year he started. His profits will pass GBP 12,570, so Class 4 National Insurance applies at 6% on profits above that up to GBP 50,270 for 2026 to 2027, and Class 2 is treated as paid because his profits exceed GBP 7,105. 7. Recording dollar income for HMRC

Your tax return is in pounds. The income you declare is the sterling value of what you earned, and the practical question is which rate to use to convert a dollar invoice. HMRC publishes monthly and yearly average rates and the monthly table above is the September 2026 one; using the published rate for the month the income arose, consistently, is a defensible method that is easy to evidence. Whatever you do, do the same thing for every invoice and write down which rate you used.

Keep three things for each payment: the invoice showing the dollar amount, the provider's record showing the fees and the sterling received, and a note of the rate. The provider fees are a business expense, so the difference between the invoice value and what landed is deductible; do not just declare what landed and forget the fee, and do not declare the invoice value and forget to deduct the fee. If you are near the trading allowance, remember that the allowance is measured against gross income, the invoice value in sterling, not against what reached your bank. And if you claim Universal Credit, HMRC's guidance says the trading allowance does not reduce your income for UC purposes, and self-employed earnings are reported monthly to DWP as received.

The honest verdict

If a US client will pay a US bank account, open a Wise account, give them the dollar details, and convert to pounds yourself; on the published fees it leaves about GBP 736 of every USD 1,000 against about GBP 680 through PayPal. Use Stripe when you need a card checkout or an invoice link and price it in pounds if the client will wear it. Accept PayPal only when it is the only way a client will pay, and treat its 7.8% as the cost of that client. Whichever route, convert income at a published rate, keep the fee records, and claim the fees as an expense.

Frequently asked

Why does PayPal cost so much more than Wise? Because it charges three times on one payment: 2.9% plus USD 0.30 to receive, 1.99% more because the sender is outside the EEA, and 3% above its base exchange rate to convert. Wise charges nothing to receive a domestic US bank payment and a single conversion fee from 0.24% at the mid-market rate. Both figures are from the companies' own UK pages, read 5 September 2026.

Is it better to invoice in pounds or dollars? In pounds if the client will accept it, because the 2% conversion line disappears on Stripe and the client's bank does the converting. Many US clients will only pay in dollars, in which case Wise's dollar account details are the cheapest route.

Which exchange rate do I use for my tax return? The income is declared in sterling. HMRC publishes monthly and yearly rates and using the published rate for the relevant period, consistently, is a defensible method. Keep a note of the rate with each invoice.

Are the payment fees tax deductible? Yes, as a business expense. Declare the sterling value of the invoice as income and deduct the fees, keeping the provider's record as evidence. If you use the GBP 1,000 trading allowance instead of expenses, remember it is measured against gross income, not what landed.
