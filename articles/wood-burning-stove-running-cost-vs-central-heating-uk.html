Logs Must Cost Under £126 A Cubic Metre To Beat Mains Gas. Most Cost £160 To £230

A stove is sold as a way of cutting the heating bill. Whether it does comes down to one number you can check with a phone call to a log merchant, and for most households buying wood at retail the answer is no.

The short version

  • Kiln dried hardwood holds about 4,500 to 5,000 kWh a tonne, so about 4.75 kWh a kilogram at 15 to 20 per cent moisture.
  • A loose cubic metre weighs roughly 375kg, so it carries about 1,781 kWh of gross energy.
  • At £190 a cubic metre through an 80 per cent stove that is 13.3p a useful kWh. Mains gas through a 90 per cent boiler is 8.86p.
  • Break-even log price is about £126 a cubic metre delivered. Below that wood beats gas. Above it, gas wins.
  • Against direct electric heating at 26.32p a kWh the stove does win, roughly halving the cost of the heat, but a £3,000 installation still takes about 15 years to pay back.

1. How much energy is actually in a log

Firewood is sold by volume and burns by mass, which is why the arithmetic confuses people. Three numbers turn a cubic metre into kilowatt hours.

Calorific value. Kiln dried logs at 7 to 15 per cent moisture are quoted at 4,500 to 5,000 kWh a tonne. Take 4,750, which is 4.75 kWh a kilogram. Individual species vary: dry oak is around 5.4 kWh a kilogram and ash around 5.2, but a mixed hardwood load averages out near the middle of the range.

Moisture matters enormously. Wet or unseasoned wood at 40 per cent moisture drops to roughly half that, because a large part of the heat goes into boiling the water out of the log before anything reaches the room. It also tars up the flue. Anything sold as ready to burn in England must now be under 20 per cent moisture, and this arithmetic assumes you comply.

Density in the load. A loose cubic metre of split hardwood logs is mostly air. Working stacked density for kiln dried hardwood comes out around 375kg to the loose cubic metre. This is where merchants differ most and where you should ask the question directly.

Multiply: 375 kilograms times 4.75 kWh is 1,781 kWh of gross energy in a cubic metre.

The break-even row is the one to remember. £126 a cubic metre delivered. Ring a merchant, ask their price a loose cubic metre delivered and stacked, and you have your answer in one call.

3. What the installation costs, and why it is most of the decision

The stove is the cheap part. A competent 5kW multifuel stove is £600 to £1,200. Everything round it is where the money goes.

Flue liner and installation, £1,200 to £2,500. An existing chimney almost always needs a stainless liner, and the work must be signed off, either by a HETAS registered installer or by building control. Unsigned work is a problem when you sell the house and a bigger problem with your insurer.

Hearth and surround, £200 to £800. There are minimum dimensions and non-combustible requirements.

Carbon monoxide alarm, mandatory, about £25.

Annual sweep, £60 to £90. This is a running cost and it belongs in the sum. On the arithmetic above, £75 of sweeping is equivalent to about 850 kWh of gas, so it eats a meaningful share of any saving.

Total, realistically, £2,500 to £4,000. Now the payback question. If you are on mains gas, there is no annual saving to divide into, because the wood costs more per unit of heat. The payback is undefined, which is a polite way of saying never.

If you are heating one room with an electric heater, there is a saving. Say you run a 2kW heater five hours a day for 150 days: 1,500 kWh at 26.32p is £394.80. The same 1,500 kWh of useful heat from a stove needs 1,875 kWh of wood, which at £190 a cubic metre is £200.06, plus £75 of sweeping, so £275.06. Saving £119.74 a year. £3,000 divided by £119.74 is 25 years.

That is not an investment. It is a purchase you make for other reasons.

4. The case where a stove genuinely saves money

There is one, and it is not marginal. It is when the wood is close to free.

If you have a supply of seasoned wood at £60 to £80 a cubic metre, or you are cutting and seasoning your own from land you have access to, the useful cost drops to 4.2p to 5.6p a kWh and the stove beats mains gas comfortably. Rural households with a woodpile have always known this and the arithmetic backs them up.

Two warnings attached to that. First, seasoning takes eighteen months to two years under cover with airflow, and burning it early destroys the economics and the flue at the same time. Buy a moisture meter, they are about £15, and do not burn anything over 20 per cent.

Second, "free" pallets and scrap timber are not free heat. Treated, painted or glued timber releases compounds you do not want in the room or the liner, and it can void the installation warranty. Untreated softwood offcuts are fine and burn fast.

The other genuine case is resilience rather than money. A stove works in a power cut, which a gas boiler with an electric pump and electronic ignition does not. If you are somewhere that loses power regularly in winter, that is worth something, and it is honest to call it what it is rather than dressing it as a saving.

5. Worked example: Marguerite in Bideford

Marguerite has an oil boiler she rarely runs and heats her sitting room in the evenings with a 2kW convector. She is quoted £3,400 for a 5kW stove, a liner and a slate hearth.

Her electric heating of that room is about 1,400 kWh a winter, so £368.48 at 26.32p.

She has been quoted £175 a cubic metre delivered for kiln dried hardwood, which is 9.83p a gross kWh, or 12.29p a useful kWh through the stove. Her 1,400 kWh of heat costs £172.06 in wood plus £70 for the sweep, so £242.06.

Saving: £126.42 a year. £3,400 divided by £126.42 is 26.9 years.

Then she rings a second merchant who quotes £120 a cubic metre for two-year seasoned hardwood, collected rather than delivered, because she has a trailer. That is 6.74p gross, 8.43p useful. Her 1,400 kWh now costs £118.02 plus the sweep, so £188.02. Saving £180.46 a year, payback 18.8 years.

Still long. What actually decides it for her is that the stove will heat that room to a standard the convector never managed, and she has decided she is buying comfort at roughly £3,400. That is a defensible thing to buy. It is not a saving.

6. The honest verdict

If you are on mains gas and buying logs at retail, a wood burning stove will not save you money. At £190 a cubic metre wood costs about half as much again as gas per unit of heat, before you have paid for the installation or the annual sweep. Buy one because you want a fire, and be honest with yourself that the bill goes up.

If you can get seasoned wood under £126 a cubic metre, or you cut your own, the arithmetic flips and the stove wins. Ring a merchant, ask the price a loose cubic metre delivered, and put it against that number. That single phone call is the whole decision.

And never run an open fire and call it heating. At 25 per cent efficiency it is the most expensive heat in this table by a distance, including the electric heater.

Frequently asked

Is it still legal to install a wood burning stove? Yes, everywhere in the UK, but with conditions. New stoves must meet the Ecodesign standard, wood sold in small volumes in England must be certified under 20 per cent moisture, and in a Smoke Control Area you may only use a Defra exempt appliance. Check your council's Smoke Control Area map before you buy anything.

How much wood does a winter take? For a 5kW stove used most evenings from November to March, plan on two to three loose cubic metres. At £190 that is £380 to £570 a year, which is the number to hold in your head rather than the price of a single bag.

Are nets and bags of logs from garages cheaper or dearer? Far dearer. A small net that looks like £6 works out at several times the bulk price per cubic metre. If you burn regularly, buy by the cubic metre from a merchant and stack it yourself.

Does a stove with a back boiler change the sums? Yes, meaningfully, because it puts heat into radiators and hot water instead of one room, so more of the wood does useful work. It also roughly doubles the installation cost and ties the stove into the heating system. Worth pricing separately if you are off the gas grid.
