An American Fridge Freezer Costs About £29 A Year More To Run, And £435 Over Its Life

The fridge freezer is the one appliance that never switches off, so a small difference in annual consumption compounds for fifteen years. Here is what the American style premium actually is, and the three practical costs nobody mentions in the showroom.

The short version

  • American style units use roughly 300 to 450 kWh a year. A standard 50:50 fridge freezer uses roughly 250 to 280.
  • Take midpoints, 375 against 265, and the gap is 110 kWh a year. At 26.32p that is £28.95 a year.
  • Over a fifteen year life that is £434.28, on top of a purchase price typically several hundred pounds higher.

1. Read the kWh, not the letter

Under the current UK energy labelling rules a fridge freezer's A to G grade is calculated against a benchmark that scales with the appliance's volume. That is a reasonable way to compare like with like, and a completely useless way to compare a 200 litre unit with a 550 litre one.

The consequence is one that catches people out constantly. A large American style fridge freezer can carry a better letter than a slim 50:50 and still use considerably more electricity a year, because it is being graded against other large appliances. The grade tells you how efficient it is for its size. Your bill does not care about its size.

Every label also prints an annual consumption figure in kilowatt hours per year, usually in the middle of the label. That number is measured under a standard test and it is directly comparable between any two appliances. It is the only number on the label worth writing down.

Multiply it by 26.32p, the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, and you have the annual running cost. A 265 kWh appliance costs £69.75 a year. A 375 kWh appliance costs £98.70. That is the whole comparison and it takes ten seconds in the shop.

The third row is the useful discovery. Dropping the water and ice dispenser takes the annual gap from £28.95 down to £17.11 and removes the plumbing, the filter cartridges and the most common failure point on these machines, while keeping the width you actually wanted.

3. The three costs nobody mentions in the showroom

One: the plumbing and the filters. A plumbed dispenser needs a cold water supply run to the back of the appliance, which is a plumber's visit if there is not one there. The filter cartridge then needs changing roughly every six months at typically £25 to £45. Call it £70 a year in filters, which is more than twice the electricity premium and is the cost people forget entirely. Non-plumbed models with a fill tank avoid the supply but not the filter.

Two: getting it into the kitchen. American style units are commonly around 90cm wide and 70cm deep, and they are heavy. Measure the front door, the internal doorways, any turn in the hall and the gap between units before ordering, not after. Delivery drivers will not remove doors and a refused delivery on a bulky appliance can carry a restocking charge.

Three: repair economics and resale. A sealed system fault, meaning the compressor or the refrigerant circuit, is expensive on any fridge and frequently uneconomic on a large one, because the parts and the labour scale with the machine. And when you move, a 90cm appliance fits far fewer kitchens than a 60cm one, so it is harder to sell and more likely to become a disposal cost. A standard 60cm unit has a real second-hand market.

None of this says do not buy one. It says the true premium is the electricity plus the filters plus the risk, not the electricity alone.

4. How to cut the running cost of whichever one you buy

These apply to any fridge freezer and none of them cost anything.

Check the temperatures with a thermometer. The fridge should be at 5 degrees or below and the freezer at minus 18. Every degree colder than needed costs money for nothing, and factory settings are frequently colder than required. A fridge thermometer is a couple of pounds.

Give the back and sides air. A fridge works by moving heat out of the cabinet into the room, and it cannot do that if the condenser is jammed against a wall or boxed into a unit with no gap. Follow the manufacturer's clearances. Vacuum the coils at the back once a year.

Keep it away from the oven, the boiler and direct sun. A warmer surrounding room means a longer duty cycle. Moving a fridge out of a sunny corner is free.

Check the door seal. Shut a sheet of paper in the door and try to pull it out. If it slides freely, the seal is failing and the machine is running against a permanent leak. Replacement seals are typically £20 to £50 and are one of the few appliance repairs genuinely worth doing yourself.

Do not put warm food straight in, and defrost a manual freezer before the ice reaches about 5mm. Both are old advice and both are still correct.

5. Worked example: Tadeusz in Basingstoke

Tadeusz has a 2011 60cm fridge freezer that still works. His kitchen has been extended and he wants something bigger. He is choosing between an American style with a plumbed dispenser and a plain American style, and he has been told the old one is costing him a fortune.

He finds the old machine's label figure: 395 kWh a year, so £103.96 at 26.32p.

The plumbed American style is 380 kWh, so £100.02. The plain American style is 325 kWh, so £85.54. A modern 60cm 50:50 is 260 kWh, so £68.43.

So the honest position is this. Replacing his 2011 machine with the plumbed American unit saves him £3.94 a year. On a £1,100 appliance that is a payback of 279 years. The energy argument for the upgrade does not exist, and he should stop pretending it does.

The plain American style saves £18.42 a year against the old machine, which on £850 is a 46 year payback. Also not an energy decision.

Only the modern 60cm at £450 gets anywhere: £35.53 a year saved, a 12.7 year payback. Still longer than most people keep an appliance, but at least it is the right order of magnitude.

He buys the plain American style, because he wants a bigger fridge and he can afford one. What he stops doing is telling himself it will pay for itself. It will cost him about £16 a year more than the 60cm alternative and he is content with that, which is a perfectly good place to end up.

6. The honest verdict

Buy an American style fridge freezer because you want the space, never because it will save energy. It will not. It costs roughly £29 a year more than a standard 50:50 with a dispenser, or about £17 without one, and £435 or £257 respectively over fifteen years.

If you do buy one, skip the plumbed water and ice dispenser. It is the single most expensive feature in the category once you count filters at £25 to £45 twice a year, and it is the part most likely to fail.

Read the kWh per year on the label, not the A to G letter, and measure your doorways before you order.

Frequently asked

Does an American fridge freezer need a plumber? Only if you choose a plumbed water and ice model and there is no cold supply within reach. Non-plumbed models with a manual fill tank need no plumbing at all and avoid the supply cost, though they still take filter cartridges.

Is a bigger freezer cheaper per litre to run? Usually yes, which is the genuine efficiency argument for going large. A big cabinet has a better volume to surface area ratio, so it loses proportionally less heat. That is why the label letter can improve with size even as the kWh figure rises.

How long should a fridge freezer last? Ten to fifteen years is the normal expectation, with the larger and more feature-heavy units at the lower end because there is more to go wrong. Door seals, thermostats and fans are all economically repairable. A sealed system fault usually is not.

Should I turn the freezer off when I go on holiday? No for the fridge freezer, unless you empty it completely. A full freezer holds temperature well and switching off for two weeks risks the food and gains a few pounds at most. Emptying and switching off a second garage freezer over summer, however, is worth real money.
