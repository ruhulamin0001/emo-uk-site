Electric Underfloor Heating Costs About 4p Per Square Metre Per Hour. In A Bathroom That Is Nothing. As The Only Heating In A Kitchen It Is Nearly £700 A Winter.

Electric underfloor heating is the one heating product in this series that is genuinely sold on comfort rather than savings, and the arithmetic explains why. The mats are rated at 150 or 200 watts per square metre on the manufacturer's own specification, and every watt comes from the grid at 26.32p a kilowatt hour under the October 2026 cap. That makes it a cheap luxury in a small tiled room used for an hour or two a day, and a very expensive way to heat anything larger for longer. We priced it room by room, and against gas.

1. The wattage, and where it comes from

Electric underfloor heating is a heating element laid under the floor covering, either as a mesh mat with cable fixed to it or as loose cable. The only number that matters for running cost is the output per square metre, and it is on every manufacturer's spec. Warmup, one of the largest UK suppliers, lists its StickyMat at 150 W per square metre, with a 200 W per square metre version "designed for high heat loss areas such as conservatories", and its foil heater for under laminate at 140 W per square metre. Other brands use the same two ratings, because they are the standard.

Price it at the Ofgem cap unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p per kWh. A square metre of 150 W mat at full power draws 0.15 kWh an hour, which is 3.95p per square metre per hour. The 200 W mat is 5.26p. Multiply by the floor area and the hours and you have the bill, before the thermostat.

The thermostat matters. A floor at full power reaches temperature and then cycles, and how much of the time the element is live depends on the room's heat loss, the floor construction and the set temperature. Our working assumption throughout is that the mat is live for 60 per cent of the time the heating is on, which is fair for an insulated floor in a normal room and generous for a conservatory. If your floor sits on uninsulated concrete, use 80 per cent or more.

Two things that do not change the sum: the brand, and the price of the mat. All 150 W mats draw 150 W. A dearer mat is dearer to buy, not cheaper to run.

3. Against gas central heating, and against panel heaters

Against gas. The kitchen-diner heated by the floor alone uses 14.4 kWh of electricity a day in the table. Delivering the same 14.4 kWh of heat from a condensing gas boiler at about 90 per cent efficiency needs 16 kWh of gas, which at 7.97p is £1.28 a day, or £232 a winter, against the floor's £690. The floor costs three times as much for the same warmth, because electricity costs 3.3 times what gas does per unit and the boiler's small efficiency loss does not close that. No purchase price divided by a negative saving gives a payback; electric underfloor heating as a main heat source in a gas-heated house has none.

Against panel heaters or storage heaters in an all-electric home. Here the fuel is the same, so the floor is not at a price disadvantage. A kilowatt hour through the floor costs the same as a kilowatt hour through a panel heater, 26.32p, and the floor's advantages are real: an even, low-temperature heat that lets you set the thermostat a degree lower for the same comfort, and room-by-room timing. Whether that is worth the installation depends on how much the lower setting saves, and a degree is worth a few per cent of a room's heating. It is a comfort and control upgrade at roughly neutral running cost, which is a reasonable thing to buy and not a saving.

Against a wet underfloor system on a heat pump. That is the comparison where underfloor heating genuinely wins on running cost, because a heat pump delivers around three units of heat per unit of electricity and works best with the low flow temperatures a floor needs. It is also a different product, a different budget and a different article. Do not confuse the two: "underfloor heating is efficient" is true of the wet kind on a heat pump and false of the electric kind on the grid.

4. Installation, insulation and the thermostat

Insulation boards under the mat. A mat laid straight onto a concrete slab heats the slab before it heats you, and the duty cycle climbs towards 100 per cent. Tile backer or insulation boards under the mat cut the warm-up time and the running cost more than any other single choice; Warmup's own guidance is that a system over insulated board heats far faster than one over bare concrete. Budget for them and do not let a fitter talk you out of them.

The thermostat. A programmable thermostat with a floor sensor is what turns 100 per cent duty into 60. A timer alone is not enough, because it does not know when the floor is warm. Set it so the floor is warm when you walk in, not on all day.

The electrics. Anything over about 2 kW, which is 13 square metres of 150 W mat, needs its own circuit rather than a spur from the ring, and any underfloor heating in a bathroom must be installed to the wiring regulations for wet rooms with an RCD. That is an electrician's job and part of the cost.

Floor coverings. Tiles and stone conduct well and are what the arithmetic above assumes. Laminate and engineered wood work with the foil type at 140 W. Thick carpet and underlay insulate the room from the floor and waste most of the output; check the combined tog rating against the manufacturer's limit before you buy.

Lifespan and warranty. The mat itself has no moving parts and manufacturers commonly give long warranties on it, sometimes lifetime; the thermostat is the part that fails, and it is a plug-in replacement. A mat cannot be repaired without lifting the floor, which is why the warranty on the mat matters more than the warranty on anything else in this series.

5. Worked example: Isla and Ben in Bristol

Victorian terrace, gas central heating, bathroom being retiled, 3.8 square metres of floor, and a 22 square metre kitchen-diner they are also thinking about

The tiler has offered to lay 150 W mat under both floors while they are up. Isla wants warm bathroom tiles. Ben has read that underfloor heating is efficient and wonders about turning the kitchen radiator off.

The bathroom. 3.8 m2 at 150 W is 570 W. Three hours a day at 60 per cent duty is 1.03 kWh a day, 27p a day, £49 a winter. They keep the radiator for the room's heat and use the floor for feet. Decision: yes, with insulation board under it, because £49 a winter for a warm floor every morning is the cheapest luxury in the house.

The kitchen-diner as comfort only. 22 m2 at 150 W is 3.3 kW, which needs its own circuit. Four hours a day at 60 per cent is 7.9 kWh, £2.08 a day, £379 a winter, on top of the gas heating that still has to warm the room. Decision: no. Warm tiles in a room they walk through in shoes are not worth £379 a year.

The kitchen-diner as the only heating. Eight hours at 60 per cent is 15.8 kWh, £4.17 a day, £759 a winter. The same heat from their boiler is about £255. Ben's "efficient" was the wet kind on a heat pump; this is the electric kind on the grid, and it costs three times the gas. Decision: no, and the radiator stays.

Total added to the bills: about £49 a year, for the bathroom alone. There is no payback and they are not looking for one.

6. The honest verdict

Buy electric underfloor heating for a bathroom or en suite that is being tiled anyway, lay it over insulation board with a floor-sensing thermostat, and expect £20 to £70 a winter for warm tiles at the October 2026 cap rate. It will not save you anything, and at that price it does not need to.

Do not buy it as the main heating for a large room in a gas-heated house, because 150 W per square metre at 26.32p costs about three times what the same warmth costs from the boiler, and there is no payback because there is no saving. In an all-electric home it is a neutral-cost comfort upgrade over panel heaters. If what you want is efficient underfloor heating, the answer is a wet system on a heat pump, which is a different purchase altogether.

Frequently asked

How much does electric underfloor heating cost to run per hour? A 150 W per square metre mat at full power costs 3.95p per square metre per hour at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p, so a 4 square metre bathroom is about 16p an hour at full power and roughly 10p once the thermostat is cycling.

Is electric underfloor heating cheaper than radiators? Not in a gas-heated house. Electricity costs 3.3 times what gas does per unit, so heating a room by electric floor costs about three times what the same heat costs from a gas boiler. In an all-electric home it costs about the same per unit as panel heaters, with better control.

How much does underfloor heating in a bathroom cost per year? On our arithmetic, a 4 square metre bathroom with a 150 W mat on for two hours a day at 60 per cent duty is about £35 over an October to March winter; three hours a day is about £52 and four hours £69.

Does electric underfloor heating pay for itself? No. It does not replace a dearer fuel with a cheaper one, so there is no saving to recover the installation cost. It is a comfort purchase, and in a small tiled room a cheap one.
