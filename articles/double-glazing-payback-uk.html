Double Glazing Takes 56 Years To Pay For Itself. Buy It Anyway, But Not For The Reason The Salesman Gives.

"Double glazing will pay for itself" is one of the most repeated claims in British home improvement, and on the Energy Saving Trust's own figures it is not true. Replacing every window in a single-glazed semi costs about £4,800 and saves about £85 a year. The windows will be worn out long before the money comes back. There are good reasons to buy double glazing. Saving money is not one of them, and the cheap alternatives, tested in a laboratory for Historic England, do most of the job for a fraction of the price.

1. The Energy Saving Trust figures

The Energy Saving Trust's windows and doors guide, last updated 20 May 2026, gives two numbers that between them settle this question. A set of A-rated windows for a semi-detached house will typically cost around £4,800. Installing A-rated double glazing in an entirely single-glazed semi-detached home can save £85 a year in Great Britain, and £140 in Northern Ireland where heating oil is the comparison. The EST adds that it also reduces carbon dioxide by 290 kg a year in GB.

Those are the figures for the biggest possible improvement, from single glazing everywhere to A-rated everywhere. Most homes in Britain are not single-glazed; they have double glazing from the 1990s or 2000s that is tired, misted or draughty. The EST does not publish a saving for replacing old double glazing with new, and we are not going to invent one. It is necessarily smaller than £85, because the starting point is already better.

The EST is an independent charity with no windows to sell. When the body that most wants you to insulate your home says the saving is £85, take the number seriously.

2. The payback, and why lifespan kills it

Payback is cost divided by annual saving. £4,800 divided by £85 is 56.5 years. That is our arithmetic on the EST's two figures, and it is the number every double glazing advert leaves out.

Now the lifespan. English Heritage's executive summary of its window research notes that modern windows tend to be designed to have much shorter lives than traditional ones, typically around 20 years. Sealed units mist when the edge seal fails, PVC frames discolour and warp, hinges and locks wear. Even if you are generous and say 30 years, the windows are replaced twice before a 56-year payback is reached. On a straight financial reading, double glazing bought to save money is a loss on day one and stays one.

That does not make it a bad purchase. It makes it a comfort purchase, like a new kitchen. The room is warmer at the glass, the traffic is quieter, the condensation stops, the locks are better, and the house is easier to sell. All of those are real. Just do not let anyone put them into a payback sum that only works if the windows last until 2082.

3. What Historic England's test found: curtains, blinds, shutters, secondary glazing

In 2009 English Heritage, now Historic England, paid Glasgow Caledonian University to put a real single-glazed timber sash window between two climate chambers, one at 2 degrees and one at 22, and measure the heat going through it with and without the things people have hung over windows for two hundred years. This is the only laboratory test of its kind we know of, and its executive summary is public. These are its whole-window results.

Read the last two rows together. A pair of heavy curtains gets you three fifths of the way to what a full window replacement achieves, for the hours they are closed. Secondary glazing, a second pane fitted inside the existing window, gets you almost all the way, all day, without touching the outside of the house. The test uncertainty is about 0.3 on the U-value, so the small differences between the middle rows are not worth arguing over; the big picture is not in doubt.

The catch with curtains and blinds is that they only work when closed, which in winter is roughly the 13 or 14 hours of darkness. Closed all day they also lose you the free solar heat that comes in through a south-facing window. So the honest saving from curtains is around half of the 41% headline, averaged over a day. Secondary glazing has no such catch.

4. The bit that actually saves money: draught-proofing

The same test measured something the double glazing brochures never mention: how much of a window's heat loss is air leaking round it rather than heat conducting through it. For the window as delivered, air leakage was over 60% of the total heat loss. After repair, about half. After draught-proofing, less than 20%.

The leakage figures are stark. At the standard 50 pascal test pressure the window as found leaked 183 cubic metres of air an hour. Repairing the joinery cut that to 120. Fitting draught-proofing strips, with the window still opening normally, cut it to 26 cubic metres an hour. Adding secondary glazing took it to 8.

So for an old, draughty window, the thing that removes most of the heat loss is not the glass at all. It is the gaps, and the fix for the gaps is a few metres of brush or rubber strip and an afternoon. Building Research Establishment testing quoted by English Heritage found a simple rubber polymer seal cut air leakage by over 50% and improved energy savings by over 25%. The EST's own overview of home energy measures lists draught-proofing among the cheapest and fastest-paying jobs in the house, and on the evidence above that is the right ranking.

If your double glazing is old rather than absent, the same logic applies. Misted units are an eyesore but the heat loss through them is barely different from clear ones; it is failed seals and dropped sashes that let the cold in. A glazier can replace individual sealed units and re-seal frames for a fraction of a full replacement.

5. Worked example: the Byrne household in Warrington

1930s semi, original single-glazed timber windows at the back, 1990s double glazing at the front

The Byrnes have a quote for £6,200 to replace all eleven windows with A-rated PVC. They want to know whether it saves money.

The honest ceiling. Even if every window were single-glazed, the EST's saving is £85 a year. Theirs are half double-glazed already, so the true saving is less. Call it £50 as a generous guess of our own. £6,200 divided by £50 is a payback of 124 years. The windows will not see 30.

What they do instead, for the five single-glazed windows at the back. Draught-proofing strips and a weekend: air leakage on those five windows falls by something in the order the Historic England test found. Heavy lined curtains in the two bedrooms and the dining room, closed at dusk, cut conduction through those windows by about 41% for the hours they are shut. Magnetic secondary glazing panels on the two north-facing bedrooms, which can come down in summer. Total outlay a few hundred pounds, not six thousand, against most of the same heat saving.

What they should still replace. One back window has rot in the sill and a sash that no longer closes. That one is a repair or replacement decision on its merits, and the payback sum does not apply, because a window that will not shut is not an energy question.

The front windows. Two of the 1990s units are misted. A glazier replaces the two sealed units for a fraction of the cost of new frames, which restores the view and changes the heat loss by almost nothing, because the problem was cosmetic.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy double glazing to save money. On the Energy Saving Trust's own figures the best case is £4,800 to save £85 a year, a 56-year payback on a product designed to last 20 to 30. If you are replacing old double glazing rather than single, the saving is smaller and the payback longer still.

Do buy it when the frames are rotten, when you want the quiet, the security and the end of condensation, or when you are doing the house up anyway. Those are honest reasons and the money is yours. And whichever you decide, draught-proof first, hang lined curtains, and look at secondary glazing on the worst windows, because Historic England's test shows those three do most of what the £4,800 does for a few hundred pounds.

Frequently asked

How much money does double glazing save per year? The Energy Saving Trust says about £85 a year in Great Britain for replacing every single-glazed window in a semi with A-rated double glazing. Replacing old double glazing saves less, and no official figure exists for it.

Does double glazing pay for itself? Not on the bills. At £4,800 to save £85 the payback is about 56 years, and modern windows are designed for around 20. It pays in comfort, noise, security and resale, which are different sums.

Is secondary glazing as good as double glazing? Historic England's laboratory test found low-emissivity secondary glazing cut heat loss through a single-glazed window by 58%, against about 67% for A-rated double glazing, and it cut air leakage further still. For heat, it is close. It is fitted inside, so the outside of the house is untouched.

Do curtains really reduce heat loss? In the same test, heavy curtains cut whole-window heat loss by 41% while closed. Averaged over a winter day, with the curtains open in daylight, the effect is roughly half that.
