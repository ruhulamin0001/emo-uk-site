You Own It Already. The Question Is What You Let People Do With It

There is no UK copyright register, no fee and no form, so the protection itself is free. Everything that decides whether your work earns anything happens afterwards, in the licence. Here is the difference between a licence and an assignment, and the sum to do before accepting a buyout.

The short version

  • UK copyright is automatic, free and needs no registration. It exists the moment the work is created. There is no form, no fee and no register to join.
  • That makes it the cheapest protection you will ever own and the easiest to give away by accident, because the money is not in owning it. It is in the licence.
  • A licence and an assignment are not the same thing. A licence lets someone use the work. An assignment hands over ownership permanently, and you cannot license what you no longer own.
  • Written work lasts 70 years after the author’s death. Sound recordings last 70 years from first publication, broadcasts 50 years, and the layout of a published edition 25 years.
  • The arithmetic that matters: an exclusive buyout is only worth taking if the fee beats the lifetime value of non-exclusive licences you give up. On the numbers below, a £250 buyout costs an illustrator £230.

1. You already own it, and it cost you nothing

Every original photograph, article, drawing, piece of code, song and design you make is protected by copyright from the moment it exists. GOV.UK puts it plainly: copyright protection starts as soon as a work is created.

There is no UK copyright register. No application, no fee, no waiting. If somebody offers to register your copyright for a fee, they are selling you a record of a date, not a right you did not already have.

What is worth doing, and costs nothing, is making that date provable: keep dated original files, keep the layered or raw version rather than only the export, and put your name in the file metadata. That is evidence, which is a different thing from registration.

That last row is there on purpose. People conflate the two constantly. Copyright protects the work. A trade mark protects the name or logo that identifies your goods. Owning the copyright in your logo artwork does not stop someone registering your business name, because they are not copying your drawing.

Copyright is free, automatic and permanent enough to outlive you. That is exactly why nobody pays it any attention until the week somebody else is making money from their work.

3. Licence against assignment, which is where the money is

Because ownership is free, the only commercial decisions are about what you let other people do with it. There are two mechanisms and they are not interchangeable.

A licence is permission to use the work on stated terms. You keep ownership. You can license the same work to many people, for different territories, media, durations and purposes. A licence can be exclusive, meaning nobody else gets it during the term, or non-exclusive, meaning you can keep selling it.

An assignment transfers ownership. Once assigned, the work is not yours. You cannot license it again, you often cannot use it in your own portfolio without permission, and you have no further claim on anything it earns.

Clients frequently ask for an assignment using the words “full rights” or “we need to own it”. Very often what they actually need is an exclusive licence for their sector and territory. Asking which one they need is a fair question, and the answer changes the price by a lot.

Four things to pin down in any licence:

  1. Scope. Which media, which products, which territories.
  2. Term. Two years, five years, perpetual. Perpetual is much closer to an assignment in value.
  3. Exclusivity. Exclusive to them, or can you keep selling it.
  4. Credit and moral rights. In the UK the right to be identified as the author must be asserted, and clients often ask for moral rights to be waived. Decide whether you mind.

4. The arithmetic of a buyout

Here is the calculation almost nobody does when a client offers a lump sum for full rights.

A buyout is worth accepting only when the fee exceeds the total you would otherwise earn from licensing the same work over its useful commercial life, discounted for the fact that future sales are uncertain.

So you need three numbers: what a typical non-exclusive licence of that work earns, how many you realistically expect, and over how long.

Worked example: Callum in Inverness

Callum draws detailed botanical illustrations. One image of a Scots pine has been licensed non-exclusively eleven times over four years, at an average of £40 a licence, which is £440 so far.

The offer. A publisher offers £250 for full assignment of copyright in that image.

The lifetime estimate. The image is still selling at roughly three licences a year. Assume it keeps doing that for another four years and then tails off, so call it 12 more licences at £40, or £480. Discount that by a third for uncertainty and the tail-off and you get roughly £320 of expected future income.

The comparison. £250 now against about £320 spread over four years. The buyout is £70 short, and that is before counting the eleven past licences he can no longer point to as a track record, or the loss of the image from his portfolio.

The counter-offer that works. He offers an exclusive licence for the publisher’s sector, print books, worldwide, five years, at £300. The publisher gets everything it actually needs. Callum keeps the copyright, can license into greetings cards and textiles, and gets the image back at the end of the term.

The honest caveat. If the image had licensed twice in four years rather than eleven times, the expected future income would be around £60 and the £250 buyout would be an obvious yes. The arithmetic decides it, not the principle.

5. The two situations where you never owned it

Two exceptions catch people out, and both are worth knowing before you invoice.

Work made in the course of employment. If you created the work as an employee, doing your job, your employer generally owns the copyright, not you. That is the default position and it does not depend on anyone signing anything. It is why a designer who leaves a studio usually cannot take the work with them.

Commissioned work with a contract that says so. A freelancer normally owns the copyright in commissioned work by default, but a contract can assign it. Many standard client agreements and platform terms do exactly that, in a clause most people scroll past. Read the intellectual property clause before signing, not after the work sells.

6. Frequently asked

Do I need to put a copyright notice on my work? Not to have the right. A notice and a date are useful evidence and a deterrent, and they cost nothing, so use them.

Does posting it online give away my copyright? No. But platform terms of service usually grant the platform a broad licence to use what you upload. That is a licence, not a transfer, and it is worth reading the wording rather than assuming.

Can I copyright an idea, a name or a business model? No. Copyright protects the expression of an idea in a fixed form, not the idea itself. Names and logos are trade mark territory, and the cost of that is set out in our trade mark guide.

What if someone uses my work without permission? Start with a polite, specific notice setting out the work, the use and what you want. Most cases end there. The Intellectual Property Office publishes guidance on the options if it does not.

7. The honest verdict

Do not pay anyone to register your copyright. It is automatic and free, and there is no UK register to be on. Spend the effort on dated original files and a clear licence instead.

Refuse blanket assignments unless the fee beats the lifetime licensing value of the work, and do the sum rather than guessing. For most freelancers the answer is an exclusive licence, scoped to what the client actually needs, at a price between a non-exclusive fee and a full buyout.

And read the intellectual property clause in every contract you sign. The cheapest protection in the country is also the easiest to sign away in a sentence you did not read.
