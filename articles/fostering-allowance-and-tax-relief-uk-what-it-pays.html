Fostering is self employment on paper, and the tax threshold is set so high most carers never touch it

You register as self employed, you file a tax return, and then a relief nobody outside fostering has heard of usually wipes the tax out entirely. The allowance itself is a wide range, and the gap between the range and the threshold is the whole point.

The short version

  • The minimum allowance is usually between £176 and £309 a week, and every foster carer receives one.
  • You register as self employed and file tax returns. That part is not optional.
  • Qualifying care relief exempts £20,440 per household in a tax year before anything else is counted.
  • On top of that, £435 a week per child under 11 and £515 a week per child aged 11 or over, for every week or part week they are in your care.
  • GOV.UK's own example reaches £51,570 tax free for one full-year placement and one ten week placement.
  • National Insurance credits may apply, which count towards the State Pension.

1. What the allowance actually is

All foster carers receive a foster care allowance to cover the cost of caring for a child. GOV.UK says the minimum is usually between £176 and £309 a week, which is a range wide enough that the midpoint tells you nothing useful.

What moves you within it, on GOV.UK's list: where you live, which fostering service you use, the child's age, whether the child has specific needs, and your own skills and experience. The fostering service you apply to will tell you how much you can get, and that is the only figure that means anything for your household.

Note the word minimum. That range is the floor rather than the offer, and independent agencies and specialist placements sit above it. We are not going to quote agency rates, because they are commercial figures that change and are not published anywhere official.

Note also the word allowance. GOV.UK describes it as covering the cost of caring for a child. It is not framed as a wage, and treating it as income to live on is the mistake that makes the tax position feel more generous than it is.

2. How qualifying care relief works

When you start fostering you register as self employed and file tax returns. Then qualifying care relief does the work, and it has two parts.

The first is a flat exemption. In a tax year, households do not pay tax on the first £20,440 they earn from fostering. That is per household, not per carer, and it does not touch tax on money you earn from a job or from investments.

The second is weekly relief, on top of the exemption, for every week or part week that a child is in your care:

  • £435 per child where the child is under 11
  • £515 per child where the child is 11 or over

GOV.UK works its own example. Laura fosters a 14 year old for the whole year and an 8 year old for 10 weeks. Her exemption is £20,440. Her relief on the 14 year old is £515 times 52, which is £26,780. Her relief on the 8 year old is £435 times 10, which is £4,350. Altogether she pays no tax on the first £51,570 she earns from fostering.

GOV.UK's own summary of the effect is the line worth remembering: this means you do not usually pay tax on your earnings from foster care, even if they go over £20,440.

3. A worked example with the gap in it

Joanne in Telford fosters one 13 year old for a full year.

Her threshold is the household exemption plus the weekly relief. That is £20,440 plus £515 times 52. The weekly part is £26,780, so her threshold is £47,220.

Now the income side. Take the top of GOV.UK's published minimum range, £309 a week. Across 52 weeks that is £309 times 52, which is £16,068 a year.

The gap between the two is £47,220 minus £16,068, which is £31,152. She is not close to the threshold. At the bottom of the range, £176 a week, her fostering income would be £9,152 a year and the gap would be £38,068.

That is the honest shape of it. For a single placement at the published minimum range, the tax question is settled before it starts, and the self assessment return is an administrative obligation rather than a tax bill. The relief starts to matter when there are several children, higher agency rates, or a mix of placements across a year, which is exactly the situation GOV.UK chose for its own example.

Two things that are not in the arithmetic. You may be entitled to National Insurance credits, which count towards your State Pension, and that is worth checking because it is a real long term benefit. And fostering can affect the benefits you already get, so you must tell whoever pays them that you are also receiving a foster care allowance.

The verdict

If you are weighing fostering as a way of replacing an income, the published allowance range is the number to plan on, and at £176 to £309 a week it is a contribution to household costs rather than a salary. The relief makes it tax efficient. It does not make it larger.

If you are already fostering, the thing worth doing this week is checking the National Insurance credits position and confirming the benefits notification has been made. Both are free, both are easy to miss, and one of them affects your State Pension record for the rest of your life.

Where this does not help you: these are the England figures and the England arrangements. Fosterline gives free fostering advice and is named on GOV.UK's own page, which is the right first call before an agency's recruitment team.

Frequently asked

Do I really have to register as self employed? Yes. GOV.UK says you register as self employed when you start fostering and file tax returns, and to check with your fostering service what you need to do.

Is the £20,440 per carer or per household? Per household. GOV.UK states it as a tax exemption of up to £20,440 per household, and its worked example applies it once.

Does a part week count for the weekly relief? Yes. GOV.UK says you get tax relief for every week, or part week, that a child is in your care.

Will fostering affect my benefits? It can. GOV.UK says you need to tell the organisation that pays you that you are also getting foster care allowance, and points to its benefits calculators and to Fosterline for free advice.
