Electricity VAT Drops to Zero on 1 October. Here Is Why Your Bill Still Goes Up.

From 1 October 2026, VAT on domestic electricity falls from 5% to 0% for six months. The government puts it at around £45 a year. What almost nobody is saying out loud is that the saving is already inside the October price cap figure, so you will never see it as a line on your bill, and a wholesale-driven rise is about to swallow it whole.

The short version

  • VAT on domestic electricity goes from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026 and is currently confirmed only until 31 March 2027. Six months, not permanent.
  • Gas is not included. Gas stays at 5% VAT. If you are on dual fuel, only the electricity half changes.
  • It is worth around £45 a year on government figures, and around £44 on Cornwall Insight's, published 21 July 2026. Both are annualised estimates for a typical household, not a payment.
  • There is nothing to claim and no refund. It arrives as slightly lower electricity unit rates from 1 October, and it is already built into the October price cap number.
  • Cornwall Insight still forecasts the October cap at about £1,700 a year, roughly 2% above today's £1,663, because wholesale gas costs are rising faster than the VAT cut helps.
  • Northern Ireland is excluded entirely. The NI Executive receives comparable funding instead.
  • The £45 figure is an annual one applied to a six month cut. The Institute for Fiscal Studies puts the real saving at around £25 for a typical household across the October to March window.

1. What actually changes on 1 October

The government announced on 21 July 2026 that VAT on domestic electricity would be temporarily zero rated. The detail matters more than the headline, so here it is plainly.

The rate goes from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026 and, as things currently stand, reverts on 31 March 2027. That is a six month measure tied to the end of the financial year. Unless it is extended, electricity bills carry a built in increase of roughly £45 a year on 1 April 2027.

It applies to electricity only. Gas remains at 5%. It covers Great Britain, plus small businesses that already qualify for the domestic rate of energy VAT, charities' non-business electricity use, and residential care homes. Northern Ireland is excluded, because VAT on energy there sits under the Windsor Framework, so the NI Executive receives comparable funding rather than the rate cut itself.

The Treasury estimates the relief costs around £850 million in 2026 to 2027, funded by cancelling the planned £1.8 billion Digital ID programme.

2. The bit competitors are missing: it is already inside the cap

This is the part that generates the angry emails to suppliers in October, so read it twice.

Ofgem's price cap is quoted including VAT. That means the October cap figure, which Ofgem confirms by 26 August 2026 and which applies from 1 October to 31 December, is calculated with electricity VAT already at 0%. The forecasters have done the same. When Cornwall Insight revised its October forecast on 21 July, it moved to about £1,700 a year for a typical dual fuel direct debit household precisely because it had folded the VAT cut in.

You will not see a £45 credit, a rebate or a separate VAT line going to zero. The money is already spent before the number reaches the news.

So there is no second saving waiting for you. The £45 is not added on top of the cap figure. It is one of the reasons the cap figure is not higher than it is.

And it still is not enough. Cornwall Insight expects the October cap to land roughly 2% above the current £1,663 for a typical direct debit household, which has applied since 1 July 2026. Dr Craig Lowrey, Principal Consultant at Cornwall Insight, attributed the underlying pressure to increased unrest in the Middle East pushing wholesale gas costs up. A tax cut on electricity does nothing about the price of gas.

One footnote worth knowing, because it confuses people comparing figures across the year: Ofgem changed its definition of a typical household on 1 July 2026, to 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year, down from 2,700 and 11,500. Under the old definition, the same October forecast would read £1,906. Nothing about your actual usage changed. Only the yardstick did.

3. The one sum that tells you what you keep

Forget £45. It is an average for a household that may look nothing like yours. VAT is proportional, so your saving scales with what you spend.

Removing 5% VAT from a VAT inclusive price cuts it by one twenty-first, or about 4.76%. Not 5%, because the 5% was charged on the pre-VAT amount, and this catches people out constantly.

So the sum is this:

Estimate what you will spend on electricity between 1 October and 31 March, then divide by 21. That is roughly what the VAT cut is worth to you in cash over the six months it runs.

Two consequences follow, and both are ignored in most coverage. If you have gas central heating and modest electricity use, you will keep well under £45. If you heat with electricity, run a heat pump, charge an electric car at home, or have high electricity use for a medical need, you will keep meaningfully more. The cut is worth most to exactly the households whose bills are worst.

There is a second reason the headline overstates it. The £45 describes the effect on the annual Ofgem cap, but the zero rate only runs for six months. The Institute for Fiscal Studies puts the actual saving at around £25 for a typical household. Slightly more than half of £45, because October to March are the heavy-use months. The IFS also makes the fairness point plainly: a VAT cut hands the biggest cash gains to the households using the most electricity, which skews towards richer homes.

Worked example: the Rahman household

Three bedroom terrace in Watford, two adults and two children, gas central heating, slightly above typical electricity use.

Their electricity spend, 1 October to 31 March: about £560 including VAT, on their own past winter bills.

What the VAT cut is worth to them: £560 ÷ 21 = £26.67, call it £27. Not £45.

What the cap rise costs them: their total dual fuel spend runs at roughly £1,750 a year. A 2% rise adds about £35.

Net position: about £8 worse off across the year, after a tax cut that made the national news. And the £27 never appears as a credit. It is buried inside slightly lower unit rates they will not notice.

This is not a reason to be cynical about the policy. Without it they would be around £27 worse off again. It is a reason to stop waiting for it to fix anything.

4. What to do before 1 October

Submit a meter reading dated as close to 30 September as you can manage. This is the single most valuable thing in this article. Rates change on 1 October. If your supplier estimates across that date, it can apply the new rates to units you actually burned in September. A real reading removes the argument entirely. Photograph the meter with the date visible.

Check your payment method. The cap for cash, cheque and quarterly direct debit customers is £1,795 a year against £1,663 on monthly direct debit, a gap of about £132. That single change is worth roughly three times the VAT cut, and it is free.

Do not fix a tariff purely because of the VAT news. Any fixed deal quoted for a period after 1 October will already price in 0% VAT, and will also price in the risk of it returning on 1 April 2027. You are not getting ahead of anything by rushing.

Diarise 31 March 2027. If the cut is not extended at the Budget, that is the date your electricity gets about 5% dearer overnight with no announcement to warn you. Our price cap guide covers the fix-or-stay decision in more depth.

Frequently asked

Do I need to apply for the VAT cut? No. There is no form, no claim and no deadline. Your supplier applies it to electricity units supplied from 1 October 2026. If anyone contacts you offering to claim it on your behalf, that is a scam.

Will my bill go down on 1 October? For most households, no. The VAT cut pulls the electricity rate down, but wholesale-driven increases are expected to push the overall cap up around 2%. The two do not cancel out in your favour.

I am on a prepayment meter. Do I get it? Yes, on electricity you top up for after 1 October 2026. Prepayment has its own cap level, so check your supplier's current rates rather than the headline direct debit figure.

I live in Northern Ireland. Does this apply? No. The zero rate covers Great Britain. Energy VAT in Northern Ireland is governed by the Windsor Framework, and the NI Executive has been allocated comparable funding instead.
