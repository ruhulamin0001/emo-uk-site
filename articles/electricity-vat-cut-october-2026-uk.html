Electricity VAT Dropped to Zero on 1 October. Here Is Why Your Bill Still Went Up.

VAT on domestic electricity fell from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026 for six months. The government puts it at around £45 a year. What almost nobody says out loud is that the saving was already inside the price cap before it was announced, so you will never see it as a line on your bill, and a wholesale-driven rise swallowed it whole.

  • VAT on domestic electricity went from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026 and is currently confirmed only until 31 March 2027. Six months, not permanent.
  • Gas is not included. Gas stays at 5% VAT. On dual fuel, only the electricity half changed.

1. What actually changed on 1 October

The government announced on 21 July 2026 that VAT on domestic electricity would be temporarily zero rated. The detail matters more than the headline, so here it is plainly.

The rate went from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026 and, as things currently stand, reverts on 31 March 2027. That is a six month measure tied to the end of the financial year. Unless it is extended, electricity bills carry a built in increase of roughly £45 a year on 1 April 2027.

It applies to electricity only. Gas remains at 5%. It covers Great Britain, plus small businesses that already qualify for the domestic rate of energy VAT, charities' non-business electricity use, and residential care homes. Northern Ireland is excluded, because VAT on energy there sits under the Windsor Framework, so the NI Executive receives comparable funding rather than the rate cut itself.

The Treasury estimates the relief costs around £850 million in 2026 to 2027, funded by cancelling the planned £1.8 billion Digital ID programme.

2. The bit everyone missed: it was already inside the cap

This is the part that generates the angry emails to suppliers, so read it twice.

Ofgem's price cap is quoted including VAT. That means the October cap figure, which Ofgem confirmed on 26 August 2026 and which applies from 1 October to 31 December, was calculated with electricity VAT already at 0%.

You did not see a £45 credit, a rebate or a separate VAT line going to zero. The money was already spent before the number reached the news.

There was never a second saving waiting for you. The £45 was not added on top of the cap figure. It is one of the reasons the cap figure is not higher than it is.

And it still was not enough. Ofgem confirmed the cap at £1,723 a year for a typical direct debit household, up 4% from £1,663, an increase of £60 a year or £5 a month. Ofgem attributed the rise to wholesale gas prices, up 11% over the preceding three months, driven by unrest in the Middle East. A tax cut on electricity does nothing about the price of gas.

Ofgem's own note is explicit: the cap would have been about £45 higher without the VAT change. That confirms the government's figure, and it also confirms that the money went into absorbing a rise rather than into your pocket.

One footnote worth knowing, because it confuses people comparing figures across the year: Ofgem changed its definition of a typical household on 1 July 2026, to 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year, down from 2,700 and 11,500. Under the old definition, the same October rates come to £1,935. Nothing about your actual usage changed. Only the yardstick did.

3. The VAT cut is hiding a 5.8% electricity rise

Here are the confirmed direct debit unit rates, from Ofgem's published tables. These are the England, Scotland and Wales averages and your region will differ.

RateJuly to September 2026From 1 October 2026Change
Electricity unit rate26.11p per kWh26.32p per kWh+0.8%
Electricity standing charge57.19p a day54.83p a day−4.1%
Gas unit rate7.33p per kWh7.97p per kWh+8.7%
Gas standing charge29.04p a day29.68p a day+2.2%

Look at the electricity unit rate again. 26.11p to 26.32p. That is a rise of eight tenths of one percent, and most coverage reported the electricity side as broadly stable.

It is stable for you, this quarter. It is not stable underneath.

The 26.11p you paid in July included 5% VAT. The 26.32p you pay from October includes none. So the like for like comparison is not 26.11 against 26.32. It is 24.87p against 26.32p.

The pre-tax price of electricity went up 5.8%. The VAT cut absorbed all of it and a little more.

The same thing happened to the standing charge. It appears to fall from 57.19p to 54.83p a day, which sounds like a genuine cut. Strip the VAT out of July's figure and it was 54.47p. The underlying standing charge went up, by 0.7%.

This matters for one specific reason. The electricity zero rate is scheduled to end on 31 March 2027. If 5% VAT comes back and sits on top of the new base, that 26.32p becomes about 27.64p, and the standing charge becomes about 57.57p. Nothing in the wholesale market has to change for your April bill to jump. The tax break simply stops covering a price rise that already happened.

None of this is hidden or improper. But "electricity bills will remain broadly stable" and "the price of electricity did not rise" are two different statements, and only the first one is true.

4. The one sum that tells you what you keep

Forget £45. It is an average for a household that may look nothing like yours. VAT is proportional, so your saving scales with what you spend.

Removing 5% VAT from a VAT inclusive price cuts it by one twenty-first, or about 4.76%. Not 5%, because the 5% was charged on the pre-VAT amount, and this catches people out constantly.

The sum

Estimate what you will spend on electricity between 1 October and 31 March, then divide by 21. That is roughly what the VAT cut is worth to you in cash over the six months it runs.

Two consequences follow, and both are ignored in most coverage. If you have gas central heating and modest electricity use, you will keep well under £45. If you heat with electricity, run a heat pump, charge an electric car at home, or have high electricity use for a medical need, you will keep meaningfully more. The cut is worth most to exactly the households whose bills are worst.

There is a second reason the headline overstates it. The £45 describes the effect on the annual Ofgem cap, but the zero rate only runs for six months. The Institute for Fiscal Studies puts the actual saving at around £25 for a typical household. Slightly more than half of £45, because October to March are the heavy-use months. The IFS also makes the fairness point plainly: a VAT cut hands the biggest cash gains to the households using the most electricity, which skews towards richer homes.

5. Worked example: the Rahman household

Three bedroom terrace, Watford

Two adults and two children, gas central heating, slightly above typical electricity use. From their own past bills, roughly 3,000 kWh of electricity and 8,900 kWh of gas a year, with about £560 of that electricity spend falling between October and March.

What the VAT cut is worth to them. £560 ÷ 21 = £26.67, call it £27 across the six months. Not £45.

Their electricity, year on year. At July rates, 3,000 × 26.11p plus 365 × 57.19p is £992. At October rates, 3,000 × 26.32p plus 365 × 54.83p is £990. Essentially flat, and that is the VAT cut doing its job.

Their gas, year on year. At July rates, 8,900 × 7.33p plus 365 × 29.04p is £758. At October rates, 8,900 × 7.97p plus 365 × 29.68p is £817. That is £59 more, and all of the increase is gas.

Net position: about £57 a year worse off. The £27 of VAT relief never appears as a credit. It is buried inside slightly lower electricity unit rates they will not notice, and without it they would be roughly £85 worse off instead.

kWh figures are this household's own annual usage, used to show the method. Run the same two sums on your own last twelve months rather than on the headline.

This is not a reason to be cynical about the policy. It is a reason to stop waiting for it to fix anything.

6. What to do now

Submit a meter reading dated as close to 30 September as you managed. If you did not, and your supplier estimated across that date, check the bill. An estimate can apply October rates to units you actually burned in September. If the opening figure looks wrong, challenge it with a photograph of the meter.

Check your payment method. Ofgem confirmed prepayment at £1,678 and paying on receipt of a bill at £1,861, against £1,723 on monthly direct debit. That is a gap of £138 a year between direct debit and billing on receipt, for exactly the same energy. It is worth roughly five times the VAT cut and it is free to fix.

Do not fix a tariff purely because of the VAT news. Any fixed deal quoted for a period after 1 October already prices in 0% VAT, and also prices in the risk of it returning on 1 April 2027. You are not getting ahead of anything.

Diarise 31 March 2027. If the cut is not extended at the Budget on 28 October, that is the date your electricity gets about 5% dearer overnight with no announcement to warn you. Our price cap guide covers the fix-or-stay decision in more depth, with the confirmed rates.

Frequently asked

Do I need to apply for the VAT cut? No. There is no form, no claim and no deadline. Your supplier applies it to electricity units supplied from 1 October 2026. If anyone contacts you offering to claim it on your behalf, that is a scam.

Did my bill go down on 1 October? For most households, no. The VAT cut pulled the electricity rate down, but the confirmed 4% cap rise, driven by an 8.7% increase in the gas unit rate, pushed the overall bill up. The two did not cancel out in your favour.

I am on a prepayment meter. Do I get it? Yes, on electricity you top up for after 1 October 2026. Prepayment has its own cap level, confirmed at £1,678 a year, so check your supplier's current rates rather than the headline direct debit figure.

I live in Northern Ireland. Does this apply? No. The zero rate covers Great Britain. Energy VAT in Northern Ireland is governed by the Windsor Framework, and the NI Executive has been allocated comparable funding instead.
