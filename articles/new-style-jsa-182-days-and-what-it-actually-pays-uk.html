New Style JSA: 182 days of payments, and savings that are ignored completely

Almost every means tested benefit in Britain stops when your capital passes a line. New Style Jobseeker's Allowance does not have that line at all. What it has instead is a National Insurance test and a six month clock.

Ruhul Amin Published 6 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • £75.65 a week if you are up to 24, and £95.55 a week if you are 25 or over (GOV.UK, Jobseeker's Allowance, page updated 4 September 2026). Those are maximums.
  • You can get it for up to 182 days, which is about six months. After that you talk to your work coach about your options.
  • Your savings, and your partner's savings, do not affect the claim. GOV.UK says so in one flat sentence, with no threshold attached.
  • You must have worked as an employee and paid Class 1 National Insurance, usually in the last 2 to 3 years. Class 2 contributions from self-employment do not count, with two narrow exceptions.
  • Income-based JSA is closed to new claims. Universal Credit has replaced it, and the two can run alongside each other.

1. What it pays, and for how long

There is one JSA left. Income-based Jobseeker's Allowance is closed: GOV.UK says plainly that "you cannot apply for income-based JSA any more" and points you at Universal Credit instead. What remains is New Style Jobseeker's Allowance, and it works on a completely different principle.

The rates are set by age.

AgeWeekly amount
Up to 24up to £75.65
25 or overup to £95.55

Note the words "up to". These are maximums rather than flat payments, and earnings can reduce what you get.

The clock. GOV.UK: "You can get JSA for up to 182 days (about 6 months). After this you can talk to your work coach about your options." 182 days is exactly 26 weeks, which makes the maximum arithmetic simple:

  • Under 25: 26 × £75.65 = £1,966.90 across the whole claim
  • 25 or over: 26 × £95.55 = £2,484.30 across the whole claim

That is the entire ceiling of the benefit, and it is worth having in mind before treating it as a plan rather than a bridge.

Timing of payments. You may have to wait up to 7 days after applying for your JSA to start, and up to 2 weeks after that for the first payment, which may not be for the full amount. After the first one, payments are usually every 2 weeks and are the full amount. So the realistic gap between applying and the first money arriving is around three weeks, and that is worth planning for on the day you claim rather than the day it does not arrive.

The Claimant Commitment. You agree it at your Jobcentre Plus interview, and GOV.UK is explicit that payments "will be reduced or stopped" if you do not keep to it and cannot give a good reason. This is not a benefit you can claim and then ignore.

2. The savings rule, and why it matters so much

Here is the sentence that makes New Style JSA different from almost everything else:

"Your savings and your partner's savings will not affect your claim."

No threshold. No tariff income. No taper. It is a contributory benefit, which means you are drawing on the National Insurance you already paid, not asking for help on the basis of need.

Compare that with the shape of a means tested benefit, where capital above a set figure reduces the award and capital above a higher figure ends it. Housing Benefit, for instance, normally stops entirely above £16,000 of savings. New Style JSA has no equivalent line.

Two groups this matters to enormously.

  • Anyone made redundant with a payout. A redundancy payment sitting in your account does not touch New Style JSA. It very much does affect means tested support.
  • Anyone with a working partner and shared savings. Household capital is irrelevant here. It is your contribution record that is assessed, and yours alone.

Your partner's income is also not part of this calculation, which is the other half of the same point. New Style JSA is an individual, contribution based claim.

What can reduce it is your own earnings, and you cannot usually get it at all if you work 16 or more hours a week.

3. The National Insurance test that decides it

The price of ignoring your savings is a strict contribution test. To be eligible you need to have both:

  • worked as an employee
  • paid Class 1 National Insurance contributions, usually in the last 2 to 3 years

And the exclusion that catches people out: "You will not be eligible if you were self-employed and only paid Class 2 National Insurance contributions", unless you were working as a share fisherman or a volunteer development worker. Those two exceptions are the whole list.

So a sole trader who has been paying Class 2 for five years and whose work has dried up is not eligible for New Style JSA, however long they have been contributing. That is a hard result and it is worth knowing before you spend an afternoon on the form.

Which tax years count. You must usually have made Class 1 contributions in the previous 2 tax years, and National Insurance credits can cover one of those years if you did not actually pay. GOV.UK gives the relevant years by application date, so the answer changes depending on when in the year you claim. Check the current example on their eligibility page rather than assuming, because claiming a fortnight later can move you into a different pair of years.

You may also be able to rely on contributions from different tax years if, during the previous 2 tax years, you claimed JSA, Employment and Support Allowance, Carer's Allowance, Maternity Allowance or Carer Support Payment, or you lived abroad with a partner serving in the armed forces.

The other conditions are short. You must be 18 or over, under State Pension age, in Great Britain, and not working 16 or more hours a week. You are not eligible during a trade dispute with your employer, and you are not eligible if an illness or disability stops you working, in which case Employment and Support Allowance is the route to check instead. Northern Ireland has its own rules through nidirect.

Students. Part-time study may be fine. Full-time study can be fine too, but only if the qualification is at Level 4 or below, you do not get a maintenance loan, grant or bursary for it, and you can still meet the work-related requirements agreed with your work coach.

4. Callum in Motherwell: £14,000 saved and a full claim

Callum is 34 and has just been made redundant from a warehouse job in Motherwell after six years. He has £14,000 in a savings account, most of it the redundancy payment, and his partner works full time.

Contribution test: six years employed with Class 1 National Insurance. Passed comfortably.

Savings test: there is not one. The £14,000 is ignored, and so is his partner's income.

What he gets: he is 25 or over, so up to £95.55 a week.

Over the full 182 days: 26 weeks at £95.55 is £2,484.30.

Now the comparison that decides what he should do. If he were relying on a means tested benefit instead, £14,000 of capital would sit inside the range where savings reduce an award, and a slightly larger redundancy payment could have removed his entitlement altogether. On New Style JSA it changes nothing.

Two things Callum should also do, and neither is obvious.

  • Claim it even though the money is modest. A New Style JSA claim brings National Insurance credits, which protect his State Pension record for the period he is out of work. Six months of gaps in a contribution record is a real cost decades later.
  • Check Universal Credit as well as, not instead of. GOV.UK says you might get Universal Credit at the same time or instead of New Style JSA. If he gets both, his JSA counts as income for Universal Credit and reduces it, but the JSA is paid more regularly and carries the National Insurance credits.

And the honest limit: at the end of 182 days the payments stop. That is a date to put in a calendar in week one, not something to discover in month six.

5. Running it alongside Universal Credit

The two benefits are designed to overlap, and the interaction is set out plainly.

If you get both, your New Style JSA payments count as income when claiming Universal Credit and reduce the amount of Universal Credit you receive. So the combined total is not the sum of the two.

Why claim both, then? GOV.UK gives two reasons. New Style JSA is usually paid more regularly than Universal Credit, which is paid monthly in arrears. And you get different National Insurance credits, which count towards your State Pension and help you qualify for other benefits.

There is a third, practical reason. Universal Credit is means tested on your household's capital and your partner's income. New Style JSA is not. For a household with savings or a working partner, the JSA may be the only thing either of you can actually get.

If you are refused, ask for the decision in writing and check which limb failed, because the two limbs have completely different fixes. A refusal on the contribution record is about your National Insurance history and can sometimes be corrected. A refusal on the hours test is about your work pattern. Citizens Advice will read the decision letter with you for free, and a mandatory reconsideration is the formal next step.

The verdict

If you have been employed and paying Class 1 National Insurance and you have just lost your job, claim New Style JSA on day one, whatever is in your savings account, because savings genuinely do not count and the National Insurance credits alone are worth the form. Treat £2,484.30 across 182 days as the absolute ceiling for someone 25 or over, budget for roughly three weeks before the first payment lands, and check Universal Credit alongside it rather than instead of it. If you have only ever been self-employed paying Class 2, this benefit is closed to you, and knowing that now saves you the application.

Frequently asked

Does a redundancy payment stop my JSA? Not New Style JSA. GOV.UK states that your savings and your partner's savings will not affect the claim. A redundancy payment held as savings is capital, and capital is not part of this test. It can affect means tested support such as Universal Credit.

I have been self-employed for years. Can I claim? Usually not. You will not be eligible if you were self-employed and only paid Class 2 National Insurance contributions, unless you were working as a share fisherman or a volunteer development worker. If you also had employment with Class 1 contributions in the relevant tax years, that may qualify you.

What happens after 182 days? The payments stop. GOV.UK says you can talk to your work coach about your options at that point. In practice that usually means Universal Credit if you are eligible for it. It is worth checking Universal Credit before the 182 days run out rather than after.

Can I work while claiming? Not 16 or more hours a week. Below that you can work, but your earnings can reduce the amount of New Style JSA you get, and you have to tell your work coach about any work you or your partner do.
