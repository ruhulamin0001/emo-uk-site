Affiliate income and HMRC: the £1,000 is measured before your costs, not after

Most people running an affiliate site think the trading allowance means they can earn £1,000 of profit before HMRC is interested. It does not. It is measured on gross commission, and the difference decides whether you have a filing obligation at all.

The short version

  • The trading allowance is £1,000 of gross trading income a tax year, and HMRC defines gross as the total before any allowances or expenses are taken off (GOV.UK, Tax-free allowances on property and trading income, read 6 September 2026).
  • Over £1,000 gross and you must register for Self Assessment by 5 October in the following tax year.
  • You choose the allowance or your expenses, never both. If your costs are under £1,000, the allowance almost always wins.
  • You must keep records even if you owe nothing. HMRC can charge a penalty if records are not accurate, complete and readable, or are not kept long enough.
  • You cannot use the allowance at all on income from your employer, your spouse's employer, or a company you or a connected person controls.

1. Gross, not profit, and why that changes the answer

The trading allowance is a tax exemption of up to £1,000 a year for individuals with trading income from self-employment, casual services, or hiring out personal equipment. Affiliate commission is trading income and sits squarely inside it.

The word that does the work is gross. HMRC: "Gross income means the total amount you would put on your tax return before any allowances or expenses are taken off."

So the test is not what you kept. It is what the networks paid you.

Take a small site that receives £1,150 in commission across a tax year and spends £400 on hosting, a domain, a keyword tool and a theme. The profit is £750. The gross is £1,150. The £1,000 line is measured against £1,150, so the obligation is triggered, and being £250 short of a profitable year does not change that.

This is the single most common error in affiliate tax write-ups, and it is a one word error with a filing deadline attached to it.

If your gross is £1,000 or less, HMRC says you may not have to tell them at all, and you do not need to declare it on a tax return. That is called full relief. There are exceptions, covered below.

One more line that catches people. The trading allowance and the property allowance are separate £1,000 allowances, and if you have both types of income you get one of each. Affiliate commission and rent from a spare room are not added together for this test.

2. The 5 October deadline and what you actually file

HMRC: "If your gross income for a tax year is more than £1,000, you must register for Self Assessment by 5 October in the following tax year."

Read the timing carefully, because it is generous and people still miss it. The UK tax year ends on 5 April. If your gross affiliate income passed £1,000 in the year ending 5 April, you have until the 5 October that follows to register. That is six months, and it is a registration deadline rather than a payment deadline.

The payment and filing deadline is separate and later, on 31 January.

There is a middle band worth knowing about. HMRC distinguishes between trading income and other income:

  • gross trading income over £1,000: register for Self Assessment
  • other gross income over £1,000 up to £2,500: contact HMRC rather than registering
  • other income over £2,500: register for Self Assessment

Affiliate commission from running a site is trading income, so the first line is the one that applies to most people reading this. If your situation is genuinely casual and occasional rather than a trade, the middle line may apply, and a phone call is cheaper than a wrong assumption.

Some people should register even under £1,000. HMRC lists four situations where you must register and file despite being inside the allowance:

  • you have made a loss and want to claim relief for it
  • you want to pay voluntary Class 2 National Insurance to help qualify for certain benefits
  • you want to claim Tax-Free Childcare based on your self-employment income
  • you want to claim Maternity Allowance based on your self-employment

Those last two are worth real money. Voluntary Class 2 in particular is the mechanism that lifts a Maternity Allowance award from the minimum rate towards the maximum, and it only exists if you are inside Self Assessment.

3. The allowance or your expenses, not both

Once you are over £1,000 gross, you have a choice, and HMRC is explicit that it is one or the other.

Partial relief: deduct up to £1,000 from your gross income instead of deducting any expenses or capital allowances. You cannot deduct more than the amount of your income, so the allowance cannot create a loss.

Or claim actual expenses in the normal way, and no allowance.

The rule of thumb falls straight out of the arithmetic: if your real costs are less than £1,000, the allowance is better. If they are more than £1,000, claim the expenses. HMRC says the same thing in its own words: "If your expenses are more than your income it may be beneficial to claim expenses instead of the allowances."

For a typical one-person affiliate site with hosting, a domain and one subscription tool, annual costs frequently land somewhere between £150 and £600, which puts the allowance ahead. For a site paying for content, links or paid traffic, expenses usually win.

Records are not optional either way. HMRC: "If you use the trading or property income allowances you must keep a record of your income." The examples given are invoices, a spreadsheet of income receipts, emails confirming income received, statements from the company that paid you, bank statements and pay-in records. And the sting: "HMRC can charge you a penalty if the records you keep are not accurate, complete and readable or if you do not retain them for the required period of time."

Network dashboards are not a records system, because access can be lost when an account is closed. A monthly export to a spreadsheet, plus the payment lines on your bank statement, is enough and takes ten minutes a month.

4. Priya in Leicester: £1,240 of commission, two ways

Priya runs a small review site from Leicester alongside a full-time job. In the tax year she receives £1,240 in affiliate commission across two networks. Her costs are hosting at £96, a domain at £14, and a research tool at £200, so £310 in total.

Does she have to register? Yes. Gross is £1,240, which is over £1,000, so she must register for Self Assessment by 5 October following the end of that tax year. Her £930 of profit is not the test.

Option A, the trading allowance. £1,240 minus £1,000 leaves £240 of taxable income.

Option B, actual expenses. £1,240 minus £310 leaves £930 of taxable income.

ApproachDeductionTaxableTax at 20%
Trading allowance£1,000£240£48
Actual expenses£310£930£186

The allowance saves her £138, on the assumption that her employment already uses her Personal Allowance so this income is taxed at the basic rate. It also means she does not have to itemise or evidence a single expense, which is the quieter benefit.

Now change one number. If her research tool cost £1,400 rather than £200, her total costs would be £1,510 and claiming expenses would leave nothing taxable at all. At that point the allowance is the worse option and she should not use it.

The decision is genuinely this simple: add up your real costs once a year and compare the total with £1,000.

5. The connection rule that voids the allowance

There is a category of person who cannot use the trading allowance at all, and it catches more affiliates than you would expect.

HMRC: you cannot use the allowances in a tax year if you have any trade or property income from:

  • a company you or someone connected to you owns or controls
  • a partnership where you or someone connected to you are partners
  • your employer, or the employer of your spouse or civil partner

Work through what that means in practice.

If you have a limited company for consulting work and you also run an affiliate site personally, and the site earns commission from that company, the allowance is unavailable. If you work for a software firm and you run a review site that earns commission from your own employer's affiliate programme, the allowance is unavailable. If your spouse works for a retailer and you take commission from that retailer's programme, the allowance is unavailable.

That last one is the surprise, because nothing about the arrangement feels connected from your side.

The allowance also does not apply to trading income from a partnership at all, and the property allowance cannot be used on Rent a Room Scheme income.

One more consequence worth planning for. If you take the trading allowance instead of actual expenses, your calculated income is reduced by the allowance for the purposes of the High Income Child Benefit Charge, student loan repayment and married couple's allowance. HMRC also notes that income for Universal Credit purposes is not affected by the allowances. So the choice between allowance and expenses can move numbers well beyond your tax bill, and if you are near the High Income Child Benefit Charge threshold or repaying a student loan, that is worth a moment's thought rather than defaulting.

If any of this is unclear, HMRC's Income Tax helpline is the free route, and its own guidance says to call if you are unsure whether you can use the allowances. TaxAid gives free help to people on low incomes. Neither charges, and both are better than guessing.

The verdict

Add up what the networks paid you across the tax year, before costs. If that number is over £1,000, you have a Self Assessment registration to do by 5 October in the following tax year, whatever your profit was. Then compare your real annual costs with £1,000 and take whichever is bigger, the allowance or the expenses, because you cannot have both. Keep a spreadsheet of your income even if you owe nothing, because the record keeping duty applies to the allowance too and carries a penalty. And check the connection rule before you rely on the allowance, because commission from your own employer, your spouse's employer or your own company takes it off the table entirely.

Frequently asked

Is the £1,000 on profit or on commission? On gross commission. HMRC defines gross income as the total you would put on your tax return before any allowances or expenses are taken off. A site earning £1,150 gross and spending £400 is over the line, even though its profit is £750.

What if my affiliate income is under £1,000? You may not have to tell HMRC and you do not need to declare it on a tax return. That is full relief. You still have to keep records of the income, and you must register anyway if you want to claim a loss, pay voluntary Class 2 National Insurance, or claim Tax-Free Childcare or Maternity Allowance based on the self-employment.

Can I claim the allowance and my hosting costs? No. It is one or the other. If you use the trading allowance you cannot deduct any other expenses or allowances. Work out your real costs once a year and take whichever figure is larger.

Does affiliate income affect my Universal Credit? HMRC states that income for Universal Credit purposes is not affected by the trading and property allowances, so using the allowance does not reduce the income figure Universal Credit works from. That is different from tax credits, the High Income Child Benefit Charge, student loan repayment and married couple's allowance, which are affected.
