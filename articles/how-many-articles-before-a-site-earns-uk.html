The Number You Are Looking For Does Not Exist, and Two Companies Have Said So in Writing

Thirty posts, fifty posts, a hundred posts. Every number you have been given is somebody's own site described as a law. The two organisations with the most data on this question have both put it in writing that they will not give you a figure, and one of them lists publishing in hope as a warning sign.

1. Where the numbers come from

Every article-count rule you have read comes from the same place: somebody's own site, reported honestly, and then generalised into a law it was never evidence for. One person earned at 30 posts in a niche with almost no competition. Another needed 200 in a crowded one. Both accounts are true and neither is a rule.

The reason the question keeps getting asked is that article count is the one variable a beginner can see and control. Traffic is invisible until it arrives, competition is hard to assess, and commission rates are somebody else's decision. Post count is a number you can put in a spreadsheet tonight, so it becomes the thing people plan with.

That is understandable and it is still the wrong input.

2. What the two relevant companies actually say

Two organisations have more data on this than every blog post about it combined, and both have published positions.

Google. In its documentation on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content, last updated 10 December 2025, under a heading about avoiding search-engine-first content, it lists this as a warning sign: "Are you producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results?"

And, on the site-wide version of the same instinct: "Are you adding a lot of new content or removing a lot of older content primarily because you believe it will help your search rankings overall by somehow making your site seem 'fresh?' (No, it won't)".

There is also a harder line for anyone thinking of solving the count with automation. The same page says that using automation, including AI generation, to produce content for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings is a violation of Google's spam policies.

Amazon. Section 4 of the Associates Programme Operating Agreement, updated 15 October 2025: "We do not make any representation, warranty, or covenant regarding the amount of traffic or commission income you can expect at any time in connection with the Associates Program, and we will not be liable for any actions you undertake based on your expectations."

Neither statement says effort does not matter. Both say the same narrower thing: quantity published is not the variable, and nobody will underwrite a forecast built on it.

3. The arithmetic that replaces the myth

You cannot forecast from post count. You can forecast from four figures you either measure or choose, and the honest way to do this is to state every assumption so a reader can substitute their own.

Amazon publishes its UK commission rates, so the last link in the chain is at least fixed. The current Amazon UK rates include 5% for Home, Home Improvement, Furniture, Kitchen and Dining, Books and Automotive, 6% for clothing and accessories, 4% for Beauty and Sports and Fitness, 2.5% for Appliances and Mobile Electronics, 1% for Grocery and Video Games, and 3% for all other categories.

The chain to a monthly figure is:

  1. Visits to pages with buying intent per month. Not all visits. The ones where the reader is choosing what to buy.
  2. The share of those who click an affiliate link. This is measurable from day one and it is the number most people never look at.
  3. The share of clickers who buy, inside the tracking window. On Amazon that window is 24 hours, extended to 89 days if the item goes in the basket during the visit.
  4. Commission per sale, which is basket value multiplied by the category rate above.

Multiply those four and you have a monthly figure. Change the first one and everything moves. Change the post count and nothing moves at all unless the new posts add to the first one.

That is the whole reason the article-count question misleads. Posts are an input to visits, and only some posts are.

4. Forty of the right pages beats four hundred of the wrong ones

A page earns affiliate commission when a reader arrives already trying to decide what to buy. Pages that do that tend to be the ones answering a specific purchase question: what something costs, whether it is worth it, which of two options fits a particular constraint.

Pages that do not do that can be excellent, well written, well ranked, and still earn nothing, because the reader was never in a buying frame of mind. There is nothing wrong with those pages. They are simply not the ones the arithmetic above applies to.

So if you want a planning number, the useful one is not "how many articles". It is "how many pages on my site answer a question somebody asks immediately before spending money". That number is usually much smaller than the post count, and on a lot of struggling sites it is close to zero.

If you are at the start of this, our guide to picking an affiliate niche covers how to find those questions, and what an affiliate site really costs to run covers the other side of the ledger, which people usually forget entirely.

Worked example: Bev in Redruth

Bev has 70 published pages and earns nothing. The count says she should be past the threshold everyone quotes.

She counts differently. Of the 70, she judges nine to be pages where a reader is actively choosing what to buy. The other 61 are explainers and news reactions. They are fine. They are not the earning surface.

The four figures. Those nine pages get 900 visits a month between them. Suppose 4% click an affiliate link, which is 36 clicks. Suppose 4% of those buy inside the window, which is roughly one to two sales. At a £90 basket in a 5% category, that is about £4.50 a sale.

The honest output is a few pounds a month. Every rate in that paragraph is an assumption chosen to be illustrative, not a measurement, and Bev should replace all three with her own the moment she has them.

What actually changes it. Publishing article 71 changes nothing unless it is a tenth buying-intent page. Doubling the nine pages to eighteen, or doubling the visits to those nine, roughly doubles the output. Improving the click rate from 4% to 8% does the same thing for far less work, and she can measure that this week.

And then she waits. Even after the sales start, roughly 90 days pass before commissions clear, and a threshold sits after that. Her first payment is a long way behind her first sale.

The honest verdict

There is no article count at which a site starts earning, and the question quietly assumes something false: that all pages are the same kind of page. Google names publishing-in-hope as a warning sign in its own documentation. Amazon has written into its agreement that it will not predict your income.

The replacement is not more discipline, it is a different count. Count the pages on your site where a reader is deciding what to buy. Then measure how many visits those pages get, what share of those visitors click, and what a sale is worth at the published category rate. Those four figures give you a forecast you can actually test, and they tell you which of the four to work on next.

Where this does not help you: if your pages are not indexed, none of it applies, because the first figure is zero and nothing downstream matters. And whatever the arithmetic says, remember the tail on the end of it. Around 90 days from sale to cleared commission, then a payout threshold, which is £50 at Skimlinks and similar elsewhere. Plan for the money to arrive a season after it is earned.

Frequently asked

Is there really no minimum number of posts? Not one that anyone credible publishes. Google's documentation treats producing lots of content in the hope some performs as a warning sign rather than a method, and Amazon states in its agreement that it makes no representation about the income you can expect. Every specific number in circulation is one site's history presented as a rule.

Does using AI to write more articles faster solve it? Not by itself, and it introduces a specific risk. Google's documentation says that using automation, including AI generation, to produce content for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings violates its spam policies. The constraint was never how fast you can produce words.

What should I count instead of articles? Pages where a reader is actively deciding what to buy, and the monthly visits those pages get. Then your affiliate click rate and your conversion rate, both of which you can measure with your own analytics long before any money arrives.

How long after the first sale does money arrive? Longer than most people expect. Skimlinks tells publishers that retailers typically take 60 days to review commissions with a further period after that, so around 90 days is normal, and it holds UK balances until they pass £50 before paying at month end.
