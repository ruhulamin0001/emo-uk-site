£500 a Month of Passive Income Needs £138,000 Behind It. The Arithmetic Nobody Shows You

Passive income is sold as a lifestyle and it is actually a division sum. Take the income you want, divide it by a realistic yield, and you have the capital you need. Most guides skip that step, because the answer is uncomfortable.

1. The division sum everybody skips

Every genuinely passive income stream, the kind that arrives whether you get out of bed or not, comes from capital. So the question is never "how do I make passive income". It is "how much capital do I need, at what yield, to produce the income I want".

That table is the whole subject. Everything else is detail about which yield you can realistically get and how much tax you pay on it.

It also explains why passive income content is so often sold rather than practised. The honest answer, "accumulate a six figure sum", does not make a compelling video.

There is no yield that turns £2,000 into a living. There is a yield that turns £138,000 into £500 a month, and the hard part was never the yield.

2. Cash, and why it is beating the index right now

An unusual thing about mid 2026: cash savings are paying more than the UK stock market's dividend yield.

  • Best easy access cash ISA: around 4.34%, withdrawable without penalty.
  • One year fixed cash ISA: around 4.69%. Two year around 4.76%. Three year around 4.78%.
  • FTSE 100 forecast dividend yield: 3.3%.

Cash also carries no capital risk up to the FSCS limit, and needs no decisions. For a first passive income pound, that combination is difficult to argue with.

Tax, which changes the comparison. Outside an ISA, your Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic rate taxpayers and £500 for higher rate. At 4.34%, a basic rate taxpayer uses up the full £1,000 allowance at around £23,000 of savings. Above that, interest is taxed at your marginal rate. Inside a cash ISA there is no tax at all, which is why the ISA wrapper matters more as your balance grows.

The change coming in April 2027. The £20,000 ISA allowance stays, but the amount you can put in cash drops to £12,000, with the remaining £8,000 available only for stocks and shares. If you have been using the full allowance in cash, that is a decision arriving with a date on it.

3. Dividends, after the allowance was cut

Dividend income is the classic passive income route, and it got noticeably less attractive outside an ISA.

  • The dividend allowance for 2026/27 is £500. It was £2,000 as recently as 2022/23.
  • Rates rose 2 percentage points for basic and higher rate: 10.75% basic, 35.75% higher, 39.35% additional.

Work it through. At a 3.3% index yield, £500 of dividends comes from about £15,150 invested. Everything above that, held outside an ISA, is taxed. So for most people the sensible order is: use the ISA, then think about anything else.

On high yield individual shares. You will see UK shares forecast at 6% or more. Those yields are real, but a high yield often reflects a share price that has fallen, and a dividend can be cut. Concentrating your income in three high yield names is a different risk from holding the index, and it is not passive, because you have to watch it.

4. Property, which is not passive

Buy to let gets called passive income more than anything else, and it is the least passive thing on this list.

You need a deposit, typically 25%. You pay stamp duty at the higher additional property rate. You have mortgage interest, which for individual landlords is no longer fully deductible and instead attracts a basic rate tax credit. You have letting agent fees if you do not want the phone calls, and you get the phone calls if you do not pay them. You have void periods, maintenance, gas safety certificates, deposit protection, and the compliance load has been rising for a decade.

None of that makes it a bad investment. Leverage is genuinely powerful and property has made a lot of people wealthy. It just is not passive, and the returns quoted on gross rental yield are not what lands in your account.

If your goal is money that arrives without you doing anything, property is the wrong tool. If your goal is building wealth and you are willing to run a small business, that is a different and more honest framing.

5. Digital income, honestly labelled

Blogs, YouTube, courses, apps, print on demand, affiliate sites. This is what most people actually mean when they search for passive income, and the honest label is front loaded work with a decaying return.

  • The work is real and it comes first. A site that earns anything meaningful usually represents hundreds of hours before the first significant month.
  • The return decays without maintenance. Content goes stale, search algorithms change, platforms adjust payouts. Untouched, most of these decline.
  • The distribution is brutal. A small minority earn most of the money, and survivorship bias means you mostly hear from that minority.
  • It is not diversification. If your income depends on one platform's algorithm, one policy change can end it.

None of that is a reason not to do it. It is a reason to call it a business rather than passive income, and to judge it the way you would judge a business: hours in, pounds out, and how long before it breaks even.

6. A worked example from zero

Aisha, 31, Birmingham. Saves £400 a month. Wants £500 a month of genuinely passive income.

The target. £6,000 a year. At 4.34%, that is £138,249.

Saving alone. £400 a month is £4,800 a year. Ignoring interest, £138,249 takes just under 29 years.

With interest at 4.34%, reinvested. That falls to roughly 19 years. Interest doing a third of the work is exactly what compounding looks like, and it is also why it feels so slow for the first decade.

What actually shortens it. Not a better yield. Going from 4.34% to 5% saves about a year. Raising the monthly amount from £400 to £600 cuts it to roughly 14 years. The contribution moves the answer far more than the yield does, which is the opposite of where most people spend their attention.

The realistic version. Aisha stops aiming for £500 a month in one jump. Her first target is £1,200 a year, which needs about £27,650. On £400 a month with interest, that is around five years. That is a target she can actually reach, and it pays her council tax.

Figures use current best buy rates and assume they persist, which they will not. Rates move. The shape of the answer does not.

7. The traps in passive income marketing

  • Yields quoted without capital. "This share pays 6.6%" is meaningful only alongside "which is £660 a year if you have £10,000".
  • Gross figures presented as income. Rental yield before costs, revenue before ad spend, dividends before tax.
  • The course that is the actual business. If someone made real passive income from a method, selling you the method adds work to their life. Ask why they are doing that.
  • Survivorship bias. You see the person whose channel worked. You do not see the ten thousand who posted for a year and stopped.
  • Anything promising a fixed high return with no risk. In the UK, if it is not FSCS protected and it is offering 9%, the extra is the risk premium and you are the one carrying it. Check the FCA register before anything else.

The honest verdict

Passive income is real, it is just capital intensive and slow. £500 a month needs roughly £138,000 behind it at today's best cash ISA rate, and there is no yield available to ordinary savers that shortcuts that.

The practical order for most UK households: fill the cash ISA while easy access pays around 4.34%, take any employer pension match because that is the only genuinely free return available, then use a stocks and shares ISA for money you will not need for at least five years. Treat any online venture as a business, not as passive income, and judge it on hours and pounds.

And aim at £100 a month first. It needs about £27,650, which is reachable, and hitting a real target beats missing a fantasy one.

Frequently asked

Is a cash ISA still worth it if rates are falling? The tax free wrapper matters more the larger your balance, and it keeps mattering after rates fall. Note the cash ISA allowance drops to £12,000 from April 2027, so the allowance you use before then is the allowance you keep.

Do I pay tax on dividends inside an ISA? No. Dividends and interest inside an ISA are free of UK income tax, which is why the £500 dividend allowance rarely bites for people who use their ISA first.

What about peer to peer lending? Higher advertised returns, and your capital is genuinely at risk with no FSCS protection on the loans themselves. Treat the extra yield as payment for that risk, not as a free upgrade.

Is premium bonds a passive income? The prize fund rate is an average, not what you get. Most holders with small balances receive less than the headline rate, often nothing in a given month. It is tax free and safe, but it is not a reliable income.
