A Water Meter Is A Two Year Experiment, Not A Decision. Almost Nobody Is Told That.

Households hesitate over water meters because switching feels permanent. In most of England and Wales it is not. Installation is usually free, and if you are unhappy you can switch back to the unmetered charge within 24 months. Here is how to work out your likely answer before you start, using the water regulator's own published figures.

The short version

  • You can usually switch back within 24 months if a meter leaves you worse off, according to CCW, the statutory consumer body for water. The exception is water-stressed areas of England where compulsory metering applies.
  • Installation is usually free, and you do not pay for it even if you switch back.
  • Renting is not a barrier. You can have a meter installed if your lease is at least six months long.

1. Why an unmetered bill has nothing to do with your water

If you do not have a water meter, your bill is based on the rateable value of your property, a valuation system that was abolished for domestic rates decades ago and survives only in water billing. It is a number about your house.

A metered bill is a number about your household: how many people live there and what they do.

That single difference explains everything about who wins and who loses. Two identical houses on the same street pay the same unmetered bill whether they contain one person or five. Put both on meters and the bills separate immediately.

Which gives the rule of thumb people repeat: you are more likely to save with a meter if you have more bedrooms than people. It is a proxy, not a rule, because it is really asking whether your property's valuation is high relative to your actual consumption. But it is a good proxy, and it is free to apply.

Where it breaks down is worth knowing. A small flat with four adults will lose on a meter despite having fewer bedrooms than people. A large house with two people who fill a paddling pool all summer and run a hosepipe may lose despite the bedroom count. The bedrooms test is a first filter. Section 3 is the actual answer.

2. The 24 month switch-back, and its one exception

Here is the fact that changes the decision from a commitment into a trial. CCW, the statutory body representing water consumers in England and Wales, states it plainly:

"If you choose to get a water meter, it's usually free and you can switch back to the unmetered charge within 24 months if you're unhappy (except for customers in water-stressed areas of England where compulsory metering is introduced). If a water meter isn't an option, the company may offer a cheaper alternative."

Three things follow, and each removes a common objection.

Installation is usually free. You are not buying anything. There is no upfront cost to recover.

You get two years to find out. That is long enough to cover a full cycle of seasons, including a summer of garden watering and a winter with the shower running longer. A one-month experiment would tell you very little; twenty-four months tells you everything.

Switching back does not cost you the installation. The meter stays in the wall, and you go back to the unmetered charge.

The exception matters and you should check it before assuming: water-stressed areas of England where compulsory metering has been introduced. In those areas metering is not a choice and the switch-back does not apply. Your water company will tell you whether you are in one, and CCW has a page on whether there is enough water where you live.

One more practical point that stops people who assume it is not for them. You can have a meter installed if you rent, as long as your lease is at least six months long.

3. Working out your own number, with CCW's figures

You do not have to guess. CCW's water meter calculator publishes the per-use assumptions it works from, and they let you build your own annual volume in about ten minutes:

UseLitres, per CCW
Each person, per year, covering washing up, teeth, drinking and miscellaneous11,000
A bath80
A shower, including power showers46
A toilet flush, including dual flush7.5
A washing machine cycle, including eco settings56
A dishwasher cycle, including eco settings25
A hosepipe, per hour800

The method is three steps.

One, add up your annual litres using the table. Count honestly rather than aspirationally: how many showers does your household really take in a week, not how many it ought to.

Two, divide by 1,000 to get cubic metres, which is the unit water companies bill in.

Three, multiply by your company's combined water and sewerage charge per cubic metre, which is on your bill or your company's charges page, and add the standing charges. Compare that with what you pay now.

We are deliberately not printing a price per cubic metre. There are more than thirty water and sewerage companies in England and Wales, the rates differ substantially between them, and they change annually. A single national figure would be wrong for almost everyone reading this.

CCW's own calculator will do the sums with your company selected, and it was updated with 2026-27 charges. Use it, then check the answer against your company's own calculator, because CCW says explicitly that its figures are estimates and you should not challenge your company's charges on the basis of them.

4. Worked example: the same house, two households

Worked example: two households at 14 Chapel Rise

The same three-bedroom semi, so the same rateable value and the same unmetered bill either way. All volumes below are our own calculations using CCW's published per-use figures.

Household one: the Whitlocks, two adults and a child. Twelve showers and three baths a week, twelve flushes a day, five washing machine loads, four dishwasher loads, and ten hours of hosepipe over the summer.

  • Personal use, 3 people: 33,000 litres
  • Showers: 28,704 · Baths: 12,480 · Toilet: 32,850
  • Washing machine: 14,560 · Dishwasher: 5,200 · Hosepipe: 8,000

Total: 134,794 litres, or about 135 cubic metres a year.

Household two: Eileen, living alone in the identical house next door. Six showers and one bath a week, five flushes a day, two washing machine loads, two dishwasher loads, five hours of hosepipe.

  • Personal use, 1 person: 11,000 litres
  • Showers: 14,352 · Baths: 4,160 · Toilet: 13,688
  • Washing machine: 5,824 · Dishwasher: 2,600 · Hosepipe: 4,000

Total: 55,624 litres, or about 56 cubic metres a year.

The gap is 2.4 times, on an identical unmetered bill. Eileen has three bedrooms and one person, so the rule of thumb points at a meter, and the volume confirms it. The Whitlocks have three bedrooms and three people, so the rule of thumb is neutral and their answer genuinely depends on their company's rates.

What Eileen should actually do: not agonise. Ask for the meter, keep her bills, and look again after a year. If it has cost her more, she has until month 24 to go back, and the installation cost her nothing either way.

What the Whitlocks should do: run their 135 cubic metres through CCW's calculator with their own company selected, because at that volume the answer turns on the rate rather than on the principle.

5. If you cannot have a meter

Some properties cannot take a meter, usually because of shared or inaccessible pipework, which is common in flats. That is not the end of the conversation.

CCW says that if your water company cannot install a meter, they may offer an assessed charge. An assessed charge is a fixed bill based on an estimate of what a household like yours would use, typically driven by property size or occupancy, rather than on the rateable value of the building.

For a single person in a property with a high rateable value, an assessed charge can be materially cheaper than the unmetered bill, and it is the closest thing available to the benefit of metering. It is worth asking about by name, because it is rarely offered unprompted.

Two other things to ask about at the same time, since you are already on the phone.

Surface water drainage. If rainwater from your property does not drain into a public sewer, because you have a soakaway or similar, you may be entitled to a rebate on that part of the bill. CCW's calculator has a specific tick box for this, which tells you how often it applies.

Help with bills. Water companies run social tariffs and hardship schemes, and eligibility varies by company. CCW maintains a help with bills page covering what exists.

6. The honest verdict

For most single people and couples in a house with more bedrooms than occupants, a water meter is very likely to save money, and the reason to act is not the saving itself but the structure: installation is usually free, and in most of England and Wales you have 24 months to change your mind. That turns it from a decision into an experiment with a refund attached.

For a full household, the answer genuinely depends on your company's rates and your own usage, and the only way to know is to add up your litres using CCW's figures and run them through the calculator with your company selected. Ten minutes.

Two things to check before you ring. Whether you are in a water-stressed area of England with compulsory metering, because the switch-back does not apply there. And if a meter cannot be fitted, ask specifically about an assessed charge, because it is the alternative most people are never told exists.

Frequently asked

Does the 24 months apply everywhere? CCW describes it as the general position in England and Wales, with the exception of water-stressed areas of England where compulsory metering has been introduced. Confirm with your own supplier before you apply, because the switch-back is the whole reason the decision is low risk.

Will a meter make me watch every tap? For most households the behaviour change is small and the savings come from the mismatch between the rateable value and actual use, not from rationing. If you find yourself anxious about running a bath, that is a sign the meter is not right for your household and you have until month 24 to reverse it.

I rent. Can my landlord stop me? CCW says you can have a meter installed if your lease is at least six months long. Tell your landlord as a courtesy, and check your tenancy agreement, because who is liable for the water bill varies and that changes who benefits.

What about a smart water meter? Some companies are installing smart meters that report readings automatically. The billing principle is the same, volume rather than rateable value, and the practical difference is accuracy of readings and quicker leak detection rather than a different tariff.
