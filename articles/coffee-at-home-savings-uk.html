Save Money

£4 Never Feels Like a Decision. It Feels Like Nothing, Roughly 240 Times a Year.

You have heard the advice before. Stop buying coffee and you will be rich. It is usually said by someone who has never worked a shift that needed a coffee to survive. So here is the version with actual numbers, the electricity counted, and no lecture at the end.

1. What you pay outside

A small flat white at Costa runs about £4.35 in 2026. Starbucks sits slightly higher, Greggs sits far lower at around £1.85, and independents vary by city. Prices in London are noticeably above the rest of the country.

Take the middle ground. One £4 coffee on each of your working days, say 250 days a year:

HabitPer weekPer year
One £4 coffee, working days only£20£1,000
One £4 coffee, every day£28£1,460
Two £4 coffees, working days£40£2,000
One £1.85 coffee, working days£9.25£463
That number surprises people because £4 never feels like a decision. It feels like nothing, roughly 240 times a year.

2. What the same drink costs in your kitchen

Supermarket beans cost around £5 to £7 for a 250g bag. That bag makes about 30 espressos, which works out at roughly 20p of coffee per drink. Milk for a flat white adds about 15p. Call it 35 to 40p a cup, and that is with decent beans rather than the cheapest on the shelf.

ComponentCost per drink
Beans, 250g bag at £6 making ~30 shots~20p
Milk, roughly 150ml~15p
Electricity, see below~1p to 3p
Totalabout 36p to 38p

So the same drink is around £4 outside and around 40p at home. On 250 working days that is £1,000 against £100.

The gap is roughly £900 a year, and that is before you count the sandwich you buy because you were already standing in the queue.

3. The electricity, since nobody counts it

Every article about this quietly ignores the running cost, which is a small thing done honestly rather than a large thing ignored. Here it is.

Electricity is capped at 26.32p per kWh from 1 October 2026 on a typical direct debit tariff. Boiling enough water for one mug in a kettle uses roughly 0.1 kWh, so about 2.6p. A moka pot on an induction hob is similar. A bean-to-cup machine drawing around 1,400W for the ninety seconds it actually runs is roughly 1p a cup, though a machine left on standby all day adds more than the brewing does.

The practical points: boil only what you need rather than filling the kettle, and switch a bean-to-cup machine off at the wall rather than leaving it warming. Neither will change your life, but they are the difference between the 40p figure being honest and being approximate. Rates and the wider picture are in our confirmed price cap guide.

4. What the kit costs, and what pays back fastest

The savings only work if the machine is not silly money. Three routes, cheapest first, with the payback measured against a £4 daily habit on working days.

RouteCostPayback at £3.60 saved a dayVerdict
Cafetiere or French press£10 to £20Under a weekIf you drink your coffee black, this is the whole answer
Moka pot or AeroPress + £10 frother£30 to £45Under three weeksThe sweet spot for most people
Bean to cup machine£200 to £500Two to five monthsFine if you will keep using it. Buy it second, not first
Pod machine£50 to £150Never fully, on refillsSkip it if the goal is money

Cafetiere. Makes good black coffee, no electricity beyond the kettle, nothing to break.

Moka pot or AeroPress. Both make something close to espresso strength. Add a handheld milk frother and you have a flat white that is genuinely close to the shop version. Total setup under £50, paid back in under three weeks.

Bean to cup. Real espresso, grinder built in, one button. Payback takes two to five months at a coffee a day, which is fine if you will keep using it. The graveyard of unused coffee machines exists for a reason, so buy this after a few months of the cheap route, not before.

Pods. They work out around 30p to 40p each, similar to beans, but you are locked into one brand and paying a premium forever on the refills. The machine is cheap because the refills are not, which is the same business model as printer ink.

5. The trap that ruins the maths

Buying £11 speciality beans every week undoes the whole exercise. Speciality coffee is a hobby, and a good one, but it is not a saving. If the goal is money, a £6 supermarket bag makes coffee that is better than most chains anyway, because it will be fresher than beans sitting in a hopper all day.

Watch the milk too. A daily oat milk habit at £2 a carton adds up faster than the coffee does, and it roughly doubles the milk line in the table above.

And be honest about what you are actually buying outside. For a lot of people the £4 is not the coffee, it is the ten minutes out of the building. A flask does not replace that, and pretending otherwise is why these plans collapse in week three.

6. Worked example: a realistic year

Not the fantasy version

Starting point: one £4 coffee on each of 250 working days. £1,000 a year.

The change: an AeroPress and a handheld frother, £42 once. One £6 bag of supermarket beans a fortnight, so 26 bags a year at £156. Milk, roughly £38 across the year. Electricity, roughly £6.

And one kept treat: one bought coffee a week, 50 weeks, £200. Because total bans fail and this is the version people actually stick to.

Year one total: £442. Against £1,000. Saved: £558, in a year that included buying the kit.

Year two: £400, because the kit is already paid for. Saved: £600, and it repeats every year after that without further effort.

The version with no treat saves about £700 a year and is the one most people abandon by March. £558 you keep beats £700 you do not.

7. Where the saved money should go

£600 a year sitting in a current account earns nothing, and money that stays where it was will quietly get spent on something else. Move it on the day you would have spent it, or on payday, into somewhere separate.

In rough order of what it is worth:

  1. Against a credit card balance charging 25% interest. This beats every savings rate available and it is not close, as our cashback card guide explains.
  2. Into an easy access savings account or an ISA. Small, real, and genuinely passive.

The honest verdict

The gap is real and it is large: roughly £4 outside against roughly 40p at home, which is about £900 a year on working days alone. Spend under £50 on an AeroPress and a frother and it pays for itself in three weeks.

But do not go to zero. Keep one bought coffee a week, move the difference somewhere separate on payday, and buy the £6 supermarket beans rather than the £11 speciality bag. That version saves around £600 a year and survives contact with February, which is the only test that matters.

And if the £4 is really about ten minutes out of the office rather than the drink, this article does not solve your problem. Take the ten minutes.

Frequently asked

Is instant coffee the cheapest option? Yes, at roughly 5p to 10p a cup. If you like it, the maths is unbeatable and there is nothing else to discuss. Most people asking this question are trying to replace a flat white, which instant does not do.

Does a cheap grinder make a real difference? More than a cheap machine does. Pre-ground coffee goes stale quickly once opened, and a basic burr grinder improves a moka pot or AeroPress noticeably. It is the upgrade worth making before a better brewer.

What about a flask for the commute? It works, and it is the single highest-return habit here, because it removes the queue you were walking past anyway. Fill it while the kettle is on for breakfast.

Is a bean to cup machine worth it eventually? If you are still making coffee at home six months in, yes. Buying one on day one is how people end up with an expensive appliance and their old habit intact.
