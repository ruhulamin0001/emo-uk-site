They Do Not Have To Tell You The Salary. They Do Have To Tell You Something Else

Every few months someone announces that UK employers must now advertise salaries. They must not. There is no such requirement, and a job advert saying competitive is entirely lawful. What does exist is a separate, mandatory, publicly searchable dataset that most candidates have never opened, and it answers a different but useful question.

The short version

  • No UK law requires a salary or a band in a job advert. Competitive and salary on application are both lawful, however annoying.
  • Employers with 250 or more employees must report gender pay gap data on a snapshot date each year, and publish it within a year of that date.
  • It is free to search. The government runs a public service where you can look up and compare any reporting employer's figures.
  • The guidance changed on 21 May 2026, replacing references to employees' gender with sex following the Supreme Court ruling on the definition of sex in the Equality Act 2010.
  • A pay gap is not a pay band, and does not prove unequal pay for equal work. It is a distribution figure, and reading it as anything else will mislead you.
  • Under 250 employees, there is usually nothing published at all, which is most employers in the country.

1. What the law actually requires, which is not much

Start with the disappointing part, plainly. There is no UK legal requirement to state a salary or a salary band in a job advertisement. An employer can advertise a role at competitive, or salary depending on experience, or say nothing about pay at all, and be entirely within the law.

This surprises people because pay transparency has been in the news continuously, and because rules elsewhere have moved. It is worth separating three things that get blurred together.

Voluntary practice. A growing number of UK employers publish bands because candidates increasingly filter out ads that do not, and because it reduces wasted interview time. That is a market response, not an obligation, and it can be withdrawn at any time.

Rules in other jurisdictions. Several countries and US states now require pay ranges in adverts. UK candidates sometimes see a band on a listing from a multinational and reasonably conclude the rules have changed here. They have not; the employer is complying with somebody else's law, or has chosen to apply one standard globally.

What you can always do. Nothing stops you asking. There is no rule preventing an employer telling you the budgeted range, and asking early is the single highest value question in any first conversation with a recruiter. The worst outcome is that they decline, which is itself information.

One thing that has genuinely changed in the other direction is worth knowing. Asking about salary history has fallen out of favour and is restricted in various places, but in the UK the relevant protection is narrower than people assume, so treat your current salary as information you choose to disclose rather than something you are obliged to hand over.

2. What employers do have to publish

Here is the dataset that does exist, is mandatory, and is free.

GOV.UK's statutory guidance is clear: any employer with 250 or more employees on a specific date each year, the snapshot date, must report their gender pay gap data. They must report and publish within a year of the snapshot date, and must do it for every year they are at or above 250 employees on that date. It applies in England, Scotland and Wales.

Crucially for a candidate, the results are not buried in a company's annual report. The government runs a public service where you can search and compare gender pay gap data by company or sector, at gender-pay-gap.service.gov.uk. It costs nothing and needs no account.

What you get for a reporting employer is a set of figures including mean and median pay gaps, bonus gaps, the proportion receiving bonuses, and the distribution of men and women across four pay quartiles. That last one is the most useful and the least discussed, and we come back to it below.

One recent change is worth flagging because it affects how the numbers are built. The guidance was updated on 21 May 2026 following the Supreme Court ruling on the definition of sex in the Equality Act 2010. The chapter formerly titled recording employees' gender was replaced with one on recording employees' sex, including what to do where an employee holds a Gender Recognition Certificate, and references to gender in the calculations chapter were changed to sex. If you are comparing a 2026 report with one from three years ago, the underlying definition has moved.

GOV.UK also added guidance in April 2026 on creating an action plan for employers with over 250 employees, which is a separate document from the raw figures and often more revealing.

And the limit, stated honestly: below 250 employees there is no obligation and usually nothing published. Most UK employers are below that line. For a small company, this entire section gives you nothing, and you are back to asking.

3. How to read a pay gap without fooling yourself

This is where candidates go wrong, and where a lot of commentary goes wrong too.

A gender pay gap is not unequal pay. Paying a man and a woman differently for the same work has been unlawful for decades. A pay gap measures something else: the difference in average pay between all men and all women at an employer, across every role. An organisation can have a large gap with perfectly equal pay in every role, if the senior roles are mostly held by one sex and the junior roles by the other.

So what is it actually good for, if you are trying to work out what a job pays?

Read the quartiles, not the headline. The quartile distribution tells you how people are spread from the lowest paid quarter to the highest. An employer with a balanced upper quartile promotes across the board. One where the top quarter looks nothing like the bottom quarter has a progression problem, whatever its headline number says. For a candidate, progression is closer to what you want to know than any single percentage.

Compare within a sector, not across sectors. Gaps vary enormously by industry for structural reasons. An employer with a 14% gap in a sector averaging 20% is doing something different from one with 14% in a sector averaging 4%. The government service lets you compare by sector, which is the whole reason to use it rather than reading one company's page.

Read three or four years, not one. A single year can move on one senior departure. A trend is much harder to fake, and reporting has run long enough now that most large employers have a run of years to look at.

Read the narrative and the action plan. Employers often publish a commentary alongside the numbers. A report that explains its gap in specific terms, with dated commitments, is a different signal from one with a page of photographs. You are not grading their diversity work. You are finding out whether this is an organisation that says true things about itself in public.

What none of this gives you is the band for the job you applied for. It gives you context, and a question or two that will make a hiring manager sit up.

4. Worked example: Katya in Bolton, two offers and no published band

Katya is a data analyst in Bolton with two live processes. Employer A is a logistics firm with about 900 staff. Employer B is a consultancy with about 60. Neither advertised a salary. Both said competitive.

Employer A, 900 staff, above the 250 threshold. She searches the government service and finds several years of reports. The headline median gap is 11%, which tells her little on its own. The quartiles tell her more: the lower quartile is 64% women, the upper quartile 29% women. Over four years the headline gap has narrowed by two points while the upper quartile has barely moved, which suggests the improvement came from hiring at the bottom rather than promoting through the middle.

That gives her one very good interview question: how many of the people in your most senior analyst roles were promoted internally in the last three years? It is specific, it is fair, and it is derived from something they published themselves.

Employer B, 60 staff, below the threshold. Nothing published. No obligation, no data, nothing to search. Everything she learns has to come from asking directly. So she asks the recruiter, in the first call, what range is budgeted for the role, and follows with what it would take to be at the top of it rather than the bottom.

What she does not do. She does not use employer A's 11% figure as evidence of anything about her own offer, because it is not. It is a distribution statistic about 900 people, not a statement about what one analyst job pays.

Where the actual number comes from. Both offers land through asking, corroborated against advertised roles at comparable employers in the same region. On the National Living Wage floor and the £12,570 Personal Allowance frozen, she also checks what each offer is worth after tax rather than comparing headline figures, because a £2,000 difference in gross is not £2,000 in her pocket.

The honest summary of her research: the public data told her something real about employer A and nothing at all about employer B, and in both cases the salary itself came from asking a person. That is the current state of pay transparency in Britain, and dressing it up as anything more would not help anyone.

The honest verdict

The clean answer is that British employers do not have to tell you what a job pays, and anyone saying the rules have changed is describing another country's law or a voluntary practice.

What does exist is genuinely useful and almost nobody uses it. Any employer with 250 or more staff must publish gender pay gap data, and it is free and searchable on a government service. It will not tell you the band, but the quartile breakdown tells you whether people progress there, which over a career matters more than the starting number.

Read it properly or not at all. A pay gap is a distribution figure, not proof of unequal pay, and a single year's headline percentage is close to meaningless. Sector comparison, several years, and the quartiles are where the information is.

And for the majority of employers, the ones under 250 staff, there is no data and there never will be. For those, the only transparency available is the question you ask in the first conversation, so ask it early and ask it plainly.

Frequently asked

Does a UK job advert have to include the salary? No. There is no legal requirement to state a salary or a band in a job advert in the UK. Many employers now do so voluntarily because candidates prefer it, but they are free to stop.

Which employers must publish pay data? Those with 250 or more employees on their snapshot date must report gender pay gap data and publish within a year of that date, for every year they are at or above 250 on the snapshot date. Below that, there is generally nothing published.

Where can I look a company up? The government runs a free public service at gender-pay-gap.service.gov.uk where you can search and compare data by employer and by sector. No account is needed.

Does a big pay gap mean women are paid less for the same job? Not by itself. Unequal pay for equal work is separately unlawful. A pay gap compares average pay across all roles, so it mostly reflects how men and women are distributed between senior and junior positions. The quartile breakdown is the part that tells you about progression.
