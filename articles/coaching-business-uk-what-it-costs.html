Nobody Can Stop You Calling Yourself A Coach. One Regulator Can Fine You

Coaching has no licence, no protected title and no legally required qualification in the UK, which is why the accreditation industry sells so hard. There is one cost the law does impose on almost every coach who keeps client notes, it is about the price of a single session, and it is the one that gets skipped.

The short version

  • Coaching is not a regulated profession in the UK. There is no licence, no protected title and no qualification you must hold to practise or to charge.
  • The ICO data protection fee is the real compulsory cost. Under the Data Protection (Charges and Information) Regulations 2018, organisations including sole traders that use personal information must pay it unless exempt.
  • Tier 1 is £52 a year, or £47 if you pay by direct debit, and covers micro organisations with turnover up to £632,000 or no more than 10 staff.
  • Not everyone has to pay. There are exemptions, and the ICO runs a free self assessment that takes about ten minutes. Do that rather than guessing either way.
  • Insurance is a commercial choice, not a legal one, but most corporate clients and most venues will not engage you without it.
  • Tax starts at £1,000 of gross income, the trading allowance, which at £60 a session is 17 sessions a year.

1. Why nobody can stop you, and what that really means

Start with the fact that shapes everything else. In the United Kingdom, life coaching, business coaching and executive coaching are not regulated professions. There is no statutory register, no protected title, and no qualification you are legally obliged to hold before you take a client's money.

That is genuinely different from professions where the title is protected and practising without registration is an offence. Nobody is coming to check your certificate, because there is no certificate anyone is required to have.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

The barrier to entry is almost nothing. You can begin this week, with clients you already know, at a price you set, without asking permission from anyone. That is the honest and underused good news, and it is why coaching keeps appearing in side hustle lists.

And that is precisely why the accreditation industry works so hard. When no qualification is required, qualifications have to be sold on something other than necessity, so they are sold on credibility, confidence and the implication that you are not allowed to start without one. Read any course page carefully and you will notice it never quite says the training is legally required, because it is not.

None of which makes training worthless. A good programme gives you a method, supervision and people to practise on, and those are real. It makes it a business investment to be judged on return like any other, not a gate you must pass through. We come back to how to judge it below.

One limit worth stating plainly, because it is where unregulated coaching does run into the law. Coaching is not therapy, and it is not regulated healthcare. If you find yourself working with a client's mental health condition, you are outside coaching and into territory where regulation, competence and referral all matter. Know where your edge is and who you refer to.

2. The one cost the law does impose

Here is the requirement that the free-to-start framing hides, and it catches almost every coach.

The Information Commissioner's Office states it directly: under the Data Protection (Charges and Information) Regulations 2018, organisations including sole traders that use personal information need to pay a data protection fee, unless they are exempt.

A coach uses personal information as a matter of course. Client names and contact details, session notes, goals, what someone told you about their job and their marriage, invoices, a mailing list. If you keep any of that on a computer or a phone, you are processing personal data, and the sole trader wording means working alone does not exempt you.

The fee at tier 1 is £52 a year, reduced to £47 if you pay by direct debit. Tier 1 covers micro organisations, which the ICO defines as having a maximum turnover of £632,000 for the financial year or no more than 10 members of staff. Every new coach is comfortably inside that.

Fifty two pounds. That is the entire mandatory cost of entry to this profession, and it is roughly one session.

Two honest qualifications. There are exemptions, and some very small operations do fall outside the requirement depending on what they do with the data. Rather than guessing, the ICO publishes a free self assessment that takes about ten minutes and tells you whether you need to pay and how much. Do that. Guessing in either direction is the expensive option: paying when you need not is £52 wasted, and not paying when you should is a matter for the regulator.

Paying the fee is also not the same as complying with data protection law. It is the registration charge, not a certificate of compliance. You still need to handle client notes sensibly, tell clients what you do with their information, and be able to delete it on request. For a one person coaching practice that is a page of writing and some thought about where the notes live, not a project.

3. The costs that are choices, ranked by whether they pay

Everything below is optional in law. That does not make it optional in practice, and the ranking is by how reliably each one earns its money back.

1. Professional indemnity and public liability insurance. Not legally required for coaching, and close to unavoidable commercially. Most corporate clients will ask for a certificate before they will contract with you, and most hired rooms require public liability. If you intend to work with organisations rather than only individuals, treat this as a cost of trading. Our guides to professional indemnity cover for freelancers and public liability for a side hustle set out what each one actually covers.

2. A way to take payment and book sessions. A scheduling tool and a payment processor, or an all in one platform. Modest monthly cost, saves real hours, and removes the awkward conversation about whether someone has paid. Worth it from about your fifth regular client.

3. Supervision. A more experienced coach you take your difficult cases to. It is the thing most likely to make you better at the work, it is not a qualification, and it is usually cheaper than a course. Underrated.

4. A website. Useful, rarely the constraint early on. Almost all first clients come from people who already know you. Build it when you have something to put on it.

5. Accreditation. Last, deliberately. It is the largest cost and the least certain return, and it is sold as the first step. Judge it on one question: will the clients you actually want ask for it? Some corporate procurement processes do, in which case it converts directly into work. Individuals almost never do. If your market is individuals, a course you take for the method and the practice is a reasonable purchase; a course you take to be allowed to start is money spent on a permission you already had.

A blunt sequencing rule that saves people thousands: get the first three paying clients before you buy anything except the ICO fee. Those three will tell you what your market actually wants, and the answer is frequently not what the course sold you.

4. Worked example: Ade in Doncaster, first twelve months

Ade is an operations manager in Doncaster who starts coaching people moving into first line management, at £60 an hour, evenings and Saturday mornings.

Month one. He pays the ICO fee by direct debit, £47, after running the ten minute self assessment. He writes a short note for clients about what he records and how long he keeps it. He spends nothing else. He takes three clients from his own network, fortnightly.

Break even on the compulsory cost: one session. £60 against £47. It happens in week one.

Months two to six. Three clients, fortnightly, is roughly 6 sessions a month, so £360 a month. He crosses the £1,000 trading allowance in his third month, at 17 sessions, and registers for Self Assessment. He adds a scheduling and payment tool because chasing bank transfers is costing him an evening a month.

Month seven. A local employer asks about coaching three of its supervisors. Procurement asks for proof of professional indemnity insurance. This is the moment insurance stops being optional, and note what triggered it: a client asked. He did not need it for the first six months and buying it in month one would have been six months of premium for nothing.

Month twelve. Eight regular clients, about 16 sessions a month, roughly £960 a month and £8,000 for the year net of the quiet start. Against that: the ICO fee, the insurance from month seven, and the booking tool. He is nowhere near the £90,000 VAT registration threshold.

The accreditation he did not buy. In month one he was close to spending several thousand pounds on a programme. Twelve months in, exactly one client has asked about qualifications, and that was the corporate one, which was satisfied by insurance and two references. He may still do the training, and now he will do it to get better rather than to feel allowed, which is a different purchase.

The number that matters in this example is not the £8,000. It is the £47. The gap between what coaching legally costs to start and what the internet says it costs is the single most useful fact in this article.

The honest verdict

The honest cost of legally starting a coaching business in the UK is about £52, and possibly nothing if you qualify for an exemption. There is no licence to buy, no title to earn and no regulator to satisfy beyond the ICO.

That single fact should change the order in which you spend money. The accreditation sold as step one is the least certain investment on the list, and the only test that matters is whether the clients you want will actually ask for it. Individuals almost never do; corporate procurement sometimes does, and it usually asks about insurance first.

So: run the ICO self assessment, pay the fee if it applies, write the page about what you do with client notes, and go and get three paying clients. Buy insurance when a client asks for it, which they will if you go anywhere near organisations, and buy training when you want to be better rather than to feel permitted.

And keep the boundary in view. Coaching is not therapy. The absence of regulation is why starting is cheap, and it is also why knowing the edge of your competence is entirely your own responsibility.

Frequently asked

Do I need a qualification to be a coach in the UK? No. Coaching is not a regulated profession, there is no protected title and no qualification is legally required to practise or charge. Training may still be a good commercial decision, particularly if you want corporate clients.

Do I really have to register with the ICO? The ICO says organisations including sole traders that use personal information must pay the data protection fee unless exempt, and a coach keeping client notes is using personal information. There are exemptions, so run the ICO's free self assessment, which takes about ten minutes, rather than assuming either way.

How much is the ICO fee? Tier 1, covering micro organisations with turnover up to £632,000 or no more than 10 staff, is £52 a year, or £47 if you pay by direct debit. Higher tiers apply to larger organisations.

When do I have to tell HMRC? When your gross trading income across all side activities passes £1,000 in a tax year, which at £60 a session is 17 sessions. Gross means fees received before your costs come off.
