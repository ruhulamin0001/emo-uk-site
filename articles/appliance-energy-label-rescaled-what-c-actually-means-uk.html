The Letter on the Energy Label Is Not the Number That Pays Your Bill

In March 2021 the energy label was rescaled. An appliance that had been A+++ on Monday was a C on Tuesday, without a single component changing. Millions of people are still shopping as though a letter means something absolute. It does not. The small kilowatt hour figure printed underneath it does, and turning it into money takes one multiplication.

The short version

  • The label was rescaled on 1 March 2021. The plusses were abolished and the scale went back to a simple A to G. Products did not change. Only the labels did.
  • An old A+++ typically became a C. Old A++ and A+ products dropped further still. If you own an appliance bought before 2021, its rating on today's scale is worse and its electricity use is identical.
  • A, B and C were deliberately left empty at launch. The top bands were reserved for future improvements, which is why almost everything on a shop floor in 2026 sits at C, D, E or F.
  • The letter is a ranking within a product group, not a cost. A C rated tumble dryer and a C rated fridge are nowhere near each other in running cost.
  • The kWh figure is the number to read. Multiply it by 26.32p, the Ofgem electricity rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, and you have the annual running cost in pounds.
  • One letter is worth roughly £5 to £25 a year on most appliances, which is why a £200 premium for a better letter usually fails the payback test, and occasionally passes it on a heavy user like a tumble dryer.

1. What actually happened in March 2021

The original label ran A to G. Then appliances got better, everything bunched up at the top, and the response was to bolt on A+, then A++, then A+++. By the late 2010s a shop floor could contain nothing below A+, which made the label useless for its one job: telling you which of two machines is better.

So on 1 March 2021 the whole scale was reset. The plusses went. The scale returned to A to G, and the thresholds were redrawn so that the top bands were hard to reach. A, B and C were left effectively empty at launch, on purpose, to leave manufacturers somewhere to go.

The effect on the day was that an appliance rated A+++ under the old scale generally landed at C under the new one, and some fell further. The machine was the same machine. The electricity it used was the same electricity. Only the sticker changed.

Nothing got worse in March 2021. The ruler got longer, and everything that had been crowded at the top was spread back out.

The rescale applied to a defined set of product groups: dishwashers, washing machines and washer dryers, refrigerators and freezers including wine coolers, lamps, and electronic displays including televisions. Other products, notably tumble dryers and ovens, were not part of that first rescale, which is a detail that trips people up constantly and is covered below.

2. How to read a label in ten seconds

The letter and the arrow take up most of the label and deserve about a tenth of your attention. Here is the order to read it in.

First, find the kWh number. It is in a box below the letter. On a fridge or freezer it is kWh per year. On a washing machine or dishwasher it is kWh per 100 cycles, which you then scale to how often you actually wash.

Second, multiply it by your electricity rate. At the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, that is 26.32p per kWh for a direct debit customer in England, Scotland or Wales. Note that there is no VAT on electricity between 1 October 2026 and 31 March 2027, so if you are comparing against an older calculation, the rates are not like for like.

Third, ignore the letter unless you are comparing two machines of the same type and size. Within one product group the letter is a fair ranking. Across product groups it means nothing at all.

Fourth, check the QR code. Every rescaled label carries one, and it links to the official product database entry with the full test data. That is the manufacturer's declared performance, not a retailer's description.

This is the question the label refuses to answer, so here it is answered in money. Every figure below is the appliance's own declared annual consumption multiplied by 26.32p. Over a ten year life that is £180 to £400, which is a real sum and worth having, but it is not the transformational figure that a big green A on a shelf edge implies.

And then there is the tumble dryer row, where the gap is £90 a year and the ten year figure is around £900. That is the one appliance where efficiency genuinely dominates the purchase decision, which is why it is covered separately in our guide to second freezer and chest freezer running costs and in the comparison of replacing versus keeping a dishwasher.

4. When paying more for a better letter works

The rule is simple and it is the same rule as everywhere else on this site. Take the price difference between the two machines, divide by the annual running cost difference, and you have the payback in years. Compare that against how long the thing will actually last.

The formula. price premium ÷ annual saving = payback in years.

Example that passes. A heat pump dryer costs £250 more than a condenser and saves £90 a year. £250 ÷ £90 = 2.8 years against a 10 year life. Buy it.

Example that fails. A fridge freezer costs £300 more and saves £20 a year. £300 ÷ £20 = 15 years against a 10 year life. Do not buy it.

Three things that quietly ruin this calculation and are worth knowing about.

The test cycle is not your cycle. A washing machine's declared figure is measured on the eco programme, which is long and cool. If you always use a 40 degree quick wash, you will use more than the label says, and the gap between two machines will be narrower than the label implies. The label is a fair comparison between machines, not a prediction of your bill.

Bigger appliances use more, whatever the letter. A large A rated fridge freezer can easily use more electricity than a small C rated one. The letter is normalised for size within its group. Your electricity meter is not.

Not every product was rescaled. Tumble dryers and ovens still carry the older style label with plusses in many cases. So an A+++ tumble dryer next to a C rated washing machine is not a worse machine, it is a different scale entirely. This is the single most confusing thing on a British shop floor and almost nobody explains it.

5. Worked example: the Ferreira kitchen in Reading

A family of four in Reading replacing a fridge freezer that died. Two candidates, same size, same brand tier. One is rated E and declares 260 kWh a year. The other is rated C and declares 160 kWh a year. The C rated one is £220 more.

Running cost, E rated. 260 kWh × 26.32p = £68 a year.

Running cost, C rated. 160 kWh × 26.32p = £42 a year.

Annual saving. £26.

Payback on the £220 premium. £220 ÷ £26 = 8.5 years.

Expected life of a fridge freezer. Around 10 to 12 years.

So it just about works, and it works on a knife edge. If the premium had been £300 it would not. If the family keeps appliances for fifteen years it comfortably does. And if they are the kind of household that replaces a kitchen every eight years, it does not.

What would change the answer more than anything else is the warranty. If the cheaper machine comes with two years and the dearer one with five, the extra three years of cover is worth more than the £26 a year, and that is a comparison you can make from the retailers' own published terms rather than from a sticker. It is also the reason our extended warranty guide exists.

Frequently asked

Is my old A+++ appliance now bad? No. It uses exactly the same electricity it always did. What has changed is that you can no longer compare its sticker to a new machine's sticker. To compare properly, find its declared kWh figure on the original documentation or the manufacturer's site and put it next to the new one's.

Why is nothing rated A? Because A was deliberately left empty when the scale was reset, to give manufacturers somewhere to improve towards. A handful of products have reached A and B in the years since. If you see an A rated appliance in 2026 it is genuinely exceptional, and it will be priced accordingly.

Does the letter affect anything other than my bill? It affects resale and, in rented property, it contributes nothing to an EPC. Appliances are not part of an EPC assessment. If you are a landlord improving a property's rating, this is the wrong place to spend, and our EPC C by 2030 guide covers what does count.

Where do I find the official figure? Scan the QR code on the label. It takes you to the registered product entry, which is the manufacturer's declaration submitted under the regulations, and it is more reliable than any retailer product page.

The honest verdict

Stop shopping on the letter. It was reset in March 2021 for good reasons, it only ranks machines against others of the same type, and a C in 2026 is a perfectly ordinary appliance rather than a poor one.

Read the kWh figure, multiply it by 26.32p, and compare running costs in pounds. Then divide any price premium by the difference. If the payback is longer than the appliance will last, the better letter is not worth paying for, and on most fridges, washing machines and dishwashers it will not be.

The exception is the tumble dryer, where the gap between technologies is around £90 a year rather than £20, and where paying more genuinely does come back. That is the one appliance where the efficiency question should drive the purchase.
