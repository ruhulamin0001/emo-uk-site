Your Council Tax Band Is A 1991 Valuation, And The Free Price Data Starts In 1995

Challenging your council tax band is one of the few genuinely free ways to cut a recurring household bill. It is also the one where most attempts fail on evidence rather than on merit, because the sale prices you are allowed to use come from a window the free public data does not reach. Here is how it actually works.

The short version

  • Bands are based on 1 April 1991 values in England and 1 April 2003 in Wales. Not today's value, and not what you paid.
  • You have a legal right to challenge if you have been paying council tax on the property for less than 6 months, or the Valuation Office changed your band in the last 6 months, or the property or the local area has physically changed.
  • Otherwise it is a band review, which you can still ask for, but you must supply the evidence.
  • The evidence: up to 5 similar properties in a lower band, matching yours on type, size, age, style and design, in the same street or estate.
  • The sale price window is 1 April 1989 to 31 March 1993 for England and 1 April 2001 to 31 March 2005 for Wales. Free public sale price data only goes back to 1995, so the easiest evidence route does not reach the English window.
  • Timing: up to 4 months for a proposal, up to 12 months for a band review. Keep paying your council tax throughout, because you must.
  • The Valuation Office decides the correct band, and GOV.UK describes the outcome only as "change your Council Tax band". It does not promise the change is downwards.

1. What your band is actually based on

Start here, because almost every failed challenge starts with the wrong question.

Council tax bands are based on what a property was worth on 1 April 1991 in England and 1 April 2003 in Wales. England has never been revalued. A house built in 2019 was given a band by estimating what it would have been worth in 1991, had it existed.

So "my house is not worth that much" and "I paid less than my neighbour" are both irrelevant unless you can turn them into a statement about 1991. The bands:

AUp to £40,000Up to £44,000
BOver £40,000 up to £52,000Over £44,000 up to £65,000
COver £52,000 up to £68,000Over £65,000 up to £91,000
DOver £68,000 up to £88,000Over £91,000 up to £123,000
EOver £88,000 up to £120,000Over £123,000 up to £162,000
FOver £120,000 up to £160,000Over £162,000 up to £223,000
GOver £160,000 up to £320,000Over £223,000 up to £324,000
HOver £320,000Over £324,000 up to £424,000
INot applicableOver £424,000

Scotland is a separate system: challenges go to an assessor at your local Valuation Joint Board or council rather than to the Valuation Office.

2. Legal right to challenge, or band review

There are two routes and they are not equally strong. Knowing which one you are on tells you how much evidence you need and how long you will wait.

You have a legal right to challenge, called making a proposal, if:

  • you have been paying council tax on the property for less than 6 months, or
  • the Valuation Office has changed your band in the last 6 months, or
  • there has been a change affecting the property's value: it has been split or merged, its use has changed (for example part of it is now used for business), or the local area has changed physically, for example a new supermarket or main road has been built, or
  • you want the property removed from, or added to, the council tax list.

That first bullet is the one worth acting on. If you moved in within the last six months you are on the strong route and the clock is running. Most people who could have used it find out a year later.

The third bullet is under-used too. A new dual carriageway or a large retail development near your street is a physical change to the local area, and it is on the list.

If none of those apply, you ask for a band review instead. You can do this if you have been paying for more than six months and there have been no recent changes but you think the band is wrong. It is a real route and the Valuation Office does act on it, but you carry the evidence burden and the wait is longer.

3. The evidence that works, and the window that traps people

For a band review you need evidence, and GOV.UK is specific about what counts.

Comparable properties. Addresses for up to 5 similar properties in a lower band than yours. Similar means matching on:

  • type, so a semi-detached house is compared with semi-detached houses
  • size, meaning number of bedrooms and total area
  • age
  • style and design

And they must be in the same street or estate if you are in a town or city, or the same village or area if you are in the countryside. Anyone can look up a neighbour's band for free on the Check your Council Tax band service, so this evidence costs nothing but time.

Sale prices, and here is the trap. You can use the price your property or similar properties sold for, but only if the sale falls between:

1 April 1989 and 31 March 1993, if your property is in England. 1 April 2001 and 31 March 2005, if your property is in Wales.

Now put that next to the next sentence on the same GOV.UK page: you can look up property sale prices online from 1995 onwards.

Read those together. For an English property, the free public sale price data begins two years after the last date you are allowed to use. The easiest evidence route, the one every "reclaim your council tax" article points you at, does not reach the English window at all.

Welsh homeowners are luckier: the 2001 to 2005 window sits comfortably inside the published data, so sale price evidence is genuinely available.

So in England the comparable properties route is the realistic one, and sale price evidence is only available if you happen to hold paperwork from a sale in that four-year window, such as your own purchase deeds if you bought then.

If you do have a qualifying sale price, GOV.UK asks for the addresses, the prices and the dates, noting that the closer the date is to the valuation date the better.

4. Worked example: building a case in an afternoon

Worked example: Martina in Doncaster

Martina lives in a 1970s semi-detached house in a street of thirty near-identical houses. She has lived there eleven years, so she has no legal right to challenge and is on the band review route.

Step one, thirty minutes. She uses the free Check your Council Tax band service to look up every house in her street. Her house is band D. Four of the near-identical houses on the same side of the street are band C.

Step two. She checks they genuinely match: same 1970s build, same semi-detached type, same three bedrooms, same estate design. Two of them she can see from her window are the mirror image of hers. That satisfies type, size, age, style and design.

Step three, the sale price question. She looks for what these houses sold for between 1 April 1989 and 31 March 1993, and finds nothing, because the free data starts in 1995. She does not have deeds from that period. So she submits on comparable properties alone, with four addresses, which is inside the "up to 5" allowance.

Step four. She submits through the online service and keeps paying her council tax, because GOV.UK requires it while a challenge is reviewed.

What happens then. The Valuation Office confirms receipt, usually within a few days but potentially up to 28 days. Because this is a band review rather than a proposal, the decision can take up to 12 months.

Her odds, honestly. Four identical neighbours in a lower band is the exact evidence GOV.UK asks for. It is not a guarantee, because the Valuation Office decides what the correct band is, and her house may be right and the four neighbours wrong.

5. What happens next, and how long

Timings, from GOV.UK:

StageHow long
Confirmation that they have your challengeUsually a few days, sometimes up to 28 days
Decision on a proposal (legal right route)Up to 4 months
Decision on a band reviewUp to 12 months

The Valuation Office will then do one of four things: change your band and tell the council, which revises your bill and pays any refund due; remove the property from the list; add it to the list with a new band; or tell you why the band cannot be changed. You can challenge the decision if you disagree with it.

Two practical points. You must continue paying council tax while your challenge is reviewed. Withholding payment does not strengthen your case. And if you are challenging for someone else, you need an Authority to act form and a paper challenge form sent together, because the online service does not cover that.

Now the caution that this genre of article routinely leaves out. The Valuation Office's job is to establish the correct band, and GOV.UK describes one of the possible outcomes simply as "change your Council Tax band". It does not say the change will be downwards, and it would be dishonest of us to imply that a review can only help you. If your evidence is that four identical neighbours are in a lower band, one possible reading is that your band is right and theirs is wrong. Go in knowing that.

6. The honest verdict

This is a free process with a real payoff, and a successful challenge cuts a bill you pay every year and can bring a refund with it. There is genuinely nothing to buy and no reason to use a company that offers to do it for a share of the savings.

Two things decide whether it is worth your afternoon. Have you been paying for less than six months? If so you are on the strong route with a four month decision, and you should act now because the window closes. Can you find similar properties in a lower band on your own street? If so you have the evidence GOV.UK actually asks for.

If neither is true, be realistic. In England the sale price route is largely closed by the 1995 start of the free data, and a band review with weak comparables is a twelve month wait for a likely no.

And do not treat it as risk-free. The Valuation Office decides the correct band, not the lower one. That is a reason to build a proper case rather than a reason not to try, but you should know it before you press submit.

Frequently asked

Can my band actually go up? GOV.UK lists the possible outcomes as changing your band, removing the property from the list, adding it to the list, or telling you why the band cannot be changed, and it does not state that a change can only be downwards. The Valuation Office is deciding the correct band. Treat a review as a genuine review rather than a one-way bet.

Is it different in Scotland? Yes. In Scotland you submit to an assessor based in your local Valuation Joint Board or council rather than to the Valuation Office, and you find yours through the Scottish Assessors Association website. The evidence principles are similar but the process and dates differ.

Should I use a company that does this for me? There is no need. Checking neighbours' bands is free, the online challenge service is free, and the evidence GOV.UK asks for is a list of addresses. A firm taking a percentage of your refund is charging for a form you can submit yourself in an afternoon.

What if my band changed because of a neighbour's challenge? That is one of the situations giving you a legal right to challenge: if the Valuation Office has changed your band in the last 6 months, you are on the proposal route rather than the band review route, with a shorter decision time.
