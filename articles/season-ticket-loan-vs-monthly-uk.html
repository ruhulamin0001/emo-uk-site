Two Ways to Pay for the Same Commute, and One Is Better Even If You Have the Cash

An interest-free season ticket loan from your employer is tax-free below a threshold most people have never heard of. It also beats paying up front, for a reason that has nothing to do with whether you can afford it. Here is a full year priced four ways.

The short version

  • An interest-free employer loan of under £10,000 is tax-free. HMRC exempts beneficial loans with a combined outstanding value below £10,000 throughout the whole tax year. No benefit in kind, no reporting, no National Insurance.
  • The loan beats paying cash even if you have the cash. With Bank Rate at 3.75% since the Monetary Policy Committee held on 16 September 2026, money left in a savings account earns while the season ticket is repaid from pay.
  • On a £3,000 annual ticket, the opportunity cost of paying up front is roughly £56 over the year, on the arithmetic below. Small, but it is free money for a form.
  • The real saving is annual against monthly, not loan against cash. Where an annual ticket is priced at 40 times the weekly rate, the gap against buying monthly is the number that matters.
  • Break even is the month you stop travelling. An annual ticket bought with a loan loses money if you leave the job, move house or change to hybrid working before roughly month nine.

1. What an employer season ticket loan actually is

A season ticket loan is one of the oldest staff benefits in the country and one of the least understood. The employer buys the ticket, or lends you the money to buy it, and takes the repayments out of your net pay over twelve months. There is no interest.

The reason employers can do this without creating a tax headache is a specific HMRC exemption. Under the rules on beneficial loans, an employer does not have to report anything or pay tax and National Insurance where the loan has a combined outstanding value to an employee of less than £10,000 throughout the whole tax year.

Three details inside that sentence do real work.

  • Combined. It is all beneficial loans from that employer added together, not each one separately. A £4,000 season ticket loan alongside a £7,000 relocation loan breaches the threshold.
  • Outstanding. It is the balance still owed, not the original amount. A loan that starts at £11,000 and drops below £10,000 in month two has still breached the threshold, because the test applies throughout the year.
  • Salary sacrifice is different. HMRC is explicit that loans made as part of a salary sacrifice arrangement must be reported. A season ticket loan repaid from net pay is not salary sacrifice, and that distinction is what keeps it clean.

Below £10,000, then, the loan is genuinely free. Not low cost. Free.

2. The part people get wrong: loan against cash

Plenty of people who could afford the annual ticket outright pay for it outright, on the reasoning that borrowing is for people who have to. On an interest-free loan, that reasoning costs money.

The Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 16 September 2026, by six votes to three, with three members voting for 4%. Easy access savings accounts track that rate closely. So money you do not hand over in April keeps earning until it is repaid.

Because a loan is repaid in twelve equal instalments, the average balance you keep hold of across the year is roughly half the total. On a £3,000 ticket that is an average of £1,500 in your account for twelve months. At 3.75% that is £56, before tax on the interest.

An interest-free loan you repay from pay is the only form of borrowing that is strictly better than paying cash. Turning it down out of a general dislike of debt costs you the interest your own money would have earned.

3. Annual against monthly, which is the bigger number

The loan question is worth about £56. The annual-against-monthly question is worth ten times that, and it is the one to get right.

UK rail season tickets are conventionally structured so that a weekly ticket costs roughly the same as five day returns, a monthly ticket costs about 3.84 times the weekly, and an annual costs about 40 times the weekly. The practical effect is that an annual ticket gives you roughly twelve months of travel for the price of about ten.

Check your own route rather than trusting that ratio, because it varies and operators price differently. But as a planning rule, expect the annual to save somewhere around 15% to 20% against rolling monthlies.

On a route where monthly tickets cost £300, twelve of them is £3,600 and the annual is likely to sit near £3,000. That is a £600 gap, and it dwarfs everything else in this article.

4. The break-even that decides it

An annual ticket is a bet that you will keep making the journey. Here is how to price the bet.

If the annual costs the equivalent of ten months of monthly tickets, you break even at month ten. Travel for eleven months and you are a month ahead. Stop at month eight and you have overpaid, unless you can get a refund.

Season ticket refunds are calculated on a formula that is deliberately unkind to short holdings. The refund is normally the price paid minus the cost of the tickets you would have bought for the period you used, minus an administration fee. Hand back an annual ticket after four months and you often recover far less than two thirds.

So the question to ask before committing is not whether the annual is cheaper. It is whether you are confident about the next nine months. Three situations where the answer is usually no:

  • You are in a probation period or actively job hunting. If you leave, most employers deduct the outstanding loan balance from your final pay, and you are then holding a ticket you cannot use on a route you no longer travel.
  • Your employer is reviewing office attendance. A move from four days to two halves the value of the ticket overnight.
  • You are house hunting. A change of station can make the ticket worthless.

Worked example: Dominic in Peterborough

Dominic commutes into London four days a week. Monthly tickets on his route cost £520. The annual is £5,400.

Twelve monthlies would cost £6,240. The annual saves him £840 a year, or 13.5%.

Break-even: £5,400 divided by £520 is 10.4, so he is ahead from part way through month eleven of use. Put differently, he needs to still be making the journey in July to have won.

The loan. His employer offers an interest-free loan. At £5,400 it is comfortably under the £10,000 threshold, so there is nothing for anyone to report and no benefit in kind. Had he also taken a £5,000 relocation loan, the combined balance would have breached £10,000 and the whole thing would have become reportable.

The cash question. He could pay the £5,400 from savings. Repaying over twelve months instead leaves an average of £2,700 in his account for the year, worth about £101 at 3.75% before tax on the interest. Not life changing, but it is the same ticket for £101 less.

Total against the worst option. Buying monthly with no loan costs £6,240. Buying annually with the employer loan costs £5,400 and keeps £101 of interest working. The gap is £941 a year, for one form.

5. Frequently asked

Does my employer have to offer this? No. There is no obligation. The exemption simply means it costs an employer nothing in tax terms to offer, which is a reasonable argument to put to a small employer who has never considered it.

What if I leave before the loan is repaid? Almost every scheme deducts the outstanding balance from your final pay. Check the wording, because some deduct from net pay in one go, which can make a final payslip uncomfortable.

Is a season ticket loan the same as salary sacrifice? No, and the difference matters. Salary sacrifice reduces gross pay before tax. A season ticket loan is repaid from net pay and gives no income tax or National Insurance saving at all. Its benefit is the interest you do not pay and the cash flow you keep.

Can I use it for a bus or tram pass? Usually yes. The HMRC exemption is about the loan, not about what the loan buys, so any travel pass your employer is willing to fund works the same way.

6. The honest verdict

If your employer offers an interest-free season ticket loan and you are confident you will still be making the same journey in nine months, take it and buy annually. On a typical route that combination is worth somewhere between £600 and £950 a year against buying monthly, and it is worth taking even if you could pay cash.

If you are within a probation period, expecting a change to office attendance, or house hunting, buy monthly and accept the premium. The flexibility is worth more than the discount, and a refund formula will not rescue you.

The one thing to check before signing: whether any other loan from the same employer pushes your combined balance over £10,000, because that single threshold is what keeps the arrangement tax-free.
