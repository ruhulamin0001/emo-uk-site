Every checking tool is gone

If you have read a "how to spot fake Amazon reviews" article in the last few years, it almost certainly told you to paste the product URL into Fakespot or ReviewMeta. Do not. All three of the well-known checkers are dead, and sending anyone to them wastes their time.

  • Fakespot: acquired by Mozilla, which shut the in-Firefox Review Checker on 10 June 2025 and the service itself on 1 July 2025.
  • ReviewMeta: dead. The site returns HTTP 403.
  • TheReviewIndex: dead. Its own site says it is permanently down due to Amazon's policy changes.

Checked 21 August 2026.

The common cause matters more than the individual closures. All three depended on scraped review data, and Amazon's API and policy changes cut that supply off. That constraint has not gone away, which means the wave of tools now advertising themselves as Fakespot alternatives are building on the same sand. Assume any tool promising an automated authenticity score is either guessing or about to close.

Manual inspection is what is left. The rest of this article is how to do it properly, and it takes about ninety seconds per product once you know what you are looking at.

How bad is it really

Honestly, the scale is contested and you should know that before you read a scary number anywhere.

One widely cited compilation puts fake or inauthentic Amazon reviews at around 43%, with a global consumer cost of 770 billion dollars in 2025. Amazon's position is that more than 99% of products in its store had only genuine reviews. Those two claims are not reconcilable, and the 43% figure traces back to Fakespot-era estimation, which is to say the methodology died with the tool.

What is documented on both sides: Amazon blocked more than 275 million suspected fake reviews in 2024, and in July 2025 won court orders transferring more than 75 fake-review broker domains to it. Both things are true at once. The platform is fighting hard, and the problem is large enough to need that much fighting.

Language red flags

Reading individual reviews, these are the patterns worth noticing:

  • Uniform praise with no acknowledged downsides. Real reviews name trade-offs, because real products have them.
  • The product name used instead of pronouns. "The X arrived quickly. The X was easy to assemble." That is a paid-per-mention artefact, or an AI one.
  • "I was sceptical, but..." An engineered credibility template. It appears far more often in paid reviews than in life.
  • Grammar that is too clean. No typos, no sentence fragments, no regional vocabulary at all.
  • Copy that mirrors the marketing bullets rather than lived use. If the review could have been written from the listing alone, it probably was.
  • Pre-emptive objection handling. "I worried about the durability, but..." means the writer read the negative reviews before writing.

Structural red flags

These are more reliable than language, because they are harder to fake at scale:

  • Burst clusters. Fifteen or more five-star reviews inside about thirty-six hours, then silence. Genuine review flow is lumpy but not that lumpy.
  • Reviewer profiles that only ever review one brand. Click through on two or three of the most enthusiastic reviewers.
  • Studio-quality photography in "customer" images. White background, professional lighting. Reverse image search it; it is frequently the press kit.
  • Review hijacking. A seller merges a new product into an old listing's variation family and inherits its reviews. The tell is reviews describing a completely different product, or a review count implausible for how long the listing has existed.
  • "Verified Purchase" is weak evidence. Brokers reimburse buyers for real purchases, which produces genuinely verified fake reviews. Treat it as a floor, not a signal.

The single best habit

If you take one thing from this article: read the three-star reviews first.

They are the least worth faking in either direction. Nobody is paid to write a lukewarm review, and no competitor bothers sabotaging a product with a middling one. Three-star reviews are where people name the actual trade-offs.

Then read the one-stars, and ask a single question: do the complaints cluster on one specific defect, or scatter across unrelated issues? Clustering means a real quality problem. Scatter is normal variance in any product with thousands of buyers.

Price history, which still works

Unlike the review checkers, price trackers survive. CamelCamelCamel is free and covers eight marketplaces. Keepa has a free tier plus a paid premium tier, covers eleven marketplaces, and adds sales-rank history.

  1. Is the deal real? Compare today's price against the twelve-month average line, never against the struck-through list price. If today sits near or above the yearly average, there is no deal.
  2. Is this the year's low? A "40% off" that lands above the ninety-day low is a worse deal than an ordinary Tuesday three months ago.
  3. Was the price raised just before the sale? A step up two to six weeks before Prime Day or Black Friday, followed by a discount back to the old price, is the classic pattern and it is plainly visible on the chart.
  4. Set an alert and walk away. For anything non-urgent this turns price psychology from a weakness into an advantage.
  5. Use the sales-rank graph as an authenticity check. A product whose rank was flat and obscure, then spiked overnight alongside a burst of reviews, is showing you a launch-manipulation signature.

There is peer-reviewed evidence that some discounts are manufactured. Research published in Marketing Science in October 2022 analysed more than 1,700 vacuum cleaners across roughly 500,000 Amazon price observations, and found sellers introducing a list price at the same moment as a price increase, manufacturing the appearance of a discount. When that happened, shoppers paid around 23% more than the previous day's price. Prevalence of the tactic ran at 28% of list-price introductions for vacuum cleaners, and 13% each for blenders, digital cameras and drones.

Understand the terminology too. The struck-through figure may be a list price, which is a manufacturer RRP and frequently fictional, or a "was" price, which is Amazon's own recent-price calculation. Neither reliably reflects what anyone actually paid recently, and the percentage-off is computed from a number the seller substantially controls.

Your UK rights, which are stronger than you think

Two things work in your favour here that Amazon's own processes do not advertise.

"Fulfilled by Amazon" is not "sold by Amazon". FBA only means Amazon warehouses and ships it; the seller can still be anyone. Only "Ships from and sold by Amazon" means Amazon is the merchant of record. This is exactly where warranty and authenticity risk lives, and most shoppers conflate the two. From 31 March 2026, Amazon ended commingled FBA inventory, so you now receive the unit from the seller you actually chose. That closes the "reputable seller, counterfeit unit" hole, though a seller who stocks fakes still ships you fakes.

Pay by credit card for anything expensive. Under Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act, for goods between £100 and £30,000, your card issuer is jointly liable with the retailer. That is a faster and stronger remedy than Amazon's goodwill, and it runs independently of it. UK and EU retailers also carry statutory conformity obligations regardless of whether the seller was an authorised dealer, which materially reduces the grey-market warranty risk that plagues US buyers.

One change worth knowing: since 8 February 2026, prepaid return labels are mandatory for nearly all seller-fulfilled orders, so the old trick of refusing return postage on expensive items is gone. Less welcome, Amazon now issues pattern-based warnings to customers whose aggregate return ratio is high, and it does not publish the threshold.

Questions people ask

Is there any replacement for Fakespot? Nothing reliable. Anything claiming to score authenticity automatically needs review data it almost certainly cannot legally obtain any more. Manual inspection is the honest answer, and it is not as slow as it sounds.

Should I just ignore reviews entirely? No. Ignore the star average, read the three-star and one-star text, and check whether complaints cluster. The average is the least informative number on the page.

Does a high review count mean a product is safe? Not on its own, particularly if the listing is young. Review hijacking specifically produces old-looking review counts on new products.

What about products with very few reviews? Genuinely new products have to start somewhere. A small number of detailed, specific, mixed reviews is a better signal than four hundred glowing ones posted in a week.

The verdict

The tools are gone and they are not coming back, which means the skill is now yours to have. Read the three-stars, look for clustered complaints, treat "frequently returned" as the one badge that means something, check the price against the twelve-month line, and pay by credit card if it is over £100. That is most of the protection available, and it costs about two minutes.
