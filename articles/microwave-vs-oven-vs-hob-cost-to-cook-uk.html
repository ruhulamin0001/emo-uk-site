Your Oven Costs About Ten Times What Your Microwave Costs To Heat The Same Plate

An oven does not heat your dinner. It heats a large insulated box, and your dinner sits in the box picking up whatever it can. Once you cost the appliances by the task rather than by the hour, several pieces of received kitchen wisdom stop being true.

The short version

  • An oven heats the box, not the food. That is the whole reason for the gap. A microwave puts energy into the food directly and heats nothing else.
  • Reheating one plate: 1.84p in a microwave, 17.50p in a fan oven. About ten times, for an identical result.
  • A £120 microwave used five times a week instead of the oven saves £40.72 a year, so it pays back in about three years and then keeps paying.
  • The oven wins as soon as you fill it. Six jacket potatoes cost 28.79p in the oven, which is 4.80p each, against 6.13p each in the microwave.

1. Why an oven is expensive, and it is not the wattage

A typical built-in fan oven is rated at about 2.1 kilowatts. A microwave rated at 900 watts of output draws about 1.4 kilowatts from the socket. On those numbers the oven looks only 50 per cent worse, and that is not remotely the real gap.

The difference is what the energy is doing. A microwave transmits energy that is absorbed by water molecules in the food. Almost all of it ends up in the dinner. An oven heats a cavity of air, plus the shelves, plus the door glass, plus the walls, and it keeps replacing the heat that leaks out of all of them for as long as it is on. Your dinner takes what it needs from that and the rest is overhead.

Then there is preheating, which is pure overhead by definition. Twelve minutes at 2.1 kilowatts before the food goes anywhere near the oven is 0.42 kWh, and at 26.32p per kWh that is 11.05p spent on an empty oven. That single figure is larger than the entire cost of most microwave tasks.

Everything below uses the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, which sets electricity at 26.32p per kWh. The oven is modelled as 2.1 kW rated, drawing full power for a 12 minute preheat and then cycling at about 35 per cent duty, so 0.735 kW on average once up to temperature. That cycling behaviour is why an oven's rated wattage overstates its running cost, and why quoting the plate rating alone is misleading in the other direction.

The third block settles an old argument. A kettle boils a litre for 3.42p, a pan on the hob for 5.26p. Boil in the kettle, then transfer. And only boil what you need, because filling a kettle to the top to make one mug is where most of that 3.42p goes.

3. The point where the oven starts winning

None of the above means the oven is a bad appliance. It means the oven is a bad appliance for one portion. The overhead is fixed, so the more food you put in it, the smaller the overhead per portion becomes.

Take the jacket potato at 28.79p for a full oven cycle. That figure barely moves whether the oven holds one potato or eight.

So the rule that actually follows from the numbers is not never use the oven. It is never run the oven half empty. If it is going on, fill it: batch the roast potatoes, put tomorrow's traybake in alongside tonight's, cook the whole packet of sausages and keep half. Every empty shelf is overhead you are paying for and getting nothing back from.

Two free habits worth more than they sound. Stop preheating for anything that does not need it, which is most casseroles, jacket potatoes and anything cooked from frozen with a long time; that saves 11.05p a go. And stop opening the door, because a fan oven loses a large share of its heat in a few seconds and then has to put it all back.

4. The slow cooker myth, costed honestly

Slow cookers are sold on the idea that a 200 watt appliance must be cheap. It is cheap per hour. It runs for a very long time.

Eight hours at 200 watts is 1.6 kWh, which is 42.11p. The same casserole given two hours in a fan oven is 0.42 kWh of preheat plus 1.47 kWh of cycling, so 1.89 kWh, which is 49.75p.

The slow cooker wins, by 7.64p. That is real and it is worth having, but it is nothing like the order-of-magnitude saving the appliance is usually marketed on. On 100 casseroles a year it is £7.64, which would take a £35 slow cooker 4.6 years to pay back on energy alone.

Buy a slow cooker because you want to leave dinner unattended all day and come home to it. That is a genuine and valuable thing. Do not buy one expecting it to change your electricity bill, because it will not.

The same discipline applies to every appliance claim in this category. Where a saving figure comes from a company that sells the appliance, treat it as marketing until you have seen the wattage and done the multiplication yourself. It takes thirty seconds and it is the whole method of this site.

5. Worked example: Bartek in Peterborough

Bartek lives alone, works shifts, and reheats a portion of something he batch-cooked at the weekend most evenings. He has no microwave and has been using the fan oven, because he had been told microwaves make food rubbery.

He counts it up: five reheats a week, so 260 a year, each one a 12 minute preheat and 20 minutes in the oven. At 0.665 kWh a time that is 172.9 kWh, or £45.50 a year, purely on reheating.

He buys a £120 900 watt microwave. The same 260 reheats at 0.070 kWh are 18.2 kWh, or £4.79.

Saving: £40.71 a year. £120 divided by £40.71 is 2.95 years. After that it is £40 a year in his pocket for as long as the machine lasts, which on a well-treated microwave is commonly eight to ten years.

He also stops preheating for the weekend batch cook, which does not need it, saving 11.05p on each of about 50 sessions a year, so another £5.53. And when the oven does go on, he fills it, which turns a £28.79 tray of six potatoes into 4.80p each rather than 28.79p for one.

Total change, about £46 a year against a one-off £120. He notes, fairly, that the reheated rice is better in the microwave and the roast potatoes are better in the oven, which is exactly how it should be. The appliance choice was never really about the food.

6. The honest verdict

If you regularly heat one portion and you do not own a microwave, buy one. At about 15.7p saved per plate against the oven it pays for itself in roughly three years on five uses a week, and it is the only appliance in this article with a payback worth writing down.

Do not buy an air fryer to save money. It beats a full-size oven and it loses to a microwave. Buy it because you prefer what it does to chips.

Do not buy a slow cooker to save money either. Eight hours at 200 watts costs 42.11p, which is only 7.64p less than two hours of oven. Buy it for the convenience, which is real.

And when the oven does go on, fill it. That costs nothing and it is worth more than any of the purchases above.

Frequently asked

Why does my oven's 2.1kW rating not mean 2.1kW all the time? Because a thermostat switches the elements off once the cavity reaches temperature and back on when it drops. Over a long bake the average draw is commonly around a third of the rated figure. Any article that multiplies the rated wattage by the full cooking time is overstating the cost by roughly three times.

Is a gas oven cheaper to run than an electric one? Per unit of heat, yes, because gas at 7.97p per kWh is roughly a third the price of electricity at 26.32p. A gas oven is less efficient at getting that heat into the food, so the gap narrows, but on running cost alone gas still wins for oven cooking.

Does a combination microwave get the best of both? It gets the convenience of both and the running cost of whichever mode you use. In microwave mode it is cheap, in convection mode it is a small oven with a small oven's overhead. Useful appliance, not a magic one.

What about the standby draw on all of these? A microwave with a clock display typically draws 1 to 3 watts continuously, which is roughly £2.30 to £6.90 a year at 26.32p. Real, small, and worth knowing when a device is left on for the other 23 hours and 50 minutes of the day.
