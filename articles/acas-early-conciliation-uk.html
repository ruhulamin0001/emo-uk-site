The Step You Cannot Skip Costs Nothing. The One That Helps Most Is Not Free

You cannot bring most employment tribunal claims without notifying Acas first, and it costs nothing. What it will not do is tell you whether your case is any good. Here is the difference between notifying and conciliating, the four routes compared, and the deadline that changes on 1 October.

The short version

  • Notifying Acas is compulsory before most tribunal claims. Conciliating is not. Acas is clear that early conciliation itself is voluntary and either side can decline, but you cannot lodge a claim without notifying first.
  • It is free. There is no fee to notify Acas and no fee to take part.
  • The limitation period extends from three months to six on 1 October 2026, under the Employment Rights Act 2025. That is twelve days away and it changes the planning, not the obligation.
  • Acas will not tell you whether you have a case. It cannot represent you, take sides, prepare your case or give legal advice. Knowing that in advance prevents a lot of disappointment.
  • Skipping the informal steps first can cost you money. A tribunal judge usually expects to see that you tried to resolve things, and compensation can be reduced if you did not.

1. What early conciliation is, precisely

Early conciliation is a legal process run by Acas. If you want to make a claim to an employment tribunal, you must notify Acas first. Acas will then offer early conciliation, in which a conciliator tries to help you and your employer reach a legal agreement and avoid a hearing.

The distinction that trips people up is this. Notification is compulsory. Conciliation is voluntary. Acas states directly that early conciliation is voluntary and that both you and your employer can choose whether to take part. If either side declines, Acas issues the certificate anyway and you are free to lodge your claim.

So the compulsory part is a form, not a negotiation. Nobody can force you into a settlement you do not want, and nobody can block your claim by refusing to talk.

2. What Acas cannot do for you

This list is short, official, and worth reading before you pick up the phone. During early conciliation Acas cannot:

  • represent you, because it is not a trade union
  • take your side or your employer’s side
  • help you prepare your case for tribunal
  • tell you whether you have a valid case
  • give you legal advice

People frequently call expecting the second-to-last one. A conciliator who will not tell you whether you are likely to win is not being unhelpful. Neutrality is the entire basis on which the employer is willing to talk at all.

Acas is the neutral channel between two sides, not the adviser to one of them. That is what makes it work, and it is the single most common misunderstanding about the service.

3. What to do before you notify

Acas is explicit that early conciliation should not be your first step, and there is money riding on that.

Before notifying, you should try to raise the problem informally, then raise a formal grievance, and appeal if a disciplinary or grievance outcome went against you. At a tribunal, the judge will usually expect you to show that you tried to resolve the dispute yourself, and if you did not it can affect how much compensation you get even on a successful claim.

One important warning that sits alongside this. Raising the problem with your employer first does not change the time limits for making a tribunal claim. An internal grievance process can run for months, and the clock does not politely wait for it. This is where good claims die.

Read the last two columns together. The first two routes are free and expected, but they do nothing for your deadline. The third is free, pauses the clock, and is the one you literally cannot skip. The fifth is the only one that will tell you whether your case is any good, and it is the only one that costs money.

That is the honest shape of the decision. Use the free steps for what they are good at, and be clear that none of them replaces advice on the merits.

5. The date that matters: 1 October 2026

Under the Employment Rights Act 2025, the time limit for bringing most employment tribunal claims extends from three months to six months on 1 October 2026.

Three things follow, and the third is the one people get wrong.

  1. It buys planning time, not complacency. Six months is still a deadline, and evidence, witnesses and memories all decay.
  2. It makes the grievance timing problem less brutal. A three month limit against a grievance and appeal process that takes two months was an uncomfortably tight fit. Six months is workable.
  3. It does not change the requirement to notify Acas. The compulsory step is unaffected by the length of the period it sits inside.

If your issue arose close to the changeover, do not guess which limit applies to you. Get advice, and notify Acas early rather than late.

Worked example: Priya in Stevenage

Priya is dismissed in a restructure she believes was a sham. She wants to challenge it.

What she does first. She appeals internally in writing, which takes five weeks and is rejected. That is the step a judge will expect to see, and it costs nothing.

The mistake she nearly makes. She assumes the appeal paused her deadline. It does not. Acas is explicit that raising the problem with your employer does not change the tribunal time limits, and those five weeks came out of her window.

What she does next. She notifies Acas. This is compulsory and free, and it pauses the limitation clock while conciliation runs.

What Acas does and does not do. The conciliator passes offers between the two sides. When Priya asks whether her case is strong, the conciliator will not say, because Acas cannot advise on the merits. She books a free half hour with a solicitor for that, separately.

The outcome. The employer offers a settlement in week four. She weighs it against a hearing that would take many months, with no guarantee, and accepts. The agreement is legally binding, which is what makes conciliation different from an informal promise.

What it cost her. Nothing for Acas. The only money she spent was on half an hour of advice on whether the offer was fair, which is the one thing the free service was never going to tell her.

6. Frequently asked

Do I have to accept what is offered? No. Conciliation is voluntary and no settlement happens without your agreement. If you decline, Acas issues a certificate and you can proceed to tribunal.

What is the certificate for? It is the proof that you notified Acas, and the tribunal will not accept most claims without the reference number from it.

Can my employer start early conciliation? Yes. Acas has a separate route for employers who want to request it.

Is a settlement final? A settlement reached through Acas is a legally binding agreement, and it will normally prevent you bringing the same claim later. Understand what you are giving up before signing.

Do I need a solicitor? Not to notify or to conciliate. You may well want one to tell you whether an offer is fair, because that is the question Acas cannot answer.

7. The honest verdict

Notify Acas, because you cannot bring most tribunal claims without doing so, and it is free. Do it earlier than feels necessary, because an internal grievance does not pause your deadline even though it feels like it should.

Take part in conciliation with realistic expectations. It can produce a binding settlement in weeks rather than a hearing in many months, and that has real value. It will not tell you whether your case is good.

Spend money on one thing only: an hour of proper advice on the merits and on whether an offer is fair. Everything else in this process is free, and paying for the free parts is the mistake to avoid.
