Open a Cupboard and Pull the Door Towards You. That Is the Whole Decision

A respray costs a fifth of a new kitchen and looks just as good for about eight years, provided the boxes behind the doors are sound. If they are not, you are painting something that is on its way to the skip.

The short version

  • Respray 900 to 2,500 pounds for a typical 12 to 15 unit kitchen, done in place over three to five days.
  • New doors and drawer fronts only, 1,400 to 3,500 pounds, and this middle option is the one most people never get quoted.
  • A full new kitchen 6,000 to 12,000 pounds fitted, more if the plumbing or electrics move.
  • Priced per year of use: respray about 200 pounds a year, new doors about 220, a new kitchen about 500. The respray is the cheapest per year by a wide margin.
  • The test is the carcass, not the door. Pull a door towards you at the top corner. If the hinge plate moves in the chipboard, or the sink base is swollen, no amount of paint fixes that.

1. The one test, before you get any quote

Everybody starts this decision by looking at the doors, because the doors are what you see and what you have got tired of. The doors are almost never the problem.

Do this instead. Open a wall cupboard, take hold of the door at the top outer corner and pull it gently towards you while watching where the hinge plate is screwed into the side of the box. If the plate stays put, the carcass is sound. If the screws move in the chipboard, or the whole side panel flexes, that unit is failing and paint will not stop it.

Then open the sink base and look at the underside of the front rail and the floor of the unit. Swollen, dark, slightly soft chipboard means water has been getting in for years, and that unit at least has to be replaced whatever else you decide.

Finally, pull out the two drawers you use most and look at the runners. Bent runners and drawers that need lifting to close are a sign the units have been carrying more weight than they were built for.

A kitchen where the boxes pass all three tests is an excellent candidate for a respray. A kitchen where two or more fail is a kitchen that needs replacing, and spending 2,000 pounds on making it look new is throwing that money away twice: once now, and again in three years.

2. Three options, not two

The industry presents this as respray or replace. There is a third answer and it is often the right one.

Respray in place. Doors, drawer fronts and sometimes the end panels are cleaned, keyed, primed and sprayed, usually with a two pack polyurethane. Done on site in a tented area or taken away to a workshop. Three to five days. Any colour you like. The carcasses and the layout stay exactly as they are.

New doors and fronts onto the existing carcasses. Most kitchen boxes are made to a standard set of sizes, so replacement doors are widely available. You get a factory finish rather than a sprayed one, new hinges and new handles, and you can change the door style entirely. It costs more than a respray and less than a third of a new kitchen.

A full new kitchen. The only option that lets you change the layout, move the sink, add a dishwasher where there was not one, or get rid of the dead corner. If your complaint is about how the kitchen works rather than how it looks, this is the only answer that addresses it, and the other two are a delay.

That last point is worth sitting with. If you keep saying there is nowhere to put anything, a respray will not fix that, and you will be back here in four years having spent the money twice.

The gap between a respray and new doors is smaller than most people expect once you divide by the years. New doors last longer because a factory applied foil or lacquer is harder than anything applied on site.

4. What a proper respray involves, and what a bad one looks like

The difference between a respray that still looks good in year six and one that chips along the leading edge by Christmas is almost entirely preparation.

A proper job degreases everything twice, because kitchen doors carry an invisible film of cooking oil that nothing will stick to. It then abrades the whole surface, not just the flat, so the mouldings and edges are keyed too. It uses an adhesion primer suited to the substrate, which is a different product for a melamine foil door than for solid timber. Then two or three coats of a two pack finish, sprayed, with proper masking and extraction.

Signs of a job you should not pay for: a quote that does not mention primer, a single day on site for a full kitchen, spraying with the doors still hanging, and no dust extraction. Overspray on the hinges is the tell that nothing came off.

Two pack coatings contain isocyanates, which is why HSE has guidance on them. This is a genuine reason to use a professional with the right mask and extraction rather than doing it in the kitchen with the window open.

Ask three questions of any sprayer. What primer are you using and why that one. Are the doors coming off. What is the guarantee and does it cover chipping as well as peeling. A firm that answers all three without hesitating is the firm to use.

5. Worked example: the Nowak kitchen in Peterborough

Marta Nowak has a 2009 kitchen in a Peterborough semi. Oak effect doors she has disliked since she moved in, seventeen units, and a layout that works fine.

She does the three tests. Every hinge plate is solid. The sink base has a small stain but the chipboard is hard. Two drawer runners are tired. That is a kitchen that passes.

Her quotes: 1,850 pounds to respray in a matt off white, or 2,900 pounds for new shaker doors and fronts, or 8,400 pounds for a new kitchen in the same layout from a mid range supplier.

Per year of use, that is 231 pounds for the respray over eight years, 242 pounds for the doors over twelve, and 467 pounds for the new kitchen over eighteen. The new kitchen is twice the annual cost and delivers nothing she said she wanted, because the layout is not changing.

She takes the respray, and spends 90 pounds of the difference on two new drawer runners and 140 pounds on new handles. Total 2,080 pounds against 8,400.

The detail that made it work: she asked for the doors to be taken away and sprayed in a workshop rather than in the house. It added two days without doors and about 200 pounds, and it is the single thing that most affects how the edges look in year five.

Frequently asked

Can you spray the worktop too? You can, and it will not last. Worktops take knives, hot pans and standing water, and no sprayed coating survives that treatment for long. If the worktop is the thing you hate, replace the worktop and leave the doors alone. It is usually 600 to 2,000 pounds and it changes the look of a kitchen more than the doors do.

Will a respray put buyers off? No, because a good one is not identifiable as a respray. A bad one is obvious at ten paces and will cost you more than it saved. This is a job where the cheapest quote is a false economy.

Does any of this need building control? Not the respray or the doors. A full kitchen refit brings in Approved Document P the moment a circuit is altered or a socket added, and it brings in ventilation requirements if the extraction changes. Your fitter should handle it and give you the certificate.

How long until I can use it? A two pack finish is touch dry in hours but takes about a week to reach full hardness. Do not scrub it, do not hang tea towels on the door, and be careful with fingernails for the first seven days. Most early damage happens in that window.

The honest verdict

If the carcasses pass the three tests and your only complaint is the colour, respray. At around 200 pounds a year it is the cheapest sensible option on this page and it looks as good as a new kitchen from more than a metre away.

If your complaint is the layout, do not respray. Buy the new kitchen, because nothing else answers the question you are actually asking, and a respray will just delay the spend by four years.
