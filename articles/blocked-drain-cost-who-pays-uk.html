Your Water Company Will Clear That Blockage For Nothing. Most People Pay 150 Pounds Instead

There is a line running through your garden, and which side of it the blockage sits on decides whether you pay nothing or you pay a drainage firm. Almost nobody knows where the line is, which is why the first phone call is usually the expensive one.

The short version

  • Since 1 October 2011, most private sewers and lateral drains in England and Wales transferred to the water companies under the Water Industry (Schemes for Adoption of Private Sewers) Regulations 2011. If the blockage is on one of those, your water company clears it and you pay nothing.
  • The rule of thumb is the boundary. Pipe inside your property that serves only your property is yours. Pipe beyond the boundary, or shared with a neighbour, is almost always theirs.
  • A shared pipe is the one people get wrong. If your drain joins a neighbour's before it leaves the land, it is usually a public sewer already, even though it is still under your lawn.
  • A private call out runs roughly 80 to 150 pounds in hours, and commonly 150 to 250 pounds out of hours, for work the water company might have done free.
  • The payback on drain cover at about 60 pounds a year is 2.5 years per blockage, so it only wins if you genuinely block up more often than once every two and a half years. Most homes do not.
  • A set of drain rods is the cheapest insurance in the house and clears a high proportion of domestic blockages in twenty minutes.

1. The line that decides who pays

Waste leaves your house through a pipe. For the first stretch that pipe serves only your home and sits inside your boundary, and while it does, it is yours. At some point it either crosses the boundary or joins a pipe carrying somebody else's waste. From that point on, in England and Wales, it is almost certainly the water company's problem.

That is the entire rule, and it explains the single most common way British households waste money on drains. The sink backs up, the manhole in the garden is full, and people ring a drainage firm because the water is visibly on their land. Whose land the water is standing on is irrelevant. What matters is which pipe it is standing in.

2. What changed in October 2011

On 1 October 2011, under the Water Industry (Schemes for Adoption of Private Sewers) Regulations 2011, the ownership of most private sewers and lateral drains connected to the public network in England and Wales transferred to the regional water and sewerage companies. Overnight, hundreds of thousands of pipes that had been the homeowner's liability became the water company's.

The practical consequences, fifteen years on, are three:

  • Your water company must investigate and clear blockages on the pipes it now owns, and does not charge you for doing so.
  • It also owns the repair liability. A collapsed section of transferred pipe is their excavation and their bill, not yours.
  • Newer estates may not be covered. Drainage built after the transfer, or private systems never connected to a public sewer, such as a septic tank, stayed with the property owner. If you live on a development built in the last decade and the roads are still unadopted, check before you assume.

Every water company publishes a diagram of exactly where its responsibility starts on your street. It is worth two minutes on their site before it is worth two hundred pounds on a call out.

Which side of the boundary the water is standing on does not matter. Which pipe it is standing in does.

4. Worked example: the Okonkwo household in Stevenage

A 1970s semi. On a Saturday morning the downstairs toilet will not clear and the inspection chamber by the side gate is full to the rim.

What most people do: ring the first drainage firm on the search results. Saturday rate, 195 pounds, machine on site in three hours, cleared in forty minutes. Cost: 195 pounds.

What the Okonkwos did: lifted the second chamber nearer the road and found it running clear. Water backing up in the first chamber but not the second means the blockage is between them, and that run also carries the attached neighbour's waste. They rang the water company's emergency line. Engineer on site the same afternoon. Cost: nothing.

The two chamber check took about six minutes and was worth 195 pounds, or roughly 1,950 pounds an hour, which is the best paid work either of them did that year.

If both chambers had been full, the blockage would have been further down the line and still the water company's. If the first chamber had been full and the pipe served only their house, it would have been theirs, and a 35 pound set of drain rods would very probably have sorted it.

5. Is drain cover worth 60 pounds a year

Home emergency add ons and standalone drainage cover typically sit somewhere around 50 to 80 pounds a year, often bundled into a boiler or home emergency policy. Call it 60.

The payback sum: one in hours private call out at 150 pounds, divided by 60 pounds a year of cover, is 2.5 years. The cover only pays for itself if you have a chargeable blockage more often than once every two and a half years.

The catch that makes it worse: most policies exclude the pipes the water company already owns, because those are free anyway, and many exclude blockages caused by wipes, fat or root ingress, which between them account for a large share of real world blockages.

So the honest answer for most households is no. You are insuring against an event that is usually free, using a policy that frequently excludes the common causes. The exception is a house with a known history: a shallow run under a mature tree, or a drain that has blocked twice in three years already. If you are in that group the arithmetic flips, and it flips hard.

If your drainage is private rather than mains connected, none of this applies and the costs are different again, which we set out in septic tank rules and emptying costs. Frequently asked

How do I know whether my pipe was transferred in 2011? Ring your water company and give them your postcode, or use the responsibility diagram on their website. They hold the record. Do not rely on a drainage firm's opinion on this point, because they are the party that gets paid if the answer is you.

Does this apply in Scotland and Northern Ireland? No. The 2011 transfer covered England and Wales. Scottish Water and NI Water operate under different arrangements, though in practice both also take responsibility for the public network. Check directly rather than assuming the English position carries across.

The water company came out and said it was my side. Now what? You pay for that clearance, but you have gained something useful: a free diagnosis from the party with no incentive to find work. Ask them where exactly the blockage sits, then get a private quote for that specific run rather than for an open ended investigation.

What if the blockage is caused by a neighbour? If the pipe is shared it is the water company's to clear regardless of who caused it, which is one of the quieter benefits of the transfer. Recovering the cost from a neighbour is a separate and usually unrewarding conversation.

The honest verdict

Before you ring anybody, lift two chambers and find out which one is full. Then ring your water company first, every time, because their engineer is free and a drainage firm's is not.

Do not buy drain cover unless your drain has actually blocked twice in the last three years. At 60 pounds a year against a 150 pound call out, it needs a blockage every two and a half years just to break even, and it very likely excludes the pipe that will actually block.

Buy the rods. They cost about a fifth of one call out and they will outlive the house.
