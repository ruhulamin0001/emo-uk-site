The Disclosure Phrase On Half The UK Money Blogs Has Already Been Ruled Against

"We may earn a small commission" is the most common affiliate disclosure in Britain. The Advertising Standards Authority ruled it ambiguous and insufficient in December 2022, and since 6 April 2025 the same conduct has been directly enforceable by the Competition and Markets Authority with fines of up to 10% of global turnover. Here is what the rules actually say.

The short version

  • There are two rulebooks, not one. The CAP Code is enforced by the ASA, which cannot fine you. The DMCC Act 2024 is enforced by the CMA, which since 6 April 2025 can fine up to 10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher.
  • Schedule 20 paragraph 12 of the DMCC Act bans using editorial content to promote a product where the trader has paid for it "without making that clear in the content". Paragraph 25 bans falsely representing yourself as a consumer. Both came into force on 6 April 2025.
  • "May earn an affiliate commission" has been ruled insufficient. The ASA said it was ambiguous because it suggested payment might not happen when, barring administrative error, it would (MailOnline, 21 December 2022).
  • A disclaimer at the bottom of the post is unlikely to be enough. CAP guidance is explicit: the label must be obvious at the point the reader meets the link, not after it.
  • The label has to be a word people understand. "Affiliate" on its own was rejected in 2020, "#collab" in 2021, and "a d/affiliate" in 2022. "Ad" is the safe word.
  • Both you and the brand are liable, even if the brand never saw your content.

1. Two rulebooks, and only one of them can fine you

Almost every guide to UK affiliate disclosure talks about the ASA and stops. That is the half without teeth.

The CAP Code is the UK advertising rulebook, enforced by the Advertising Standards Authority. Rule 2.1 requires marketing communications to be obviously identifiable as such. Rule 2.3 requires them to make their commercial intent clear if that is not apparent from the context, and prohibits falsely implying that the marketer is acting as a consumer. CAP's own guidance confirms affiliate marketing falls inside the Code because affiliate references are "directly connected with the supply or transfer of goods, services, opportunities and gifts". The ASA can name you in a published ruling, ask platforms to remove your content, and refer you onward. It cannot fine you.

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 is law, and it can. Its consumer protection provisions came into force on 6 April 2025 under SI 2025/272. Two entries in Schedule 20, the list of practices banned outright, matter here, and they are worth reading in the Act's own words.

Paragraph 12 bans:

"Using editorial content in the media to promote a product where a trader has paid for the promotion without making that clear in the content or by images or sounds clearly identifiable by the consumer."

Paragraph 25 bans:

"Falsely claiming or creating the false impression that the trader is not acting for purposes relating to the trader's business or falsely representing oneself as a consumer."

Neither paragraph mentions the word affiliate. Both describe exactly what an unlabelled affiliate review is. And from 6 April 2025 the Competition and Markets Authority can investigate and enforce consumer law directly, without going to court first, with penalties of up to 10% of global annual turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher, for a substantive breach.

To be plain about proportion: the CMA is not going to open a case against a hobby blog. But the standard the law sets does not scale with your traffic, and "the CMA has bigger fish" is a risk position, not a compliance position.

2. The exact phrases the ASA has already rejected

This is the part that saves you guesswork. The ASA has published rulings on specific wordings, so copying a phrase that has already failed is the easiest mistake to avoid.

"May earn an affiliate commission" was rejected. In a ruling against Associated Newspapers, publisher of MailOnline, dated 21 December 2022, several articles that focused wholly on affiliate linked products were judged to be advertising in their entirety. The articles carried a disclaimer at the top, and the ASA still found it insufficient. Its reasoning was that the phrase "may earn an affiliate commission" was ambiguous and confusing, because it suggested the publisher might not receive payment when, barring errors in the administration of the affiliate arrangement, it would.

Read that again if you run a UK money or reviews site, because the phrase "we may earn a commission" is close to standard boilerplate across the British blogging world, and it has been publicly ruled ambiguous by the regulator.

"Affiliate" on its own was rejected. In a ruling against Asos.com dated 22 April 2020, an Instagram Story labelled "*affiliate" was found not recognisable as an ad. Placement was part of it, but the ASA also said the term "affiliate" itself was not widely understood by consumers, citing its own labelling research.

"#collab" was rejected in a ruling against Charlotte Tilbury Beauty dated 13 October 2021. "a d/affiliate" was rejected in a ruling involving J Choo Ltd dated 30 November 2022, partly because of the space inserted between the letters of "ad".

A generic site banner was rejected. In a ruling against DSG Retail and Reach plc dated 17 April 2019, a Liverpool Echo Black Friday article carried a banner above the headline reading "IN ASSOCIATION WITH REACH SOLUTIONS". The ASA considered readers would treat that as website furniture rather than a statement about that story.

The pattern is consistent. The regulator is not testing whether you technically told the truth somewhere. It is testing whether an ordinary reader, at the moment they meet the link, understands that it is advertising.

3. Where the label has to sit, format by format

CAP's guidance turns on one question: is the whole page advertising, or only part of it?

If the whole article is about affiliate linked products, for example a "best budget headphones" roundup where every product carries your link, the commercial nature has to be clear before the reader clicks through. That means an identifier such as "Ad" in the title, visible in the search result, not only once the page has loaded.

If only some links are affiliate links, a title label is unlikely to be needed. What is needed is that the specific links and the content around them are identifiable. CAP suggests two routes: "(Ad)" immediately before the relevant part, or a clear statement at the beginning that asterisks used in the text indicate advertising and that you receive a share of sales through those links.

A disclaimer at the foot of the post is specifically flagged as unlikely to be sufficient, because the reader meets the link before they meet the disclaimer.

On short-form social the rules tighten. On X, "#Ad" at the start is the clearest option. On Pinterest, "Ad" needs to be at the start of the description, because only a fraction of it shows without a click. In video, use on-screen text or say it before you talk about the product.

One more thing, because it surprises people. The ASA has treated posts as affiliate ads even where no affiliate link appeared in the post itself, on the basis that the creator had a commercial interest in driving clicks to a page where the code lived. It has also treated a post as an ad after the agreement had ended and no commission was actually paid.

4. Worked example: fixing one page properly

Worked example: Imran in Watford

Imran runs a small UK gadget review site. One page, a roundup of eight budget power banks, carries an Amazon link on every product. At the very bottom of the page, above the footer, sits the line: "This post contains affiliate links. We may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you."

That single sentence contains three separate problems.

One, position. It is at the bottom. A reader who clicks the second product has already left the page without ever seeing it. CAP's guidance says a disclaimer of this nature at the foot of a post is unlikely to be sufficient.

Two, the word "may". This is the exact construction the ASA called ambiguous in the MailOnline ruling of 21 December 2022. Imran will be paid on qualifying sales. There is no "may" about it.

Three, the whole page is advertising. Every product is affiliate linked, so under CAP's guidance the commercial nature needs to be clear before the click, which means the title, not the footer.

The fix costs him about ten minutes. The page title becomes "(Ad) Eight Budget Power Banks Worth Buying". Directly under the H1, before any product, he adds: "Ad. Every product below is linked through Amazon Associates and we earn a commission on qualifying purchases. It does not change what you pay."

Nothing about his rankings, his revenue or his product choices changes. What changes is that the page now says what it is at the point the reader needs to know it.

5. What to do this week if your site is not compliant

Most UK affiliate sites can be brought in line in an afternoon.

  1. Search your site for the word "may". Every instance of "may earn", "may receive" or "may be compensated" needs rewriting to a plain statement of fact: we earn a commission.
  2. Sort your pages into two piles. Wholly commercial roundups in one, articles with a couple of incidental links in the other. They need different treatment.
  3. Move the disclosure above the first link on every page. This is the single highest-value change and it is usually a template edit rather than a per-page one.
  4. Put "Ad" in the title of wholly commercial pages, and check it survives into your meta title, because that is what people see before they click.
  5. Use the word "Ad". Not "affiliate", not "collab", not "partner", not a spaced-out variant. Three separate rulings have gone against creative alternatives.
  6. Check your social posts, including old ones. Deleting or relabelling is easier than arguing.

There is nothing to buy here, no plugin that solves it and no service worth paying for. The requirement is a sentence in the right place using the right word.

6. The honest verdict

UK affiliate disclosure is not vague, and it is not a matter of taste. The ASA has published rulings naming the exact phrases that fail, and since 6 April 2025 the underlying conduct sits inside a statute the CMA can enforce directly with turnover-based fines.

The practical standard is one sentence long: an ordinary reader should know it is advertising at the moment they meet the link, in a word they actually understand. "Ad", above the first link, on every page that carries one.

If you take one thing from this, take the MailOnline ruling. The most popular disclosure sentence in British blogging, the one with "may" in it, has already been examined by the regulator and found wanting. Changing it costs nothing.

Frequently asked

Does the ASA actually go after small blogs? Rarely, and its published rulings are dominated by large publishers and well-followed influencers. But ASA rulings are public and name the party, and the CMA's powers under the DMCC Act do not have a small-site exemption. The realistic risk for a small site is reputational and platform-level, for example an affiliate programme closing your account for non-compliance, rather than a regulator's fine.

Is "#ad" enough on its own? On short-form social with a character limit, CAP indicates that labelling with "#Ad" or similar at the start is likely to be the clearest approach. On a blog post it is the identifier, not the whole job: it also has to be positioned so the reader sees it before the link.

What if the brand never approved my content? It makes no difference to your liability. CAP guidance is explicit that both the business and the affiliate marketer are responsible under the Code, even where the ad was created solely by the affiliate with no input from the brand.

Do I need a separate affiliate disclosure page? It is good practice and most programmes require something like it in their terms, but it does not substitute for labelling at the point of the link. CAP's guidance on voucher sites says explicitly that marketers should not rely only on explanations buried in "About Us", "FAQs" or terms and conditions pages.
