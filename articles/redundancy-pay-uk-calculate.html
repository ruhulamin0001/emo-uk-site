Twenty-Four Years Of Service, Twenty-Five Weeks Of Pay: How The Redundancy Caps Bite

Statutory redundancy pay looks generous until you meet the two caps. Your weekly pay counts only up to £751, and only your last 20 years of service count at all. Put together they create a hard ceiling of £22,530, and the people who hit it are precisely the ones who gave the most years.

The short version

  • You need 2 years' continuous service as an employee to get statutory redundancy pay at all.
  • The rate is age-banded: half a week's pay for each full year you were under 22, one week for each year you were 22 to 40, and one and a half weeks for each year you were 41 or older.
  • Cap one: your weekly pay counts only up to £751 if you were made redundant on or after 6 April 2026.
  • Cap two: length of service is capped at 20 years. Year 21 onwards counts for nothing.
  • Maximum statutory redundancy pay is £22,530. That is 20 years times 1.5 weeks times £751, which is our own check of the government's stated figure.
  • You have 6 months from the day your job ends to claim it. Miss that and you are relying on a tribunal's discretion.
  • Statutory pay is a floor, not a norm. Many contracts and settlement agreements pay more, and the first £30,000 of a genuine redundancy payment is generally free of Income Tax.

1. Who qualifies, and who does not

You are normally entitled to statutory redundancy pay if you are an employee and you have worked for your current employer for two years or more. Both parts matter. Workers and the genuinely self-employed are outside it entirely, and employment status turns on the reality of the arrangement rather than what the contract calls you.

There are situations where you get nothing despite qualifying on service:

  • your employer offers to keep you on
  • your employer offers you suitable alternative work and you refuse it without good reason
  • you are dismissed for misconduct, which is not redundancy

The second one is where most disputes happen, because "suitable" and "without good reason" are both judgement calls. A role at the same pay in a different town is not automatically suitable. Take advice before refusing anything.

Some categories are excluded outright: former registered dock workers and share fishermen, crown servants, members of the armed forces or police services, apprentices who are not employees at the end of their training, and a domestic servant who is a member of the employer's immediate family.

2. The formula, and the two caps

The calculation has three inputs: your age during each year of service, your weekly pay, and your length of service. Then two caps land on top.

The age bands. For each full year of service you get:

Your age during that yearWhat you get for that year
Under 22half a week's pay
22 to 40one week's pay
41 or olderone and a half weeks' pay

Your weekly pay is the average you earned per week over the 12 weeks before the day you got your redundancy notice. Not your contractual salary divided by 52, and not this month. Twelve weeks before notice. If those twelve weeks contained unpaid leave or reduced hours, that matters, and if you were paid less because you were on furlough during the pandemic, the calculation is based on what you would normally have earned.

Now the two caps, and they are the reason this article exists.

If you were made redundant on or after 6 April 2026, your weekly pay is capped at £751 and the maximum statutory redundancy pay you can get is £22,530.

Cap one, the weekly pay cap of £751. Earn £600 a week and your actual pay is used. Earn £1,500 a week and the calculation still uses £751. Everything above that line is invisible to the formula.

Cap two, the 20 year service cap. Length of service is capped at 20 years. Twenty-five years of service produces exactly the same statutory entitlement as twenty.

Multiply the two caps together at the most generous age band and you get the ceiling: 20 years, times 1.5 weeks, times £751, equals £22,530. That is our own arithmetic and it reproduces the government's published maximum exactly, which is a useful confirmation that the ceiling really is just the two caps meeting.

The uncomfortable implication is worth stating plainly. The caps do not bite on short-serving low earners at all. They bite hardest on people with long service and above-average pay, which is to say the people who most expect a large payment.

3. Worked example: what the caps cost one person

Worked example: Priya in Coventry

Priya joined her employer at 38 and is made redundant at 62. That is 24 years of continuous service. Her average weekly pay over the 12 weeks before notice is £900.

Step one, the service cap. Only 20 years count. Because service is capped by taking the most recent years, the 20 that count are the years she was aged 42 to 62.

Step two, the age band. Every one of those 20 years was a year in which she was 41 or older, so each is worth one and a half weeks' pay.

Step three, the weekly pay cap. Her £900 is above the £751 cap, so the calculation uses £751.

The result: 20 x 1.5 x £751 = £22,530. She receives the statutory maximum.

What the caps cost her. Without either cap, 24 years at 1.5 weeks of her real £900 pay would be £32,400. The two caps together remove £9,870, which is about 30% of the uncapped figure.

The number worth sitting with. £22,530 at her actual pay of £900 a week is 25 weeks of take-home-equivalent pay, for 24 years of service. Roughly one week of pay per year worked, once both caps have done their work.

None of this is an error or an injustice being hidden. It is the scheme operating exactly as designed. But it is very far from the impression most people have of what "a week and a half for every year" is going to produce after two decades.

4. When you get more than the statutory minimum

Statutory redundancy pay is a legal floor. A great many people receive more, and the gap between the floor and what you actually get is usually where the real money is.

Contractual redundancy pay. Check your contract, your staff handbook and any collective agreement. Enhanced schemes are common in large employers, the public sector and unionised workplaces, and they often ignore one or both statutory caps. This is the first document to find, before you do any arithmetic at all.

Settlement agreements. Where an employer wants a clean break, they may offer a payment above the statutory amount in exchange for you waiving claims. These are legally binding only if you take independent legal advice, and the employer normally contributes to that cost. Do not sign one on the day it is handed to you.

Notice pay is separate. Statutory redundancy pay is not your notice period. You are entitled to your notice, or pay in lieu of it, on top.

Holiday pay is separate too. Accrued and untaken holiday is paid out on termination and has nothing to do with the redundancy calculation.

The tax position is genuinely favourable. The first £30,000 of a genuine redundancy payment is generally free of Income Tax and National Insurance. Note the word genuine: pay in lieu of notice and holiday pay are taxed as normal earnings, so a headline "package" figure is not all tax free. GOV.UK has a dedicated page on the tax treatment and it is worth reading before you agree a number, because how a payment is labelled changes what you keep.

5. Claiming, deadlines and lay-offs

You have six months from the date your job ends to apply for statutory redundancy pay. That is a real deadline, not a guideline. Most people never think about it because the employer simply pays, but if your employer disputes it or drags its feet, the clock is running on you.

There is also a route most people have never heard of, for people who are still technically employed but not being given work.

You can claim statutory redundancy pay if you have been temporarily laid off, meaning without pay or on less than half a week's pay, for either:

  • more than 4 weeks in a row, or
  • more than 6 non-consecutive weeks in a 13 week period

The procedure is precise. Write to your employer saying you intend to claim statutory redundancy pay, within 4 weeks of your last non-working day in that 4 or 6 week period. If they do not reject your claim within 7 days of receiving it, write again giving your notice. Your claim can be rejected if your normal work is likely to start within 4 weeks and continue for at least 13 weeks.

That is a lot of specific dates, and they are easy to miss while you are hoping work picks up. If you are being laid off repeatedly, diarise them.

6. The honest verdict

Work out your number before any conversation about a package, because you cannot judge an offer without knowing the floor. The formula is short: age band per year, times weekly pay capped at £751, times service capped at 20 years, with a hard ceiling of £22,530.

Then go and find your contract, because the statutory figure is frequently the least you will receive rather than the most, and an enhanced scheme is the single thing most likely to change your number materially.

The honest summary of the caps is this. If you have short service or modest pay, they will not affect you and the scheme does roughly what you would expect. If you have twenty-plus years and above-average pay, the scheme will pay you a great deal less than the headline formula implies, and the shortfall is not negotiable with your employer because it is written into the statute. Knowing that before you walk into the meeting is worth more than any negotiating tactic.

Frequently asked

Is the GBP 22,530 maximum before or after tax? It is the gross statutory entitlement. In practice a genuine redundancy payment is generally free of Income Tax and National Insurance up to GBP 30,000, so a statutory-only payment normally arrives untaxed. Pay in lieu of notice and holiday pay are taxed as earnings and sit outside that GBP 30,000 shelter.

Which 20 years count if I served longer? The cap works on your most recent 20 years of continuous service. For anyone with long service that is usually the better outcome, because those later years are more likely to fall in the 41-or-older band worth one and a half weeks each.

What if my employer cannot afford to pay? If your employer is insolvent you can apply to the Redundancy Payments Service for statutory redundancy pay, notice pay, holiday pay and certain arrears from the National Insurance Fund. GOV.UK runs a redundancy payments helpline for this.

Does part-time work count for less? Not in years, only in pay. A part-time year is a full year of service. What is lower is your weekly pay figure, since it is your actual average weekly earnings over the 12 weeks before notice.
