Replacing A Working Boiler Pays Back In Seven Years At Best And Thirty-Nine At Worst. Here Is How To Find Your Number.

A boiler is the most expensive thing in most homes that is bought in a panic, usually in January, usually after the old one has stopped. The Energy Saving Trust publishes a cost and a saving for every combination of house type and old boiler rating, and turning those into payback years shows which households should plan a replacement and which should keep repairing. The difference is not small: seven years in a detached house with a G-rated boiler, twenty-one in a flat even from a G-rated one, and thirty-nine in a flat from a D-rated one.

The short version

  • The Energy Saving Trust's average cost to replace a gas boiler with an A-rated one and thermostatic radiator valves is £3,500 in Great Britain, on its page updated 20 May 2026.
  • The saving depends on two things: your house type and the efficiency letter of the boiler you have now. In a semi-detached house it runs from £190 a year (replacing a D-rated boiler) to £350 (replacing a G-rated one), at July 2026 fuel prices.
  • Payback in a semi is therefore 10 years from a G-rated boiler and 18 years from a D-rated one. A detached house does better, 7 to 16 years; a flat does worst, 21 to 39 years.
  • If your boiler was installed after 2005 it is almost certainly a condensing boiler and probably rated A or B. Replacing it early saves very little; the Energy Saving Trust does not even publish a figure for A, B or C boilers because the saving is too small.
  • Repair beats replace while the repair bill is under about one year's saving. A £300 fan or pump on a G-rated boiler in a semi is a good spend; a £900 heat exchanger on a fifteen-year-old one is not.
  • Plan it, do not panic-buy it. A boiler bought in a January emergency is priced as an emergency. The arithmetic above says most households have years to get three quotes.

1. The cost and saving figures, and where they come from

The Energy Saving Trust is the only body that publishes a cost and a saving for boiler replacement by house type and by the rating of the boiler being replaced, and it states its assumptions: an A-rated condensing gas boiler with a programmer, room thermostat and thermostatic radiator valves, replacing an older boiler, at fuel prices from July 2026. Its average installed cost for Great Britain is £3,500. The page was last updated on 20 May 2026 and we read it again on 4 September 2026.

Its annual savings, in pounds, for the five house types it models:

House typeReplacing a D-rated boilerReplacing an E-rated boilerReplacing an F-rated boilerReplacing a G-rated boiler
Detached house£220£270£350£500
Semi-detached house£190£210£240£350
Mid-terrace house£140£160£180£270
Detached bungalow£170£190£220£320
Mid-floor flat£90£100£120£170

Three things to notice before the payback sum. The saving roughly doubles between a D-rated and a G-rated boiler, because a G-rated boiler is throwing away around a third of the gas it burns up the flue and a D-rated one much less. The saving also roughly halves between a detached house and a flat, because a flat has less to heat. And there is no column for A, B or C-rated boilers, because the Energy Saving Trust does not model them: the saving from replacing a boiler that is already condensing is too small to be worth a figure.

The £3,500 is an average, and boilers are not. A straight swap of a combi for a combi on the same wall is at the bottom of the range; moving the boiler, converting from a system boiler with a cylinder to a combi, or adding a magnetic filter and powerflush pushes it up. Use your own quote in the sum once you have one.

2. Payback in years for 20 house and boiler combinations

Payback is £3,500 divided by the annual saving above. This is our arithmetic on the Energy Saving Trust's figures.

Mid-terrace house25.0 years21.9 years19.4 years13.0 years
Detached bungalow20.6 years18.4 years15.9 years10.9 years
Mid-floor flat38.9 years35.0 years29.2 years20.6 years

Read this against how long a boiler lasts. Manufacturers' guarantees on new boilers run from two years to twelve depending on brand and installer, and a well-serviced boiler is commonly still going at fifteen. So a payback under ten years means the boiler pays for itself comfortably inside its own life; ten to fifteen is a saving, but a thin one; over fifteen means you are buying the new boiler for reliability and comfort, and the fuel saving is a bonus rather than a reason.

Only four of the twenty combinations come in at ten years or under, and all four involve an F or G-rated boiler. That is the headline of this article: the case for replacing a working boiler is strong only when the old one is genuinely inefficient, which mostly means a non-condensing boiler installed before 2005.

The figures are at July 2026 fuel prices. Gas under the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 is 7.97p per kWh, up from 7.33p in the July to September period, so the savings above are about 9 per cent understated for the coming winter and the paybacks correspondingly shorter. We have left the Energy Saving Trust's figures as published rather than adjust them, because the next cap lands on 1 January 2027 and will move them again.

3. How to find your boiler's efficiency letter

The letter is the whole sum, so find it before you get a quote. Three ways, from easiest to most reliable.

The age test. Gas boilers installed after 2005 had to be condensing under building regulations, and condensing boilers are A or B-rated. If the flue is plastic and there is a plastic condensate pipe running from the bottom of the boiler to a drain, it is condensing. If the flue is metal and there is no condensate pipe, it is not, and it is almost certainly E, F or G.

The model number. Every boiler has a data plate, usually inside the front drop-down panel or on the side. Put the model number into the Product Characteristics Database, the free industry register used for building regulations, and it returns the seasonal efficiency as a percentage. Above 90 per cent is A; 86 to 90 is B; 82 to 86 is C; 78 to 82 is D; 74 to 78 is E; 70 to 74 is F; under 70 is G.

Your EPC. If your home has an Energy Performance Certificate from the last ten years, the heating section names the boiler type and rates it. Certificates are free to look up on the government register by postcode, and this is the quickest check for a house you are buying.

A G-rated boiler in 2026 is a rarity: it means a boiler from the 1990s or earlier, often a floor-standing one or a back boiler behind a fire. If you have one, the arithmetic is unambiguous and the only question is when. A D-rated boiler is a much more common find, an early non-condensing model from the late 1990s or early 2000s, and there the arithmetic says repair it until it stops.

4. Repair or replace: the one-year rule

The rule we use is simple. Repair if the bill is less than one year's saving from replacement. Replace if it is more, or if this is the second big repair in two years.

For a semi with a D-rated boiler, one year's saving is £190. A new fan, a diverter valve, a pump or a printed circuit board all typically land between £150 and £400 fitted, so the first of those is a repair; the second in the same winter is the sign. For a semi with a G-rated boiler, one year's saving is £350 and the calculus tips faster, because every winter you keep it costs you £350 you would not otherwise spend.

Two exceptions. A heat exchanger failure on an older boiler is usually the end, because the part alone can approach £1,000 and the rest of the boiler is the same age. And a boiler that fails the annual safety check, or for which parts are no longer made, is replaced whatever the arithmetic says.

What the annual service buys you. A service costs £80 to £120 and is required to keep most guarantees valid. It does not measurably improve efficiency on a modern boiler, whatever the leaflet says, but it does catch the fan bearing or the leaking seal before it becomes the January emergency, and the January emergency is where the money is lost.

The controls are half the saving. The Energy Saving Trust's saving figures assume the new boiler comes with a room thermostat, programmer and thermostatic radiator valves. If your old boiler has none of those, some of the saving is available now, for a few hundred pounds, without touching the boiler. We have covered smart thermostats and smart valves separately.

5. Worked example: the Okafor family in Northampton

Three-bed semi, gas, non-condensing wall boiler installed 2001, serviced yearly, one repair last winter

The Okafors' engineer told them at the last service that the boiler "will not last forever" and offered a quote of £3,200 for a like-for-like combi swap with new valves. The old boiler works. They want to know whether to take the quote now or wait.

Step one: the letter. The data plate gives a model number; the Product Characteristics Database returns 78.4 per cent seasonal efficiency. That is the bottom of band D.

Step two: the saving. Semi-detached, D-rated: £190 a year at July 2026 prices, a little more at the October to December cap.

Step three: the payback. £3,200 divided by £190 is 16.8 years. On the Energy Saving Trust's £3,500 average, 18.4 years.

Step four: the repair history. One repair last winter, a fan at £260. That is under one year's saving, so it was the right call. A second repair this winter would be the signal.

Decision. Keep the boiler, keep servicing it, put £100 a month aside so the replacement is a planned purchase and not a January one, and get two more quotes in the spring when installers are quiet. If it fails first, they have the money and the quotes. The £190 a year they are forgoing is real, but it is a fraction of what a distress purchase would cost them in the wrong month.

6. The honest verdict

Replace a working boiler on savings grounds only if it is F or G-rated, which in practice means a non-condensing boiler from before 2005 with a metal flue. In a house, that pays back in seven to fifteen years; in a flat, twenty or more, and there the honest answer is to wait for it to fail.

If your boiler is D-rated or better, do not replace it to save money: the payback runs to eighteen years and beyond, longer than the new boiler will last. Repair it while repairs cost less than a year's saving, service it, fit the controls you are missing, and save towards the replacement so that when the day comes you buy it on your terms rather than the coldest week's.

Frequently asked

How long does a new boiler take to pay for itself? Using the Energy Saving Trust's £3,500 average cost and its savings at July 2026 prices, between 7 years (detached house, replacing a G-rated boiler) and 39 years (mid-floor flat, replacing a D-rated boiler). A semi replacing a G-rated boiler is 10 years; replacing a D-rated one, 18.

Should I replace a 15 year old boiler that still works? A 15 year old boiler was installed in 2011 and is almost certainly condensing and A or B-rated, so the fuel saving from replacing it is small. Replace it when repairs cost more than a year's saving or when parts stop being available, not on efficiency grounds.

How do I find out my boiler's efficiency rating? Look for the model number on the data plate and search the Product Characteristics Database, which gives seasonal efficiency as a percentage. Above 90 per cent is A-rated; under 70 per cent is G. A metal flue with no condensate pipe means a non-condensing boiler, usually E to G.

Does a boiler service save energy? Not measurably on a modern boiler. It keeps the guarantee valid, keeps the safety check current, and catches failing parts before they cause an emergency, which is where the money is.
