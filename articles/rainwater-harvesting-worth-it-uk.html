Your Roof Catches 80,000 Litres A Year. The Reason That Is Not A Saving Is The Interesting Part

Rainwater harvesting has the most attractive arithmetic in home energy and water, right up until you look at the second half of it. The supply is free, abundant and already landing on your house. The problem is that almost nothing in a British home is allowed to drink it.

The short version

  • The collection sum is simple and generous. Roof area times annual rainfall times a yield coefficient. A 100 square metre roof at the UK average of about 1,000mm collects roughly 80,000 to 85,000 litres a year after real world losses.
  • Real world collection efficiency is 75 to 85 per cent, because of evaporation, gutter overflow in heavy rain and filter drainage. The theoretical number always overstates it.
  • Metered water costs roughly 2.47 pounds per cubic metre for supply with Thames Water, plus about 1.55 for sewerage. Rates vary a lot between the seventeen companies in England and Wales.
  • The constraint is demand, not supply. Only the garden, the washing machine and WC flushing can legally and practically use it, and in most homes that is well under 40 cubic metres a year.
  • The payback on a full system at 3,000 pounds against about 161 pounds a year saved is roughly 18.6 years. The tank will still be there. The pump probably will not.
  • A 200 litre water butt at about 80 pounds pays back in around 6.7 years on garden use alone, and faster if you are metered and water a lot. That is the version most households should buy.

1. The collection sum, which is the easy half

The standard method, set out in the current UK code of practice for rainwater harvesting, is straightforward:

Annual yield in litres = roof area in square metres x annual rainfall in metres x yield coefficient x filter coefficient x 1,000.

A 100 square metre roof, 1,000mm of rain, a yield coefficient of about 0.9 for a pitched tiled roof and a filter coefficient of about 0.9, gives roughly 81,000 litres, or 81 cubic metres a year.

Real world collection efficiency averages 75 to 85 per cent of the theoretical figure once you account for evaporation, gutter overflow during heavy rain, and water lost through the filter. So the honest planning number for a typical semi is somewhere around 80,000 litres.

At Thames Water's 2026 to 2027 metered rate of about 2.47 pounds per cubic metre for supply, 80 cubic metres of water is worth roughly 198 pounds. Add sewerage at about 1.55 pounds per cubic metre for any of it that goes down the drain rather than onto the garden, and the headline number gets bigger still.

This is where most rainwater harvesting sales pitches stop. It is also where the actual analysis starts.

2. The demand ceiling, which is the hard half

You cannot drink it, wash in it, or run it through a combi boiler. Rainwater in a domestic UK system is non potable, which means it is restricted to a short list of uses: garden watering, WC flushing, and washing machine supply where the system is designed and marked for it. Everything else, drinking, cooking, showering, the basin, the bath, the dishwasher, has to come off the mains.

The roof is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is that most of the water a household uses has to be drinkable, and rainwater is not.

Put numbers on what is left. A typical UK household uses somewhere around 120 to 150 cubic metres of water a year. WC flushing is commonly a quarter to a third of that, so call it 35 cubic metres. Garden watering in an average year, in an average garden, is a surprisingly small number, often under 10 cubic metres, because it rains. Laundry might add another 15 if plumbed in.

So the realistic replaceable demand is around 40 to 50 cubic metres a year, not 80. The roof collects twice what you can use, and the surplus overflows to the drain exactly as it did before you spent anything.

There is a second, quieter problem. Rain and demand are out of phase. The roof collects most in the winter, when nobody waters a garden, and least in July, when everybody does. That is why domestic systems need 3,000 to 7,500 litres of storage to carry water from wet periods into dry ones, and why the tank is the expensive part.

4. Worked example: the Petrie household in Wakefield

A 1950s semi with a 78 square metre roof, four people, on a meter, a greenhouse and a vegetable patch that gets watered seriously from May to September.

What the roof collects. 78 x 1.0 x 0.9 x 0.9 x 1,000 = about 63,000 litres, or 63 cubic metres a year.

What they can actually use. Garden and greenhouse, about 14 cubic metres in a normal year. WC flushing for four people, about 32 cubic metres. Nothing else. Total realistic demand 46 cubic metres, which the roof easily covers.

Option A, two 200 litre butts on the downpipes, 160 pounds. Storage is the limit here, not collection: 400 litres runs out in four days of dry weather, so they realistically capture about 8 cubic metres of the garden demand. At 2.47 pounds, that is 19.76 pounds a year. Payback 8.1 years.

Option B, full underground system with WC and outside tap, 3,200 pounds. Replaces about 40 cubic metres at the combined 4.02 pound rate, so 160.80 pounds a year. Payback 19.9 years, before the first pump replacement.

Option C, one 1,000 litre tank on a stand feeding a gravity outside tap, 420 pounds. Captures nearly all of the 14 cubic metre garden demand. 34.58 pounds a year. Payback 12.1 years.

None of those is a good financial return, and it is worth saying so directly rather than picking the least bad one and calling it a saving. If the Petries put 3,200 pounds into loft insulation or a proper heating control upgrade instead, the payback would be measured in single figures, which is the comparison anyone considering this should run, using the same method as our loft insulation payback guide.

What the water butts do buy is a hosepipe ban that does not kill the vegetable patch, and that is a real thing to want for 160 pounds.

5. If you are not on a meter, stop reading

This needs stating in one blunt paragraph, because it is the single most common wasted purchase in this category.

If your water bill is based on rateable value rather than a meter, rainwater harvesting saves you exactly nothing. Not a reduced amount. Nothing. Your bill is not calculated from your usage, so reducing your usage does not change it. Every payback figure in this guide assumes a meter.

Whether you should be on a meter at all is a separate question with a genuinely useful answer, and it usually comes down to whether you have more bedrooms than people, which we worked through in is a water meter worth it.

Frequently asked

Can I plumb rainwater into my toilet myself? No, not properly. Any connection between a non potable supply and the household plumbing has to be protected against backflow to the standard required by the Water Supply (Water Fittings) Regulations, and the pipework has to be marked. Get it done by someone who works to those regulations, and tell your water company.

Does a water butt need emptying in winter? Leave it part full and the ice has room to expand, which is kinder to the butt than a full one. What matters more is that the diverter is set so heavy winter rain overflows back to the downpipe rather than pooling near the wall.

Is the water safe for a vegetable garden? For watering soil, generally yes, and gardeners have done it for centuries. Avoid spraying it directly onto leaves you will eat raw, avoid collecting off a moss treated or bitumen roof, and do not store it in an open container that birds can reach.

Do I get a discount for using less water? Only through the meter, and only on what the meter measures. Some water companies also offer a surface water drainage rebate if your rainwater does not enter the public sewer at all, which is worth asking about separately and is usually a larger sum than people expect.

The honest verdict

Buy a water butt or two if you have a garden and a meter. At about 80 pounds each with a payback around six to eight years they are marginal on money and genuinely useful in a hosepipe ban, which is the real reason to own one.

Do not buy a full underground harvesting system for an existing house on financial grounds. At roughly nineteen years before the first pump replacement, it does not pay back, and the money buys far more insulation than it does water.

If you are unmetered, buy neither, because you will save nothing at all. Sort the meter question first, then come back to this.
