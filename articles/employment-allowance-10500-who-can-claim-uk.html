£10,500 Off Your Employer NI Bill, Unless You Are the Only Person on the Payroll

Employment Allowance is one of the few genuinely large reliefs available to a small employer, and two things stop people getting it. The first is a rule about one-director companies that catches a very large number of contractors. The second is that nobody applies it on your behalf: it is a claim, and an unclaimed allowance is simply money the company hands over.

The short version

  • £10,500 for 2026 to 2027, set against your employer Class 1 National Insurance bill.
  • Employer NI is 15% on earnings above a secondary threshold of £5,000 a year, which is what makes the allowance worth so much.
  • The one-director rule. If your company has only one director, they must not be the only employee liable for secondary Class 1 NI. Most one-person companies are out.
  • It is not automatic. You claim it through your payroll software, and you can claim for previous tax years if you missed it.
  • The £100,000 cap is gone. From April 2025, employers paying more than £100,000 in Class 1 liabilities can apply.
  • Connected companies get one allowance between them, not one each.

1. What the allowance is worth in practice

Employment Allowance lets an eligible employer reduce their annual employer National Insurance liability by up to the allowance amount. For 2026 to 2027 that amount is £10,500.

To see why that is large, you need the rate it works against. For 2026 to 2027, employer Class 1 National Insurance is charged at 15% on earnings above a secondary threshold of £5,000 a year, which is £96 a week or £417 a month.

A low threshold and a 15% rate together mean employer NI starts early and builds fast. On one employee paid £30,000, it is 15% of £25,000, or £3,750. Three such employees and the bill is £11,250.

The rule of thumb. The allowance covers employer NI on roughly £70,000 of pay above the secondary threshold. Below that it wipes out your employer NI. Above it, you pay the excess. The apprenticeship levy cancels itself the same way at a far higher level: 0.5% of the pay bill against a £15,000 allowance that is exactly 0.5% of £3 million, so an employer at or under £3 million pays nothing at all.

2. Who can claim, and who cannot

The core test is short. You can claim for the current tax year if you are a business or a public body and you do less than half your work in the public sector. Charities can claim, including community amateur sports clubs, and so can someone employing a care or support worker. The exclusions are where the detail sits.

  • Off-payroll workers. Someone whose earnings fall within the IR35 rules cannot be included.
  • Domestic staff. A nanny or gardener employed for personal or household work cannot be included, unless they are a carer or support worker.
  • Connected companies. If you are part of a group of companies or charities, only one company in the group can claim. Not one each.
  • Multiple payrolls. One claim, against one payroll, even if you run several.
  • More than half your work in the public sector. This catches some contractors to councils and the NHS.

One restriction no longer applies, and outdated guidance is still everywhere: the £100,000 Class 1 liability cap was removed, so from April 2025 employers above that level can apply.

3. The one-director rule, in detail

This is the provision that disqualifies the largest number of small companies, and it is worded more precisely than most summaries admit. GOV.UK puts it this way: if your company has only one director, they must not be the only employee liable for secondary Class 1 National Insurance.

Read the words carefully, because two of them do all the work.

  • "Only one director". A company with two directors is not caught at all.
  • "Liable for secondary Class 1". The test is not headcount. It is whether anyone else on the payroll is paid enough to generate employer NI, which means above the £5,000 secondary threshold. A second employee paid £3,000 a year does not create liability and so does not help.

The practical effect is that the classic one-person limited company, a single director taking a salary with nobody else on the payroll, cannot claim. That is the single most common reason a claim is wrong.

There is an obvious sounding workaround and it deserves a blunt warning. Putting a spouse on the payroll above the secondary threshold purely to unlock £10,500 only works if the employment is real: the work has to be done and the pay has to be justifiable for it. A salary that fails that test is not a deductible business expense, and manufacturing an employment to obtain a relief is the sort of arrangement HMRC looks at closely.

4. Worked example: Dara's company in Norwich

Dara, Norwich. Runs a limited company with four employees on £25,000 each, plus herself as director on a salary above the threshold.

Employer NI per employee. £25,000 less the £5,000 secondary threshold is £20,000. At 15%, that is £3,000 each.

Four employees. £12,000 of employer NI before the allowance.

After Employment Allowance. £12,000 less £10,500 leaves £1,500 to pay. The allowance covers 87.5% of the bill.

Her eligibility. She is the only director, but she is not the only employee liable for secondary Class 1 NI, because all four staff are well above the £5,000 threshold. So the rule does not bite and the company can claim.

The contrast. If she ran the company alone, the £10,500 would vanish, because she would become the only employee generating employer NI.

5. Claiming it, including for past years

The most expensive misunderstanding about Employment Allowance is that it arrives on its own. It does not. It is claimed through your payroll software, by submitting an Employment Payment Summary with the claim indicator set. If nobody ticks that box, the company pays employer NI it did not owe. Three points that save money:

  • It does not roll forward automatically in every setup. Some payroll software carries the claim into the new tax year and some asks you to re-state it. Check every April rather than assuming.
  • You can claim for previous years. If you were eligible and did not claim, there is a route to claim for past tax years, with its own rules. Worth checking if you have had staff for a while.
  • Claim early in the year. The allowance offsets your liability as it arises, so claiming in month one beats reclaiming later.

The honest verdict

For an employer with a handful of staff, this is one of the largest and simplest reliefs available, and the arithmetic is unusually friendly: £10,500 against a bill charged at 15% above a £5,000 threshold wipes out employer NI on roughly the first £70,000 of pay above that threshold.

For a one-person limited company it is, bluntly, not available, and no amount of reading around the rule changes that. Time is better spent on the reliefs that do apply to you.

For everyone else, the thing that costs real money is not the eligibility rules. It is the unticked box. Check your payroll software today, check it again in April, and check whether you were eligible in years you did not claim.

Frequently asked

I am the only director and the only employee. Can I claim? No. GOV.UK states that if your company has only one director, they must not be the only employee liable for secondary Class 1 National Insurance. A single director with nobody else on the payroll above the £5,000 secondary threshold is outside the allowance.

Does a second employee on a small wage unlock it? Only if that employee is actually liable for secondary Class 1 National Insurance, which means paid above the £5,000 a year secondary threshold. Someone paid £3,000 a year creates no employer NI liability and so does not change the position.

Is the allowance per company or per group? Per group. If you are part of a group of connected companies or charities, only one company in the group can claim the allowance, and you choose which.
