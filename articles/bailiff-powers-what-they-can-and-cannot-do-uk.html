The 14 days between a bailiff letter and a bailiff knock are worth hundreds of pounds

Enforcement fees in England and Wales are not negotiable and not invented by the firm. They are set out in a table in a 2014 statutory instrument, in three stages, and the jump from the first stage to the second is the part nobody explains in time.

The short version

  • Three fee stages, fixed by law. Compliance £75, enforcement £235, sale or disposal £110, under the Taking Control of Goods (Fees) Regulations 2014.
  • The percentage only bites above £1,500. At the enforcement and sale stages an extra 7.5 per cent is added on the part of the debt over £1,500, and nothing below it.
  • At least 14 days' notice. GOV.UK says bailiffs must usually give you at least 14 days' notice of a first visit. That window is the compliance stage.
  • You usually do not have to open the door. They cannot force entry, cannot come in if only under 16s or vulnerable people are there, cannot call between 9pm and 6am, and cannot come in through anything but a door.
  • Work tools up to £1,350 are protected as a combined total, not per item, and essentials such as clothes, a cooker and a fridge cannot be taken at all.
  • Scotland is a different system with sheriff officers, and none of the England and Wales fee table applies there.

1. The three fee stages, and what moves you from one to the next

The fees an enforcement agent can charge in England and Wales are not set by the firm. They are in the Schedule to the Taking Control of Goods (Fees) Regulations 2014, and they work in stages. For ordinary enforcement, which covers council tax, parking penalties, court fines and most county court judgments, the table reads like this.

  • Compliance stage: £75 fixed, no percentage. This is triggered the moment the enforcement agent sends you the notice of enforcement. You have not seen anybody yet.
  • Enforcement stage: £235 fixed, plus 7.5 per cent of the sum to be recovered above £1,500. This is triggered when an agent attends.
  • Sale or disposal stage: £110 fixed, plus a further 7.5 per cent above £1,500. This is triggered when your goods are taken away to be sold.

A second table covers enforcement under a High Court writ, which is more expensive: £75 compliance, £190 first enforcement, £495 second enforcement, £525 sale or disposal, with the 7.5 per cent running above £1,000 rather than £1,500. If your letter names a High Court enforcement officer, you are in that table.

The practical point is the shape of it. Stage one is £75 and stage two is £235. Doing nothing for a fortnight is the single most expensive decision available in the whole process, and it costs you £160 before anybody has even looked at your television.

2. What a bailiff is not allowed to do

GOV.UK's guidance, read on 13 September 2026, is unusually blunt. You usually do not have to open your door or let them in. They cannot enter by force, for example by pushing past you. They cannot enter if only children under 16 or vulnerable people, which GOV.UK gives as including people with disabilities, are present. They cannot enter between 9pm and 6am, or come in through anything except the door.

There is one carve out, and it is worth knowing rather than being surprised by it. Bailiffs are allowed to force their way in to collect unpaid criminal fines, Income Tax or Stamp Duty, but GOV.UK says only as a last resort. Council tax arrears and parking penalties are not in that list.

What they can take: non essential items such as a television or a games console are fair game if you let them in. They cannot take things you need, and GOV.UK names clothes, a cooker and a fridge. They cannot take work tools and equipment which together are worth less than £1,350. That word together is doing a lot of work, because it is a combined cap on the whole set, not a per item exemption. They cannot take someone else's belongings, but you have to prove the goods are not yours.

You can check who you are dealing with through the letterbox or at the window. Ask for identity, the company, a contact number and a detailed breakdown of the amount owed. Certificated enforcement agents appear on a public Ministry of Justice register. If someone says they are an Approved Enforcement Agent, GOV.UK lists only three firms: CDER Group, Marston Holdings Limited and Jacobs Enforcement. Anyone claiming to be a bailiff who is not is committing fraud.

3. A worked example with the actual arithmetic

Nadia in Luton owes £1,900 in council tax arrears. The council has passed it to a certificated enforcement agent, and a notice of enforcement lands on her mat.

At that moment the compliance fee of £75 has already been added. Her balance is £1,975. If she rings the firm inside the 14 day window and agrees a payment arrangement she can keep it there.

If she puts the letter in a drawer and an agent attends, the enforcement stage fee applies. That is £235 fixed, plus 7.5 per cent of the amount above £1,500. Her debt is £1,900, so the slice above £1,500 is £400, and 7.5 per cent of £400 is £30. The enforcement stage therefore costs her £265.

Her total fees are now £75 plus £265, which is £340, and her balance is £2,240. She has paid £265 for a knock at the door. If it went further and goods were removed for sale, the sale stage would add £110 plus another £30, taking the fees to £480.

Now change one number. If her arrears were £1,400, the percentage would be zero at every stage, because nothing sits above £1,500, and the enforcement stage would cost exactly £235. The percentage is not a general surcharge, and many write ups describe it as though it is.

4. What actually helps, and what does not

Paying on the doorstep is allowed and you do not have to let anyone in to do it. Get a receipt. If you cannot pay it all, GOV.UK says you can offer weekly or monthly payments, and says equally plainly that the bailiff does not have to accept. That is why the compliance window matters more than the doorstep conversation.

Free help exists and is genuinely free. GOV.UK points at Citizens Advice, MoneyHelper, National Debtline and StepChange. Nobody needs to pay a company to talk to an enforcement agent for them.

Two things this guide does not help with. In Scotland the whole system is different, it runs through sheriff officers, and mygov.scot is the place to start. And where the debt is a criminal fine, an Approved Enforcement Agent can arrest you if there is a warrant for breaking a community penalty order, which is a different problem from a fee table.

The verdict

The fees are fixed, public and not up for debate, which is the good news. The bad news is that the cheapest stage is the one that looks like it can be ignored. A notice of enforcement costs £75. Letting it expire costs at least £235 more, and more again once the debt is over £1,500. If a notice arrives, the phone call inside those 14 days is worth more per minute than almost anything else you will do that month.

This is general information about how the rules work in England and Wales, not legal advice. If you are being enforced against, speak to one of the free advice services named above before you agree to anything.

Frequently asked

Can a bailiff break down my door? Not for council tax, parking penalties or a county court judgment. GOV.UK says bailiffs are allowed to force entry only to collect unpaid criminal fines, Income Tax or Stamp Duty, and even then only as a last resort.

Can they take my car off the driveway? If you do not let them in or agree to pay, GOV.UK says they could take things from outside your home, and gives a car as the example. Goods outside are not protected the way the inside of your home is.

Is the £1,350 tools exemption per tool? No. GOV.UK words it as work tools and equipment which together are worth less than £1,350. It is a combined total for the set.

Do the same fees apply in Scotland? No. The Taking Control of Goods (Fees) Regulations 2014 do not run there. Scotland uses sheriff officers under a separate system and mygov.scot explains it.
