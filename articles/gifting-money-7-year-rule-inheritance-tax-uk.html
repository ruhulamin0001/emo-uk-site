Gifting money: what you can give away this year without starting any clock at all

The seven year rule gets all the attention, and most of what is written about it is wrong. A large amount can be given away with no clock running, and the taper relief everyone quotes almost never applies to an ordinary family gift.

Ruhul Amin Published 6 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • £3,000 a year is exempt straight away, and you can carry one unused year forward, so a first gift can be £6,000 with no clock (GOV.UK, page updated 2 September 2026).
  • Small gifts of £250 per person are unlimited in number, as long as you have not used another allowance on that same person.
  • Regular payments out of your normal income are unlimited and exempt immediately. This is the most powerful and least used rule in the whole system.
  • Taper relief only applies once your gifts in the 7 years before death exceed £325,000. Below that it never applies at all, so a £50,000 gift is never taxed at 24%.
  • Keep records. Whoever handles your estate has to reconstruct every gift from the last 7 years, and if they cannot, they have to assume the worst.

1. What you can give away with no clock at all

Before any seven year rule is involved, several kinds of gift are simply exempt the moment you make them. GOV.UK sets them out on its "Rules on giving gifts" page, updated 2 September 2026.

Your annual exemption: £3,000 a tax year. You can give it all to one person or split it. Any unused part can be carried forward, but only for one tax year, so the most you can ever have available is £6,000. The tax year runs 6 April to 5 April.

Small gifts: £250 per person, as many people as you like. The condition is that you have not used another allowance on that same person. So you can give £250 each to twenty different grandchildren, but you cannot give one grandchild £3,000 from the annual exemption and then £250 on top.

Wedding and civil partnership gifts. £5,000 to a child, £2,500 to a grandchild or great-grandchild, £1,000 to anyone else. These can be combined with the annual exemption on the same person, so a parent can give a child £5,000 as a wedding gift and £3,000 from the annual exemption in the same year.

Regular payments out of your normal income: no limit. This is the one nobody uses. GOV.UK's own words: "There's no limit to how much you can give tax free, as long as you can afford the payments after meeting your usual living costs" and "you pay from your regular monthly income". It has to be a pattern, not a one-off. Paying a child's rent, paying into a savings account for a child under 18, supporting an elderly relative: all named on the page as examples. Like wedding gifts, it can be combined with the annual exemption.

Gifts to a spouse or civil partner: unlimited, provided you are legally married or in a civil partnership and they live in the UK permanently.

Gifts to charities and political parties: unlimited.

Add those together and a married couple supporting a child can move a very substantial sum every year with no clock running at all, before anyone needs to think about seven years.

2. The 7 year rule, and what actually happens if you die inside it

Anything that is not covered above is a gift that only becomes fully exempt if you live for seven years afterwards. GOV.UK's phrasing: "No tax is due on any gifts you give if you live for 7 years after giving them", with an exception for gifts into a trust.

Here is the part that is almost always described wrongly. If you die inside seven years, the gift does not simply get taxed. It goes back into the calculation and it eats your nil rate band first, before the rest of your estate.

The nil rate band is £325,000. Gifts made in the seven years before death are set against it in order. Only once those gifts have used the whole £325,000 does any tax fall on a gift itself, and only then does taper relief come into the picture.

So for the overwhelming majority of families, a gift made within seven years of death produces no tax bill on the recipient at all. What it does is reduce the nil rate band available to the estate, which increases the estate's own tax bill by 40% of the gift. That is a real cost, and it is a completely different mechanism from "the gift is taxed".

3. Taper relief: the most misunderstood table in British tax

This is the table everyone quotes, straight from GOV.UK.

Years between gift and deathRate of tax on the gift
Less than 3 years40%
3 to 4 years32%
4 to 5 years24%
5 to 6 years16%
6 to 7 years8%
7 or more0%

And here is the sentence directly underneath it on the same page, which is the one that gets dropped: "Taper relief only applies if the total value of gifts made in the 7 years before you die is over the £325,000 tax-free threshold."

Read that carefully. If your gifts over seven years add up to less than £325,000, taper relief is irrelevant to you. There is no tax on the gift for it to reduce. Someone who gives away £50,000 and dies four years later has not "saved 16%" and does not "pay 24%". Their gift is simply set against the nil rate band.

GOV.UK's own worked example makes the point. Sally gave £325,000 to her sister four years and two months before dying, and there was no tax on it, because it sat inside the threshold. Her friend received £100,000 three years before her death and paid 32% on it, £32,000, precisely because the earlier £325,000 had already used the whole band.

The practical takeaway: taper relief is a rule for people giving away more than £325,000. For everyone else the only question that matters is whether you live seven years, and the answer is binary.

4. Margaret in Ipswich: £50,000 to her son at 68

Margaret is 68, widowed, and lives in Ipswich. Her house and savings come to £400,000 after the gift. She wants to give her son Owen £50,000 to help with a deposit. She made no gifts last year.

Step one, the free part. £3,000 for this tax year plus £3,000 carried forward from last year is £6,000, exempt immediately and gone from the calculation for good.

Step two, the rest. The remaining £44,000 is a gift with a seven year clock on it.

If Margaret lives seven years, the £44,000 disappears from her estate entirely. Her estate of £400,000 pays 40% on the amount over £325,000, which is £75,000, so £30,000 of tax.

If Margaret dies in year four, the £44,000 comes back and is set against her nil rate band first. It is nowhere near £325,000, so there is no tax on the gift and Owen owes nothing. What happens is that her band available to the rest of the estate falls from £325,000 to £281,000. Her estate of £400,000 now pays 40% on £119,000, which is £47,600.

The gift cost the estate £17,600 more than making no gift at all, which is 40% of the £44,000. It did not cost 24%, and Owen was never taxed. Those are two entirely different sentences and only one of them is true.

What Margaret could do instead. If she can genuinely afford £400 a month out of her pension income after covering her normal living costs, those payments are exempt immediately under the normal expenditure out of income rule, with no clock at all. Over five years that is £24,000 out of the estate with no seven year risk whatsoever, and it can run alongside her £3,000 annual exemption. It is slower, and for a house deposit it may not fit the timetable, but it is the cleanest route there is.

5. Gifts with reservation, records, and who actually pays

You cannot give something away and keep using it. GOV.UK calls this a "gift with reservation" and gives three examples: giving your home to a relative but still living there, giving away a caravan but still using it for your holidays, giving away a painting but still hanging it in your house. The item stays in your estate no matter how long ago you signed it over. Giving the family home to the children and carrying on living in it rent free is the classic version, and it does not work.

Selling something cheaply is a gift. If you sell your house to your child for less than its market value, GOV.UK counts the difference as a gift. So is money you lose selling anything below its worth.

Keep a written record. The person dealing with your estate has to work out every gift you made in the last seven years: what it was, who got it, what it was worth, and when. A single page in a folder with the date, the amount and the recipient will save your executor weeks and may save your family money, because gaps get resolved conservatively.

Who pays. Any Inheritance Tax on gifts is normally paid by the estate. But once you have given away more than £325,000 in the seven years before death, the recipients themselves become liable for the tax on their own gift. That is worth telling anyone you are giving a large sum to, because a person who spent the money and then receives a tax bill has a serious problem.

Trusts are different. The seven year rule as described here does not apply in the same way to gifts into trust, and trusts have their own charging regime. If a trust is on the table, this is the point to pay a solicitor.

The verdict

Use the exemptions before you use the clock. £3,000 a year, £6,000 in a first year, £250 small gifts to as many people as you like, wedding gifts, and above all regular payments out of surplus income, which are unlimited and exempt on the day you make them. Only what is left over needs seven years. And when you read that a gift made four years before death is "taxed at 24%", check the sentence GOV.UK puts under its own taper table: that only happens once your seven-year gifts pass £325,000. For most families the real cost of dying inside the seven years is 40% of the gift falling on the estate, not a taper on the recipient.

Frequently asked

Do I have to tell HMRC when I make a gift? No. There is no form to file at the time and no registration. The record only matters at death, when your executor has to list gifts made in the previous 7 years. That is exactly why keeping your own note is worth doing, because nobody else is recording it.

Does the £3,000 allowance reset for each person I give to? No. It is £3,000 in total across everybody, per tax year, per giver. A couple each have their own £3,000, so between them they can move £6,000 a year, or £12,000 in a year where both have an unused allowance carried forward.

Can I give my house to my children and keep living in it? Not without consequences. GOV.UK treats that as a gift with reservation and the house stays in your estate for Inheritance Tax. Paying a full market rent to the new owners can change the position, but that rent is income in their hands and the arrangement needs proper advice. This is not a do-it-yourself area.

Is Inheritance Tax changing? The threshold has been £325,000 for many years and is frozen, and from 6 April 2027 unused pension pots come into the estate for Inheritance Tax, which will pull far more families over the line. The Budget on 28 October 2026 could change any of it. The figures here are those on GOV.UK on 2 September 2026.
