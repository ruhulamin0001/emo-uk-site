A Letter From HMRC About Your Online Selling Is Not a Tax Bill, and Two Numbers Explain Why

Selling platforms have been handing seller data to HMRC, and nudge letters have been landing all year. The panic is mostly manufactured, because the number that triggers a report and the number that triggers a tax return are different numbers measuring different things.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Nothing about what you owe has changed. The rules on when side income is taxable are the same as they were. What changed is how easily HMRC can see it.
  • Platforms report goods sellers who pass either trigger: more than 30 sales in a calendar year, or total sales above the sterling equivalent of EUR 2,000. Either one is enough.
  • For services platforms there is no threshold at all. Ride hailing, delivery, letting and freelance marketplaces report from the first payment.
  • The tax test is different: GBP 1,000 of gross trading income in a tax year. Not profit, gross. Cross it and you may need to register for Self Assessment.
  • 30 sales is not GBP 1,000, and GBP 1,000 is not profit. Confusing those three numbers is the entire reason people think they are in trouble when they are not.
  • Selling your own used possessions is generally not trading. Clearing a wardrobe you bought to wear is not a business, however many items it takes.
  • Platforms must tell you if your data was shared, so a notification is information, not an accusation.
  • If you do need to register, the deadline for 2025 to 2026 is 5 October 2026. Late registration is a failure to notify and the penalty is geared to the tax owed.

1. What changed, and what did not

Under rules the UK adopted from the OECD model for digital platforms, selling and gig platforms have to collect information about their sellers and pass it to HMRC once a year. Vinted, eBay, Etsy, Airbnb, Uber, Deliveroo and the rest are all in scope. Data for the 2025 calendar year was due with HMRC by 31 January 2026, and HMRC now matches it against Self Assessment records to spot gaps.

Through 2026 that fed a campaign of nudge letters to people whose platform income did not appear on a tax return, and the coverage that followed was largely about a crackdown.

Here is the part that got lost. No tax rule changed. If you were trading and making more than the allowance in 2019, you owed tax then and simply might not have been caught. If you were clearing your loft in 2019, you owed nothing then and you owe nothing now. The visibility changed. The liability did not.

2. The reporting triggers, in the platform hands

For a platform selling goods, your data gets reported if you pass either of two triggers in a calendar year: more than 30 sales, or total proceeds above the sterling equivalent of EUR 2,000. Either one on its own is enough.

Note that the second one is set in euro, so the pound figure moves with the exchange rate and the platform applies its own conversion. That alone should tell you it was never designed as a tax threshold. No tax rule in the UK is denominated in euro.

For platforms providing services, meaning ride hailing, food delivery, short-term letting, freelancing and similar, there is no threshold. Reporting applies from the first payment.

The platform also has to notify you if your information is among that shared. That notification is a copy of what was sent. It is not a demand and it does not mean HMRC has concluded anything about you.

Thirty sales is a platform reporting trigger set in euro. A thousand pounds is a tax threshold set in sterling. They were written by different people for different reasons, and nothing makes them line up.

3. The tax test, which is a different number

Whether you owe anything turns on the trading allowance, which is GBP 1,000 per tax year. Below that, trading income is covered by the allowance and there is generally nothing to report. Above it, you may need to register for Self Assessment.

Two details do most of the damage here.

The GBP 1,000 is measured on gross, not profit. It is total money in, before stock costs, postage, platform fees or packaging. Someone with GBP 1,400 of sales and GBP 1,200 of costs has made GBP 200, but their gross is GBP 1,400, so they are over the line and have to register. That catches a lot of people who reasonably assumed a GBP 200 profit could not possibly be a tax matter.

It is a tax year figure, not a calendar year figure. The tax year runs 6 April to 5 April. Platform reporting runs on calendar years. So the total on the letter and the total that matters for your return cover different periods and will not match. That is expected, not a discrepancy.

If you do need to register for the 2025 to 2026 tax year, the deadline is 5 October 2026. Missing it is a failure to notify, and the penalty is geared to the tax you owed, so registering late but voluntarily is far better than waiting to be found.

4. Selling your own things is not trading

This is the single most useful thing in this article and it is the thing the crackdown coverage almost never says.

Income tax on trading applies to trading. Selling personal possessions that you bought to use and no longer want is not trading, and it does not become trading because there were a lot of them or because they added up to a decent sum. There is no business, no intention to profit from resale, and generally no taxable trading income.

What distinguishes trading is a pattern: buying in order to sell on, repeating it, making or modifying goods to sell, running it in a businesslike way. HMRC looks at the substance of the activity rather than the total on a platform statement.

So a person who sells 74 items of their own old clothing and receives GBP 1,850 will be reported by the platform on both triggers, and may owe absolutely nothing. A person who buys job lots at car boot sales and lists them at a markup is trading from their first sale, even if the numbers are much smaller.

If you are genuinely unsure which you are, HMRC publishes a free additional income checker that walks through it, and the Low Incomes Tax Reform Group explains the borderline cases without trying to sell you anything.

Worked example: two sellers in Taunton

Rachel spent 2026 clearing out her own wardrobe on a selling app. 74 items, GBP 1,850 received.

She passes both reporting triggers: 74 is more than 30, and GBP 1,850 is over the euro threshold. The platform reports her and tells her it has. If a nudge letter arrives, it is because the data showed up and her tax return did not.

What she owes: nothing. These were her own clothes, bought to wear, sold second hand for less than she paid. That is not trading. There is no trading income, so the GBP 1,000 allowance is not even in play. She does not need to register. What she does need is a record: a note of what the items were and that they were her own used possessions, in case she is asked.

Dev, her neighbour, buys job lots at car boot sales and resells them. In 2025 to 2026 he took GBP 1,200 and spent GBP 900 on stock, postage and fees. Profit GBP 300.

He is well under either reporting trigger on value if his sale count stays low, so he may not be reported at all. He is still trading, and his gross is GBP 1,200, over the GBP 1,000 allowance, so he must register by 5 October 2026. Less visible than Rachel and considerably more obliged.

Which method leaves him better off. He can either claim the trading allowance or deduct actual expenses, whichever suits, but not both. Claiming the allowance: GBP 1,200 minus GBP 1,000 leaves GBP 200 taxable, which at the 20% basic rate is GBP 40. Deducting real costs: GBP 1,200 minus GBP 900 leaves GBP 300 taxable, which is GBP 60. The allowance is the better choice here by GBP 20.

The point of the pair: the person who got the letter owes nothing and the person who did not get one owes something. Reporting and liability are not the same thing and they do not track each other.

5. The honest verdict

If a platform notification or a nudge letter has landed, do not panic and do not ignore it. Those are both the wrong response.

Work through three questions in order. Was I trading, or selling my own used things? If the latter, you are almost certainly done, and a short written note of what you sold is enough. If I was trading, what was my gross? Not profit, gross. Under GBP 1,000 and the allowance covers it. If over, register by 5 October 2026, and then work out whether the allowance or real expenses gives the lower bill.

Where this genuinely bites is the person with a modest, real trading profit and a gross above GBP 1,000. Dev is filing a tax return over GBP 40 of tax. That is a compliance burden out of all proportion to the money, and the Chartered Institute of Taxation has said as much publicly. It is the system working as designed rather than anything going wrong, and it is worth knowing before you scale up a hobby.

What I would not do is pay anyone to handle this at these numbers. HMRC checker is free, the registration is free, and the Low Incomes Tax Reform Group guidance is free and better than most of what is sold.

Frequently asked

I got a nudge letter but I only sold my own things. What do I do? Do not ignore it. Respond, explain that the sales were personal possessions rather than trading, and keep a record of what they were. HMRC additional income checker will confirm your position.

Does the GBP 1,000 include postage the buyer paid? Gross means the money coming in, so treat postage received as part of it rather than assuming it is excluded, and check the trading allowance guidance for your situation.

I drive for a delivery app. Am I reported? Yes, from the first payment. Services platforms have no reporting threshold at all.

What if I am employed and this is a sideline? The GBP 1,000 trading allowance applies to the trading income regardless of your job. If you go over it you may need to register even though your employer already handles PAYE on your wages.
