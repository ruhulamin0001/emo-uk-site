You Can Still Get a Court Fee Back Three Months After You Have Paid It

Most people who qualify for help with court and tribunal fees never find out, because they assume it is only for people on benefits and they assume it has to be done first. Neither is true. The income test is a specific monthly figure, and there is a three month window to claim a fee back after you have already handed the money over.

The short version

  • You can apply before or after you pay the fee. To get money back you must have paid it in the last 3 months and have been eligible when you paid.
  • The income limit is £1,420 or less if you are single, £2,130 or less if you have a partner. Add £425 for each child aged 0 to 13 and £710 for each child aged 14 and over.
  • Savings are usually capped at £4,250, including a partner's. That rises with the size of the fee, up to £16,000 where the fee is over £7,000.
  • If you or your partner are 66 or older, the savings limit is £16,000 whatever the fee is.
  • Certain benefits qualify you on income: income-based JSA, income-related ESA, Income Support, Universal Credit where you earn under £6,000 a year, and Pension Credit (Guarantee Credit). The savings test still applies.
  • One application per fee. If you have more than one fee to pay, you apply separately for each.

1. Who qualifies, in numbers

Eligibility for help with court and tribunal fees turns on three things: how much you have in savings, which benefits you get, and your income. All three are published thresholds rather than a judgement call.

The income test. When you apply you report either your income in the last calendar month, or your average income over the last three calendar months, whichever is lower. Income means anything you earn before tax plus any other payments you receive.

You can get money off your fee if that reported income is:

  • £1,420 or less if you are single
  • £2,130 or less if you have a partner

Each child raises the ceiling. Add £425 for each child aged 0 to 13, and £710 for each child aged 14 and over. GOV.UK's own example: a couple with two children aged 7 and 5 can have reported income of up to £2,980.

The benefits route. You qualify on income if you claim income-based Jobseeker's Allowance, income-related Employment and Support Allowance, Income Support, Universal Credit where you earn less than £6,000 a year, or Pension Credit (Guarantee Credit). You still have to pass the savings test.

If you earn more than the limits, you may still get some money off rather than all of it, depending on the size of the fee. It is not a cliff edge.

2. The savings test, which is the one that fails people

The savings limit is not one number. It scales with the fee.

If you and your partner are 65 or younger, the maximum savings you can hold depends on how large the fee is. You can have up to £4,250 in savings if the fee is £1,420 or less, and up to £16,000 if the fee is over £7,000. GOV.UK notes that most court and tribunal fees are less than £1,420, which means in practice most applicants are working to the £4,250 figure.

If you or your partner are 66 or older, the limit is £16,000 whatever the fee is. That is a large difference and it is purely age based.

Your partner's savings count as yours for this test. That is the single most common reason an otherwise eligible application fails.

3. The sequencing trap

The route you use to apply for help has to match the route you use to make the claim. Get them the wrong way round and you create work for yourself.

Applying online. Apply for help with fees online before you make your court application. You get a help with fees reference number, and you enter that number when you make the claim.

Applying by post. Download and complete form EX160 and submit it at the same time as your court application, sending both to the address on the court application form. For a money claim by post that means form EX160 and form N1 going together to the Civil National Business Centre in Northampton.

The practical rule is: do not start an online claim, get to the payment page, and only then go looking for help with fees. Sort the reference number first. If you are already mid-claim, that is when people give up and pay a fee they did not owe, which brings us to the part most people do not know about.

Two exceptions worth flagging. If you need help with Court of Protection fees you cannot use this service at all and must use form COP44A instead. And if you cannot get online or do not feel confident doing so, We Are Group provides free help with the online application on 03300 160 051.

4. Worked example: Marcus in Swansea

Marcus wants to bring a money claim for £2,400 against a builder. The court fee for a claim between £1,500.01 and £3,000 is £115.

He works part time and his pay last month was £1,310. He has a partner who is not working and one child aged 9. Their savings, combined, are £3,100.

Income. With a partner his ceiling is £2,130, plus £425 for a child aged 0 to 13, so £2,555. His reported income has to include his partner's income, and their combined figure is what gets tested. On his £1,310 alone they are comfortably inside it.

Savings. The fee is £115, which is well under £1,420, so the applicable savings limit is £4,250. At £3,100 they are inside it.

Result. He applies for help with fees online first, gets a reference number, and then makes his claim online quoting it. His court fee is £115 and he expects it to be covered.

Now the version where he does it wrong. He starts the claim, pays the £115 on a card because he did not know help existed, and finds out a fortnight later. He is not stuck. He paid within the last three months and he was eligible when he paid, so he can apply for the money back. It is slower and it is more paperwork, but the £115 is not gone.

Worth saying: if Marcus is weighing up whether to bring the claim at all, the fee is only the entry price. Court mediation is free, and there are further fees later if the case goes to a hearing or a judgment has to be enforced. 5. Claiming back a fee you have already paid

You can apply for money off a court or tribunal fee before or after you pay it. To get money back after the event, both of these must be true:

  • you paid the fee in the last 3 months
  • you were eligible for help at the time you paid it

If you are applying for money off a fee you have already paid, send the help with fees application to the court or tribunal you paid the fee to, rather than to a central address.

Three months is not long and nobody reminds you. If you have paid a court or tribunal fee since the middle of June and your income is anywhere near the figures above, that is worth half an hour today rather than next month.

The honest verdict

The thresholds are higher than most people assume. £1,420 a month single and £2,130 with a partner, before you add £425 or £710 per child, is not a destitution test. Plenty of working households on modest wages sit inside it and never apply.

The two things that actually decide it are savings, where a partner's money counts and the usual ceiling is £4,250, and sequencing, where applying online before you start the claim saves a lot of grief. And if you have already paid, check the date. Three months from payment is a real window and it is the single most overlooked part of this scheme.

Frequently asked

Can I get help with more than one court fee at a time? Yes, but you have to apply separately for each fee. GOV.UK states that if you have more than one fee to pay you must apply for help with each one individually.

Does my partner's income and savings count? Yes. Both the income limits and the savings limits are assessed on you and your partner together, which is why the couple thresholds are higher than the single ones.

What if I earn slightly more than the limit? You may still get some money off your fee rather than the whole thing. How much depends on the size of the fee. It is a taper, not a cut-off.

What form do I need if I apply by post? Form EX160. Submit it at the same time as your court application and send both to the address on the court application. Further guidance is in EX160A.
