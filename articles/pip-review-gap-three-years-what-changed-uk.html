Your PIP Review Could Come Every Nine Months. It Is Moving to Three Years, and That Cuts Both Ways

DWP is extending the gap between Personal Independence Payment award reviews for most claimants aged 25 and over, and at the same time putting five times as many assessments back in a room with a person. The two halves of that announcement pull in opposite directions, and almost every write-up covers one and skips the other.

The short version

  • The review gap is going up. For the majority of PIP claimants aged 25 and over, the gap between award reviews moves to a minimum of three years on a new claim, rising to five years at the next review if you remain entitled. DWP says reviews can currently come as soon as nine months apart.
  • Face-to-face assessments go up five times over. PIP assessments done face-to-face rise from 6% in 2024, which was 57,000 of them, to 30% of all assessments.
  • The Work Capability Assessment moves too, from 12% in 2024, which was 74,000, to 30%.
  • DWP corrected its own figure on 11 September 2026. The WCA number was originally published as 13%. It is 12%. The correction is logged on the page's own update history, and the wrong figure is still being repeated.
  • The effective date is April 2026 and the stated saving is £1.9 billion by the end of 2030/31.
  • This is not the Timms Review. DWP says so explicitly. These are operational changes, sitting separately from the review of what PIP is actually for.

1. What DWP has actually announced

On 18 December 2025 the Department for Work and Pensions published a package of welfare measures it says will save £1.9 billion by the end of 2030/31. The page was last updated on 11 September 2026. Two things in it matter to anyone on PIP.

First, the review gap. DWP's own words are that the time between award reviews "can be as short as nine months and most people do not see a change in their award at their review". That is being extended for the majority of PIP claimants aged 25 and over to a minimum of three years for a new claim, rising to five years at their next review if they remain entitled.

Read that carefully, because two qualifiers are doing real work. It is the majority, not everyone. And it is aged 25 and over. If you are under 25, this paragraph is not about you.

Second, assessments move back into rooms. Contracts agreed by the previous government required 80% of assessments to be completed virtually. That is being unwound:

PIP assessments face-to-face: from 6% in 2024, which DWP puts at 57,000 assessments, to 30% of all assessments.

Work Capability Assessments face-to-face: from 12% in 2024, which was 74,000, to 30%.

The stated logic connects the two. Extending the review gap frees up health professionals, and those freed-up professionals do more assessments face-to-face and clear the WCA backlog. Alongside it sit the redeployment of 1,000 work coaches and the Connect to Work programme, which DWP wants to reach 300,000 people by the end of the parliament.

The changes take effect from April 2026, alongside separate changes to Universal Credit.

2. The number DWP corrected on 11 September

This is small and worth knowing anyway, because it tells you something about how these figures travel.

When the release first went out, it said Work Capability Assessments done face-to-face in 2024 were 13%. On 11 September 2026 DWP amended the page. The correct figure is 12%. The change is recorded in the page's own update history, in one line: "Corrected an error: face-to-face assessments for Work Capability Assessment (WCA) in 2024 was 12%, rather than 13%."

One percentage point is not going to change anyone's life. What matters is what the correction does not do, and the answer is anything at all. It changes no rate, no eligibility rule, no date, and nobody's award. If you see a piece written before 11 September quoting 13%, it is out of date rather than wrong-headed. If you see one written after, it was not checked.

3. Why a longer gap cuts both ways

Most coverage of this frames it as good news, and for a lot of people it is. A review is not a neutral administrative event. It is paperwork, it is often an assessment, and for people whose condition is permanent it arrives with the implication that it might not be. DWP's own line is that most people do not see a change at their review, which is a fair admission that a good number of these reviews were achieving very little.

But there is a second edge, and it is the part nobody writes down. A review is also how an award goes up. If your condition deteriorates and you wait for the review to say so, you have just been moved from a possible nine month wait to a possible three year one.

DWP addresses this, though it does not put it in the headline. Its additional information says that if a condition deteriorates or improves, claimants "should report a change of circumstances and ask for their PIP award to be reviewed". That is the mechanism. It has always existed. It becomes considerably more important the moment the automatic review moves three years away.

The same page also commits to keeping adjustments in place for people who cannot attend face-to-face because of their disability or condition, with home visits or alternative channels where appropriate. That is a commitment on a press release, not a rule you can point an assessor at, so it is worth knowing it was said.

4. Worked example: Naseem in Dewsbury

Naseem is 41 and lives in Dewsbury. She has a fluctuating condition and is on the standard rate of the PIP daily living component. Her last award review came round eleven months after the one before it, and nothing changed at it.

Under the old pattern she could reasonably expect the next one inside a year. Under the new one, on a new claim she is looking at a minimum of three years, and if she is still entitled at that point, five years until the one after.

What she gains: roughly two fewer review cycles across the next four years, and the paperwork and worry attached to them.

What she loses: the automatic prompt. In March her condition worsens. Waiting for a review to notice this is now potentially a three year wait. Reporting a change of circumstances is the only route that moves at the speed her condition does.

The arithmetic that actually matters to her is not in the press release. It is the gap between standard and enhanced daily living. If a deterioration would move her between those two rates and she waits three years rather than reporting it, the cost of waiting is that difference, every week, for however long she waits. That is why the boring administrative point above is the useful one.

5. What is worth doing about it

If your condition is stable, do nothing. This is genuinely good news for you and it requires no action. Fewer reviews, same award.

If your condition fluctuates or is progressive, learn the change of circumstances route now. Not when you need it. It is the mechanism that replaces the frequent review, and DWP has explicitly pointed at it. Keep a dated note of changes as they happen rather than reconstructing a year later.

If you cannot attend face-to-face, say so early. With 30% of assessments moving into rooms rather than 6%, the odds of being asked to attend one go up substantially. The adjustment commitment exists. Use it in advance of an appointment rather than on the day.

Do not read this as a signal about the Timms Review. DWP separates them in the release itself, in a sentence written to be quoted: these operational changes "are separate to the Timms Review, which will look at the role of PIP, its assessment and the criteria". Whatever that review concludes about what PIP is for, it is a different question from how often it is checked.

The honest verdict

For a claimant with a stable condition this is a clear improvement, and it should be said plainly rather than hedged. Fewer reviews of awards that mostly do not change is a sensible thing to do, and DWP's own admission that most reviews change nothing is the best argument for it.

The part to hold onto is that the review was doing two jobs, and only one of them was surveillance. It was also the thing that eventually caught a deterioration. Move it three years out and the burden of noticing shifts entirely onto the claimant. That is workable, but only if you know it, which is why the change of circumstances route stops being a footnote and starts being the main mechanism.

And treat the 11 September correction as a reminder rather than a scandal. A department amended its own figure a week ago and the old one is still circulating. On anything that decides money, check the date on the page, not the date on the article.

Frequently asked

Does this mean my current award is being extended? The announcement describes a minimum of three years for a new claim, rising to five at the next review. It does not say existing awards are being retrospectively lengthened, and the effective date given is April 2026. If you are mid-award, check what your own award letter says about your review date rather than assuming this changes it.

Am I now more likely to be called in for a face-to-face assessment? On the published figures, yes. PIP face-to-face assessments are going from 6% of assessments to 30%. That is a five-fold increase in the proportion. DWP says adjustments including home visits remain available where a disability or condition means someone cannot attend.

Does the three year gap apply to everyone? No. DWP's wording is the majority of claimants aged 25 and over. It does not say all, and it does not include under 25s. If you are under 25 or your award has particular conditions attached, this is not a safe assumption to make about your own case.

Is this the Timms Review? No, and DWP says so in the release. These are operational changes to how often awards are reviewed and how assessments are delivered. The Timms Review is looking at the role of PIP itself, its assessment and its criteria. They are running separately.
