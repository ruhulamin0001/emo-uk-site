The Sick Pay Figure Everyone Quotes Only Applies If You Earn Over £154.06 A Week

Statutory Sick Pay is widely reported as £123.25 a week. The rule is actually £123.25 or 80% of your normal weekly earnings, whichever is lower. That single word turns a flat rate into a sliding one, and the crossover point sits at £154.06 a week, a number nobody publishes.

The short version

  • SSP is £123.25 a week or 80% of your normal weekly earnings, whichever is LOWER, paid by your employer for up to 28 weeks.
  • Our calculation of the crossover: £154.06 a week. Earn more than that and you get the flat £123.25. Earn less and you get 80% of what you actually earn.
  • There is no lower earnings limit any more. Eligibility is now just: you are an employee, you have done some work for the employer, and you have been ill for at least one full working day.
  • There are no waiting days. You are paid for all the full days you are off sick that you would normally have worked.
  • 28 weeks is the maximum, which is £3,451 in total at the flat rate, by our own arithmetic.
  • Two jobs can mean SSP from each employer. Each employment is assessed separately.
  • Tell your employer by their deadline, or within 7 days if they have not set one. You can lose SSP for telling them late.

1. The rule, in the government's own words

Almost every article about Statutory Sick Pay leads with a single number, currently £123.25 a week. GOV.UK's own wording is different, and the difference is the whole article:

If you're eligible, you can get £123.25 a week Statutory Sick Pay or 80% of your normal weekly earnings, whichever is lower. It's paid for up to 28 weeks.

The operative words are whichever is lower. SSP is not a flat rate that everybody gets. It is a ceiling of £123.25 sitting on top of an 80% formula.

Your employer works out your normal weekly earnings using your average over an 8-week period. If you started recently and have not yet had 8 weeks of pay, you still qualify; your employer uses what there is.

SSP is paid the same way as your wages, weekly or monthly, and tax and National Insurance are deducted. So the figure that reaches your bank account is lower again than any number in this article.

2. The crossover nobody publishes

If SSP is the lower of a flat £123.25 and 80% of earnings, there must be an earnings level at which the two are equal. Below it you are on the percentage. Above it you are on the flat rate.

The arithmetic is one division. £123.25 divided by 0.80 gives £154.06 a week. That is our own calculation from the government's own rule, not a published figure, and as far as we can find nobody states it plainly.

Who actually sits below £154.06 a week? At the National Living Wage of £12.71 an hour, £154.06 is about 12 hours a week. So this is the world of short part-time contracts, weekend jobs, term-time work and second jobs. It is a large number of people, and every one of them will find the widely quoted £123.25 does not describe what they receive.

The flip side is the genuinely good news buried in the same change. Under the old system, workers earning below the lower earnings limit got nothing at all. Now they get 80% of their earnings. For the lowest-paid part-time workers, that is a payment where previously there was a zero. Both of these things are true at once, and most coverage reports neither.

3. Who qualifies now, and what changed

The current eligibility test on GOV.UK is remarkably short. To qualify for SSP you must:

  • be classed as an employee
  • have done some work for your employer
  • have been ill for at least one full working day

That is the whole list. Two things that were in it for decades are gone.

The lower earnings limit is gone. There is no minimum weekly earnings figure you must clear to be eligible at all. Low earners are inside the system now, receiving 80% rather than nothing.

The waiting days are gone. GOV.UK now says you are paid SSP for all the full days you are off sick that you normally would have worked. There is no unpaid period at the start of a sickness absence. For short illnesses, which are the overwhelming majority, this is the single most valuable change, because it is precisely the short absences that used to fall entirely inside the unpaid window.

You will not qualify if you have already received the maximum 28 weeks, or if you are getting Statutory Maternity Pay. Agency workers may be entitled to SSP, and GOV.UK has separate guidance on how different employment types are treated.

One point worth knowing if you have more than one job: you may get SSP from each employer, because each employment is looked at on its own. That also means the 80% test is applied separately to each, on that job's earnings.

4. Telling your employer, fit notes and linked periods

Tell your employer before the deadline they set, or within 7 days if they have not set one. GOV.UK says plainly that you could lose some of your SSP if you do not tell them in time. This is the most avoidable way to lose money in the whole scheme.

A fit note is needed only after 7 days in a row, including non-working days. So a Thursday-to-Monday absence does not need one, even though it spans a weekend.

Fit notes no longer have to come from a GP. They can be issued by a GP or hospital doctor, registered nurse, occupational therapist, pharmacist or physiotherapist, and can be printed or digital. If your employer agrees, other evidence can be used instead, such as an Allied Health Professional Health and Work Report or a return-to-work plan.

Linked periods. If you have repeated absences, they may count as linked. To be linked they must each last more than one full working day and be 8 weeks or less apart. Linked periods matter because they count towards the same 28 week limit, and you stop being eligible for SSP if a continuous series of linked periods lasts more than 3 years.

Worked example: Deniz in Portsmouth

Deniz works 14 hours a week in a shop at the National Living Wage of £12.71 an hour, so his normal weekly earnings are about £177.94. He is signed off for three weeks.

Which rule applies? £177.94 is above the £154.06 crossover, so he is on the flat rate. His SSP is £123.25 a week, less tax and National Insurance.

What he actually loses. His normal weekly earnings are £177.94 and SSP replaces £123.25 of it, so he is down roughly £54.69 a week before tax, or about £164 over the three weeks.

Now change one thing. Suppose his hours had been cut to 10 a week, giving earnings of £127.10. He is now below the crossover, so he gets 80% of £127.10, which is £101.68, not £123.25. Fewer hours reduces his SSP twice over: less earnings, and a lower replacement rate.

The old system, for comparison. Before the 6 April 2026 changes he would have faced unpaid waiting days at the start of the absence, and on the lower hours he might have been below the lower earnings limit and received nothing at all. Both of those are gone. He is better off than he would have been, and still well short of his normal pay.

5. What SSP is actually worth, and what to do if it is not enough

Some arithmetic to size the thing honestly. At the flat rate, 28 weeks of SSP totals £3,451 gross. Against a full-time week at the National Living Wage, 37.5 hours at £12.71 giving £476.63, SSP replaces about 26% of pay. Both figures are our own calculations.

That is a quarter of a full-time minimum wage. It is not designed to keep a household running and nobody should plan on the basis that it will.

So, the practical steps.

Check your contract first. GOV.UK is explicit that you cannot get less than the statutory amount and you can get more if your employer has an occupational sick pay scheme. Many do, often full pay for a period then half pay. This is the single biggest variable and it is in a document you already have.

Know about form SSP1. If you do not qualify for SSP, your employer must send you form SSP1 within 7 days of your first day off sick. If your SSP is ending, they must send it within 7 days of it ending if that happens unexpectedly, or on or before the beginning of the 23rd week if the end is foreseeable. SSP1 is what you use to support a claim for Universal Credit or Employment and Support Allowance, so a missing SSP1 is a real problem worth chasing.

Look at Universal Credit alongside, not after. UC can be claimed while receiving SSP, because SSP is treated as earnings rather than as a disqualifying benefit. Waiting until SSP runs out at week 28 to start thinking about it is a common and expensive mistake.

If you think the amount is wrong, raise it with your employer first, and if you are still not satisfied, contact the HMRC enquiry line. SSP disputes are an HMRC matter, not an employment tribunal one.

6. The honest verdict

The headline £123.25 is accurate for anyone earning over £154.06 a week and misleading for everyone below it. If you work part time, do the 80% calculation on your own earnings before you assume the flat rate applies to you.

The two changes from 6 April 2026 are genuinely good and genuinely under-reported. No waiting days means short absences are now paid from day one, which is where most sickness actually falls. No lower earnings limit means the lowest-paid part-time workers get something instead of nothing.

What has not changed is the size of it. Twenty-six per cent of a full-time minimum wage, capped at 28 weeks, is a floor to stop people falling, not an income. The most valuable thing you can do today, while you are well, is find out whether your employer has an occupational sick pay scheme, because that number matters far more to you than anything in the statute.

Frequently asked

Do I get SSP for weekends? Only for days you would normally have worked. SSP is paid for the full days you are off sick that were working days for you. Weekends do count when you work them, and they count towards the 7 days that trigger the need for a fit note, which is a separate calculation.

Is SSP taxed? Yes. GOV.UK states that tax and National Insurance are deducted, because SSP is paid by your employer through payroll in the same way as wages.

What happens after 28 weeks? SSP stops. Your employer must give you form SSP1, on or before the beginning of the 23rd week if the end is foreseeable, so that you have it in time to apply for Universal Credit or Employment and Support Allowance without a gap.

Can I get SSP if I am self-employed? No. SSP is only for employees, because it is paid by an employer. Self-employed people generally need to look at Employment and Support Allowance or Universal Credit instead, or at income protection insurance bought privately.
