Why Two Landlords on the Same Street Pay £65 and £140 for the Same Certificate

The annual landlord gas safety check is the one legal obligation in this series that costs less than a tank of fuel. It is also the one most often confused with a boiler service, which is a different job at a different price. Here is what regulation 36 actually requires, and why the quotes vary so much.

The short version

  • Regulation 36 of the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 requires an annual check of every gas appliance and flue in a let property, within 12 months of the last one.
  • A check is not a service. The check confirms the appliance is safe. A service cleans and maintains it. Many engineers quote the two together, and the certificate alone is cheaper.
  • The record must reach the tenant within 28 days of the check, and a new tenant before they move in. Records are kept for two years.
  • Since 2018 you can do the check up to two months early without losing your anniversary date, which removes almost every scheduling excuse for going over.
  • Price tracks appliance count. One combi boiler is the cheap end. A boiler, a gas hob and a gas fire is three appliances and roughly double.

1. What regulation 36 actually says

The Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 are the governing law, and regulation 36 is the landlord duty. It requires that every gas appliance and flue provided for a tenant's use is checked for safety within 12 months of being installed, and at intervals of not more than 12 months thereafter.

The check must be carried out by a Gas Safe registered engineer. Not a plumber, not a handyman, not somebody who used to be CORGI registered. The register is public and searchable, and an engineer carries an ID card with the specific appliance types they are qualified for on the back.

Three further duties sit with it.

  • Give a copy of the record to existing tenants within 28 days of the check.
  • Give it to a new tenant before they occupy the property.
  • Keep the record for two years, meaning until two further checks have been carried out.

The duty covers appliances the landlord provides. A tenant's own gas cooker sits outside it, though the installation and pipework serving it does not, and the flue in the same room still must be checked. That distinction catches people out.

2. Check, service, and why the quotes differ

This is the heart of the pricing question, and it is genuinely simple once stated plainly.

A gas safety check asks one question of each appliance: is it safe to use. The engineer verifies it is burning correctly, that it is supplying gas at the right operating pressure, that harmful combustion products are being removed by an adequate flue, that ventilation is sufficient, and that all safety devices work. That is it. Half an hour to an hour for a single appliance.

A boiler service is maintenance. Stripping down, cleaning the heat exchanger and burner, checking seals, replacing what is worn. It takes longer, uses parts, and extends the life of the appliance. It is not a legal requirement in a rented property, though many manufacturers require it annually to keep a warranty alive, which is a separate and often decisive reason to do it.

When one landlord pays £65 and the neighbour pays £140, the usual explanations are: the second quote bundles a service, or the second property has three gas appliances against one, or the second engineer priced in a mid-afternoon appointment to suit a tenant. Ask any quote to state how many appliances it covers and whether a service is included, and almost every mysterious price gap resolves.

3. What the check does not cover, and the three gaps that catch landlords

The certificate feels comprehensive because it is a legal document with a legal name. It is narrower than it looks, and three gaps in particular catch otherwise careful landlords.

Gap one: the tenant's own appliances. If the tenant brought their own gas cooker, that appliance is not yours to check under regulation 36. What remains yours is the installation pipework, the connection point and the flue or ventilation serving the room. An engineer who writes "tenant's own appliance, not inspected" has done the right thing, and a landlord who reads that as "nothing here is my problem" has not.

Gap two: carbon monoxide alarms. These are a separate legal duty in England under the Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarm (England) Regulations 2015 as amended in 2022, which require a carbon monoxide alarm in any room used as living accommodation containing a fixed combustion appliance, excluding gas cookers. That duty does not disappear because the gas check passed, and it applies from the first day of a tenancy. Scotland and Wales have their own, in places stricter, requirements.

Gap three: the gap between checks. A certificate is a snapshot on a single morning. An appliance that fails in month four is not covered by a certificate issued in month one, and a tenant reporting a smell of gas or a yellow flame is an emergency regardless of what the paperwork says. The national gas emergency number is 0800 111 999 and it is free from any phone.

None of these three gaps is expensive to close. All three are expensive to discover after something has gone wrong.

The fourth row deserves scepticism. A cover plan is insurance, and like all insurance it is priced to make money. It suits a landlord with several properties and no time; it is poor value for one modern boiler under warranty.

Worked example: the Farrell household, Swindon

A three-bed let with a combi boiler, a gas hob and a decorative gas fire in the front room. Three appliances.

The first quote was £155, described only as "landlord certificate and service". Asked to itemise, it came back as £115 for the three-appliance safety check and £40 for the boiler service.

The boiler was six years old and out of warranty. The service was therefore optional. They kept it anyway, because a service on a six-year-old boiler is cheap insurance against a January breakdown, but they made the decision knowingly rather than paying a bundled figure.

They then asked a second question: does the gas fire still earn its keep. It was used perhaps twice a year, and it was adding roughly £35 a year to the check. Capping it off permanently, at a one-off cost of about £80, pays for itself in 2.3 years and removes an appliance from every future check.

That is the only payback calculation available in this article, and it is a real one.

5. The two-month rule that solves the scheduling problem

Before 2018 the anniversary was rigid, so a landlord who got the check done early lost time from the next cycle and gradually walked the date earlier every year. The Gas Safety (Installation and Use) (Amendment) Regulations 2018 fixed this.

You may now carry out the check up to two months before the due date and keep the original anniversary, as if it had been done exactly twelve months after the previous one. In practice that means you have a sixty-day window rather than a single day, which removes essentially every legitimate reason for a lapse.

Use it deliberately. Book the check in the quiet part of the engineer's year rather than the fortnight when everybody's boiler has just failed, and you will pay less and wait less.

6. The honest verdict

This is a small, fixed, unavoidable cost, and the only real decisions are how many appliances you are carrying and whether you want a service alongside the check.

Count your gas appliances. If one of them is a decorative fire nobody uses, capping it off is a genuine saving that repeats every year, and the sum above shows it paying for itself in around two years.

Use the two-month early window and book off-season. And insist that any quote states the appliance count and whether a service is included, because that single question explains almost every price difference you will be quoted.

Frequently asked

Is a gas safety certificate needed for an owner-occupied home? No. Regulation 36 applies to let property. Homeowners are strongly advised to have appliances checked annually but are under no legal duty to do so.

What is the difference between a CP12 and a gas safety record? None in substance. CP12 was the old CORGI form number and the name stuck. The legal requirement is for a record containing specified information, whatever the form is called.

Can I use the same engineer for the check and any repairs? Yes, and it is usually cheaper. Be aware of the obvious incentive though: an engineer who finds a fault and quotes for it is not automatically wrong, but a second opinion on anything expensive costs little.

What if the tenant will not let the engineer in? Take and document all reasonable steps: written requests, attempted appointments, and a clear explanation of why access is needed. HSE guidance recognises that a landlord who has genuinely tried and kept records is in a very different position from one who has not.
