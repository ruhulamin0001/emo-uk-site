A 5kWh Battery Costs About £4,600 And Earns Only The Gap Between Two Rates. For Most Houses That Is A 27 Year Payback

The Energy Saving Trust publishes solar payback figures with and without an export tariff. It does not publish a payback for a battery on its own. That absence is the most useful thing on the subject, and this page explains why, then gives you the sum to run with your own numbers.

The short version

  • A 5kWh battery system costs around £4,600. Energy Saving Trust figure, updated 19 August 2026, within a range of £1,500 to £10,000. It also notes a typical home system might be 10 kWh.
  • A battery does not generate anything. It moves a kWh from when it is cheap to when it is dear. Its entire income is the gap between the rate you avoid paying and the rate you gave up receiving.
  • At a 15p export rate the gap is 11.32p a kWh. Import is capped at 26.32p from 1 October 2026. Cycle 5 kWh a day for 300 days and you earn £169.80 a year. £4,600 divided by that is 27 years.
  • At a 5p export rate the gap is 21.32p and the payback falls to 14.4 years. Still longer than most battery warranties, which is the number you should ask the installer for before anything else.
  • The honest answer for most houses is do not buy one yet. It changes if you are on a time-of-use tariff with a wide overnight-to-peak spread and you are out during the day, because then the gap is much larger. Section 5 has that version.

1. A battery does not generate anything

This sounds obvious and it is the single most misunderstood thing about home storage. Solar panels make electricity. A battery makes nothing. It takes a kWh you already have and holds it until later.

That means the value of a battery is never the price of a unit of electricity. It is the difference between two prices: the price you would have paid to import that unit at the moment you use it, and the price you would have been paid to export it at the moment you generated it.

If you had no export payment at all, the battery would be worth the full import price, because every stored unit displaces a bought one. But almost everyone with solar has an export arrangement, and that is money you give up the moment you store a unit instead of selling it.

Our solar panel payback page handles the generation side. This page handles the storage side only, and deliberately keeps them separate, because bundling them is how a bad battery decision gets hidden inside a good solar one.

2. The gap, which is the only thing it earns

Ofgem's price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 sets electricity at 26.32p a kWh, with no VAT on electricity until 31 March 2027. That is the import rate to use if you are on a standard capped tariff.

Your export rate is whatever your own supplier pays you under the Smart Export Guarantee. We are not going to print a supplier's rate here. Export rates differ by supplier, change without much notice, and a number typed into a static page is misleading within weeks. Go and read your own tariff, or the published rate of the supplier you are considering, and use that.

The sum is short:

Gap per kWh = import rate minus export rate.
Annual earning = gap, times the kWh you actually cycle through the battery in a year.
Payback in years = the installed cost, divided by the annual earning.

The middle line is the one people get wrong. A 5 kWh battery does not cycle 5 kWh every day of the year. In a British winter your panels may not fill it at all, and in summer you may fill it by ten in the morning and then export the rest anyway. Three hundred full cycles a year is a reasonable working assumption and is more generous than many households achieve.

Read the last row carefully. It is the only one that works, and it works entirely because of the assumed capital cost, not because of anything the battery does. The Energy Saving Trust's range runs from £1,500 to £10,000, so the price you are quoted matters more than every other variable on this page put together.

Note also the perverse result in the first four rows: the better your export rate, the worse your battery payback. A generous export tariff is not a reason to add storage. It is a reason not to.

4. Why the Energy Saving Trust publishes solar paybacks but not battery paybacks

The Energy Saving Trust publishes solar payback tables broken down by region and by whether you have an export tariff. For battery storage it publishes the cost, the range, the typical sizing and practical advice, and it tells you to ask about warranties and guarantees. It does not publish a payback figure.

The reason is structural, not evasive. A solar payback can be calculated from figures a national body can reasonably estimate: the system cost, the generation for a given roof orientation and region, and the split between what you use and what you export. All of those are physical or statistical.

A battery payback depends on your tariff, which the Energy Saving Trust cannot know, and which is the dominant variable. Publish a single number and it would be wrong for almost every reader. So it publishes the inputs and leaves the sum to you, which is the correct call and is the reason this page exists in the form it does.

When you see a battery payback quoted as a single confident figure, ask what import rate, export rate and cycle count it assumes. If the seller cannot tell you all three, they have not done the sum.

5. When a battery does make sense

There are three situations where the arithmetic above changes materially. All three are about the tariff, not the hardware.

A time-of-use tariff with a wide spread. If you can import overnight at a low rate and avoid a much higher peak rate, the battery is arbitraging that spread rather than the solar export gap, and it does not need solar panels at all. Run the same sum with the overnight rate in place of the export rate. A spread of 20p or more changes the answer completely.

You are out all day. The classic bad case for solar without storage is a house that generates at noon and consumes at seven. If your daytime consumption is near zero, almost all your generation is exported at the low rate, and the battery has a large gap to work on every single day. This is the household where the 300 cycle assumption is realistic rather than optimistic.

You need backup power. If your supply is unreliable, or you have medical equipment, a battery has a value that is not measured in pence per kWh at all. That is a legitimate reason to buy one and this page will not argue with it. Just do not pretend the purchase is a financial one. Check specifically that the model you are buying supports islanding, because many home batteries shut down in a power cut for safety and give you no backup at all.

Outside those three cases, the honest advice is to wait. Battery prices have been falling, the £1,500 end of the Energy Saving Trust's range is where the sums start to work, and there is no penalty for adding storage to an existing solar installation later.

6. Worked example: Rory in Inverness

Rory has a 4 kWp solar array already installed and an export arrangement paying him 12p a kWh. He is being quoted £5,200 for a 5kWh battery. He works from home three days a week.

Gap: 26.32p import minus 12p export is 14.32p a kWh.

Cycles: because he is home three days a week, he already uses a good share of his generation directly. He estimates the battery would genuinely cycle about 3.5 kWh a day, and only for about 220 usable days given Scottish winter generation. That is 770 kWh a year, not 1,500.

Earning: 770 kWh at 14.32p is £110.26 a year.

Payback: £5,200 divided by £110.26 is 47 years. The warranty on the battery is ten.

Rory's answer is no, and it is not close. The three things working against him are all real: a decent export rate, so the gap is small; high daytime occupancy, so he is already using the cheap electricity directly; and a northern latitude, so fewer usable days.

What would change it is a time-of-use tariff. If he could import overnight at 7p and avoid a 26.32p day rate, his gap becomes 19.32p and he could cycle the battery every night of the year regardless of sunshine. 5 kWh times 350 nights is 1,750 kWh at 19.32p, which is £338.10 a year, and the payback drops to 15.4 years. Better, still longer than the warranty, and still not a yes.

7. The honest verdict

Do not buy a home battery for the money. On the Energy Saving Trust's own cost figure and the October 2026 capped import rate, a typical household gets a payback somewhere between fourteen and twenty-seven years, which is longer than the warranty on the thing you are buying. The two circumstances that change the answer are a time-of-use tariff with a spread above about 20p, and a house that is empty all day. If neither applies, put the £4,600 into insulation, where the paybacks on this site run from eight months to eleven years, and revisit storage when you are quoted a price near the bottom of the Energy Saving Trust's range.

Frequently asked

How big a battery do I need? The Energy Saving Trust says battery storage capacity is measured in kilowatt hours and that a typical home system might be 10 kWh. Bigger is not better here: a battery you cannot fill or cannot empty earns nothing on the unused capacity, and you have paid for it.

What warranty should I expect? The Energy Saving Trust's advice is simply to remember to ask about any warranties or guarantees for your battery and its installation. Ask for it in years and in cycles, and ask what capacity is guaranteed at the end of the term, because batteries degrade rather than fail outright.

Does a battery work in a power cut? Not automatically. Many home batteries disconnect during an outage for the safety of anyone working on the network. If backup matters to you, ask the installer specifically about islanding or backup circuits, and expect it to cost more.

Will my export payments stop if I fit a battery? No, but they will fall, because you will be exporting less. That reduction is exactly the cost side of the gap calculation in section 2, and it is the part sellers most often leave out.
