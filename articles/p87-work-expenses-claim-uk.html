Your Employer Pays 25p. HMRC Says 55p. The Difference Is Yours to Claim

The approved mileage rate rose to 55p in April, and no employer was obliged to match it. The gap between what you are paid and what HMRC allows is a tax relief claim that can be backdated four years, and most people entitled to it have never made one.

The short version

  • The approved mileage rate rose to 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles in the 2026 to 2027 tax year, up from 45p. After 10,000 miles it stays at 25p.
  • If your employer pays you less than that, you can claim relief on the shortfall. This is the single biggest P87 claim most people are entitled to and never make.
  • Relief is at your tax rate, not the full amount. A £1,500 shortfall is worth £300 to a basic rate taxpayer and £600 to a higher rate taxpayer.
  • You can claim for the current tax year and the four previous ones, so a first claim can cover five years at once.
  • Commuting does not count. Only travel to a temporary workplace or between workplaces, and HMRC now wants start and end postcodes for every journey.

1. The rate went up, and employers did not have to follow

HMRC’s approved mileage rates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year are:

VehicleRate per business mile, 2026/27Rate before 6 April 2026Change
Cars and goods vehicles, first 10,000 miles55p45pup 10p
Cars and goods vehicles, after 10,000 miles25p25pno change
Motorcycles24p24pno change
Bicycles20p20pno change

Here is the part that creates the claim. These are the rates an employer may pay tax-free. They are not rates an employer must pay. Plenty of employers are still paying 45p, and some pay considerably less.

Where your employer pays less than the approved rate, the difference is called Mileage Allowance Relief, and you can claim tax relief on it. Where your employer pays more, the excess is taxable, which is the reason most employers stop exactly at the approved figure.

The rate rising by 10p did not put 10p in anyone’s pocket. It widened the gap between what employers pay and what HMRC will give relief on, and that gap is the claim.

2. What the shortfall is worth

Relief is not a refund of the shortfall. You get back the tax you would have paid on it, at your marginal rate.

The five year column is the one that makes people sit up. Because you can claim for the current tax year and the four previous ones, a first-time claimant on the fourth row is looking at roughly £1,500 at basic rate, against a form that takes an evening once the mileage logs are together.

The five year figures are deliberately simplified: the rate was 45p rather than 55p before 6 April 2026, so earlier years produce a smaller shortfall where the employer paid 45p. For anyone whose employer pays 25p or nothing, the earlier years are still substantial.

3. What counts, and what absolutely does not

The rule that disqualifies most claims is simple: travelling to and from your normal place of work does not count, unless it is a temporary workplace.

What does count:

  • Travel between two workplaces on the same day.
  • Travel to a temporary workplace, meaning somewhere you attend for a limited duration or a temporary purpose.
  • Client visits, site visits, deliveries and collections made for work.

Two further limits worth knowing. You cannot claim separately for fuel, electricity, vehicle tax, MOTs or repairs, because the approved rate is designed to cover the whole cost of owning and running the vehicle. And you cannot get back more tax than you paid in the year, so if you paid no tax there is no relief.

If you use a company car the position is different: you claim on the actual cost of fuel or electricity for business trips, not the approved mileage rate, and you need records of what you spent.

4. How to claim without it being rejected

HMRC has tightened the evidence requirements, and this is where claims fail. When you claim for vehicle use you must send copies of your mileage logs, and each log must include:

  1. The reason for every journey. Not “client visit” on twenty rows. The actual reason.
  2. The postcode for the start point of every journey.
  3. The postcode for the end point of every journey.

If you are claiming across more than one employment, you need a separate log for each.

Then the mechanics. Add up mileage by vehicle type for each tax year, subtract anything your employer paid you towards costs, and claim the difference. If the claim is for the current year HMRC usually adjusts your tax code so you pay less tax as you go. For earlier years it either adjusts the code or issues a refund. If you complete a Self Assessment return you must claim through the return instead, not on a P87.

One warning worth repeating: you do not need to pay anyone a percentage to do this. It is a free claim on a government service, and refund agents typically take a substantial cut of money you would have received anyway.

Worked example: Bethan in Swansea

Bethan is a community nurse. She drives 7,000 business miles a year between patient visits and her employer pays her 25p a mile.

What she is paid. 7,000 at 25p is £1,750.

What HMRC approves. 7,000 at 55p is £3,850.

The shortfall. £2,100 a year.

What the relief is worth. She is a basic rate taxpayer, so 20% of £2,100 is £420 a year.

Backdating. She has never claimed. She can go back four years as well as the current one. The earlier years used the 45p rate, so her shortfall in those years was 7,000 at 20p, or £1,400, worth £280 each. Four earlier years at £280 plus this year at £420 comes to roughly £1,540.

What it cost her. An evening reconstructing mileage from her patient visit records, which already carried postcodes, and one online claim. She kept every penny, because she did not use a refund agent.

The bit she nearly got wrong. Her first attempt included the drive from home to her base at the start of each shift. That is ordinary commuting and is not claimable. Stripping it out reduced the claim slightly and made it far more likely to be accepted.

5. Frequently asked

Is the form still called a P87? The claim is usually made through HMRC’s online service for employment expenses. The postal form still exists, and a claim by post or phone is possible, but online is faster.

What if my employer pays exactly 55p? Then there is no shortfall and nothing to claim. That is the correct outcome, not a missed opportunity.

Can I claim if I am self-employed? Not on a P87. Self-employed people claim mileage as a business expense through Self Assessment instead.

How far back can I really go? The current tax year plus the four previous ones, provided you were eligible and paid tax in each of those years.

Does a claim change my tax code permanently? HMRC may carry an estimate forward into your code. If your mileage changes you must tell them, or you will end up owing the difference.

6. The honest verdict

If you drive for work and your employer pays less than 55p a mile for the first 10,000 miles, you have a claim, and it is almost certainly the largest employment expense claim available to you. At 5,000 miles on a 25p allowance it is worth £300 a year at basic rate and £1,500 across five years.

Do it yourself through HMRC’s online service. It is free, and a refund agent will take a share of money that was always coming to you.

Spend the effort on the mileage log rather than the form. Reason, start postcode, end postcode, every journey, commuting excluded. That is what decides whether the claim is paid.
