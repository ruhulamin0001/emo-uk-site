A Full Week on a Jury Pays £324.75. The Same Week on the Minimum Wage Pays £476.63

Jury service is compulsory and it does not pay a wage. What it does is reimburse a capped amount for the earnings you lose, and for anyone on an ordinary full time salary that cap sits below the legal minimum for the same hours. The number that changes everything is day eleven.

The short version

  • You are not paid for jury service. You claim back a capped amount for lost earnings. GOV.UK is explicit about that distinction.
  • First 10 days: up to £64.95 a day if you are at court more than 4 hours, and £32.47 if it is 4 hours or less.
  • After 10 working days it doubles to £129.91 and £64.95 respectively.
  • Food and drink: £5.71 a day, rising to £12.17 if you are there over 10 hours.
  • Travel by car is 31.4p a mile, bicycle 9.6p, motorcycle 31.4p, plus 4.2p for one passenger and 3.2p for each extra. Bus, underground and standard class train are the cost of the ticket.
  • Your employer chooses whether to pay you. They must give you the time off, but paying you during it is their decision.
  • Benefits continue for the first 8 weeks. After that the court gives you a loss of earnings form for your benefit office.

1. What you can actually claim

GOV.UK opens the page with the sentence that sets the whole thing up: "You will not be paid for doing jury service, but you can claim some money back if your earnings are affected."

That is not a wage. It is a capped reimbursement of a loss you have to demonstrate. The caps are:

First 10 days of jury service

More than 4 hours at court: up to £64.95 a day

4 hours or less: up to £32.47 a day

After 10 working days

More than 4 hours at court: up to £129.91 a day

4 hours or less: up to £64.95 a day

Note the word up to. The cap is not an entitlement. It covers loss of earnings and the cost of any care or childcare outside your usual arrangements, together, within the same daily limit. If you lose £40 of pay and spend £30 on childcare on the same day, that is £70 of cost against a £64.95 ceiling.

Food and drink is a separate allowance: £5.71 a day up to and including 10 hours at court, and £12.17 if you are there over 10 hours.

Travel is also separate and is paid at published rates: the cost of the ticket for bus or underground, standard class return for train, 31.4p a mile for a car or motorcycle, 9.6p a mile for a bicycle, plus 4.2p a mile for carrying one other juror and 3.2p a mile for each additional passenger. Taxis are paid at the fare but you must ask the court's permission first. Check whether your court pays for parking, because that is flagged as a per court question rather than a national rule.

2. The cap as an hourly rate, which nobody publishes

The daily caps are always quoted as daily figures, which makes them hard to judge. Convert them and the picture is clearer.

Take a standard full time week of 37.5 hours over 5 days, which is 7.5 hours a day. A juror at court more than 4 hours a day claims the full £64.95.

£64.95 a day x 5 days = £324.75 a week.

£324.75 over 37.5 hours = £8.66 an hour.

The National Living Wage from April 2026 is £12.71 an hour, which over the same 37.5 hours is £476.63 a week.

The gap is £151.88 a week, or about 32% of the minimum wage equivalent.

Two caveats so this is not misread. This is not an illegal underpayment. Minimum wage law governs work for an employer. Jury service is a civic duty and the court allowance is not wages, so the comparison is a yardstick rather than a legal claim.

And the cap only bites if you actually lose that much. If your employer pays you in full while you serve, you have lost nothing, you claim nothing for loss of earnings, and none of this arithmetic applies to you. The people it applies to are those whose employers do not pay, and the self employed.

What the conversion does show is who this system quietly costs the most. Someone on £8.66 an hour or less loses nothing at all. Someone on £20 an hour loses more than half their pay. The cap is flat, so the loss rises with your earnings, and there is no relief above the ceiling.

3. Why day eleven changes the whole calculation

The doubling after 10 working days is the most important number on the page and it is buried under a subheading.

Day 1 to 10: £64.95 a day, so £8.66 an hour on a 7.5 hour day.

Day 11 onwards: £129.91 a day, so £17.32 an hour on the same day.

That second figure is comfortably above the National Living Wage. So a long trial is financially very different from a short one, and not in the direction people assume. The cheap part for the state, and the expensive part for you, is the first two weeks.

Most jury service is around two weeks, which means most jurors spend their entire service inside the lower band and never reach the doubling. If you are called and the trial is estimated at eight or nine days, you are in the worst case: long enough to lose real money, short enough never to reach the higher rate.

Note the wording carefully. It is 10 working days, not 10 calendar days, and the higher rate applies once your jury service lasts longer than that.

4. Worked example: Dermot in Warrington, two weeks on a trial

Dermot earns £33,000 a year in a 37.5 hour week, which is about £16.92 an hour and £634.62 a week gross. His employer does not pay during jury service. He drives 14 miles each way to court and is there about 7 hours a day.

Week one, 5 days. He loses £634.62 of pay. He can claim £64.95 x 5 = £324.75. Shortfall: £309.87.

Food. Under 10 hours a day, so £5.71 x 5 = £28.55.

Travel. 28 miles a day at 31.4p is £8.79 a day, so £43.95 for the week. That reimburses a cost he actually incurs, so it is not income.

Week two, 5 more days, still inside the first 10. Same again: another £309.87 shortfall.

Two week total shortfall: £619.74 of gross pay, before considering that the £5.71 food allowance is unlikely to cover lunch near a Crown Court.

Now change one thing. The trial overruns and he sits 15 days. Days 11 to 15 pay £129.91 a day, which is £649.55 for that week against £634.62 of lost pay. That week he is marginally better off than working.

The lesson in that reversal: his worst outcome is a trial that lasts exactly ten days. His best outcome is a long one. Nobody plans jury service around that, but it explains why the two week estimate people are given is precisely the expensive length.

5. Your employer, your benefits, and what you can insist on

Time off is not optional for your employer. Give them a copy of your jury summons. They must let you have the time off. They can ask you to delay your jury service if your absence would have a serious effect on their business, which is a request to the court, not a veto.

Pay during it is optional for your employer. GOV.UK: your employer can choose whether or not to pay you. If they do not, you claim loss of earnings from the court. Some contracts and staff handbooks provide for paid jury leave, so check yours before assuming.

If you are refused time off, you can complain to an employment tribunal. If you are sacked for doing jury service you may be able to claim unfair dismissal.

If you are on benefits, show the summons to your benefit office or work coach as soon as you get it. Financial support including Universal Credit continues for the first 8 weeks. After 8 weeks the court gives you a loss of earnings form to hand in.

Two things the scheme does not do. There is no accommodation allowance mentioned, and travel is covered within England and Wales. And you claim after your jury service ends, not during it, which means the shortfall lands on your bank account first and the reimbursement follows.

The honest verdict

Jury service is a duty rather than a job and it should not be judged as employment. But the money is real and it is worth seeing plainly: for the first ten days the cap works out at £8.66 an hour on a standard full time week, which is below the legal minimum for the same hours, and there is no relief above the ceiling however much you earn.

Who it hurts is predictable. If you earn at or near the minimum wage and your employer does not pay, the cap covers most of the loss. If you earn upwards of £17 an hour, or you are self employed and every day at court is a day not billed, you carry a real cost for doing something you are legally required to do. Two weeks at Dermot's salary is over £600.

Three practical things. Ask your employer in writing whether they pay during jury service, before you assume they do not, because plenty do and it is often in the handbook. Keep every travel receipt and mileage note from day one, since travel and food are paid on top of the earnings cap and are the parts people forget to claim. And if the loss would genuinely damage you, the route is not to complain about the cap but to ask the court to defer, which is a normal request and is decided on the facts.

Frequently asked

Does my employer have to pay me? No. They must give you the time off, but GOV.UK states your employer can choose whether or not to pay you during your service. If they do not, you claim loss of earnings from the court.

What if I am at court for only part of the day? The half day rate applies. For the first 10 days that is £32.47 if you are there 4 hours or less, and £64.95 if you are there more than 4 hours.

Can I claim for childcare? Yes, but not separately. The daily cap covers loss of earnings and the cost of any care or childcare outside your usual arrangements together, within the same limit.

Will my Universal Credit stop? Not for the first 8 weeks. Show your summons to your benefit office or work coach as soon as you get it. After 8 weeks the court gives you a loss of earnings form to pass on.

What can I claim for driving? 31.4p a mile for a car, plus 4.2p a mile if you carry one other juror and 3.2p a mile for each additional passenger. Check with your court about parking.
