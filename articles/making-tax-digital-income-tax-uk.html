HMRC Starts Signing People Up to Digital Tax This Month, Whether They Asked Or Not

Buried in HMRC's own announcement is a number nobody has led with. 570,000 people have signed up to Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. Only 436,000 of them actually filed. And from this month, HMRC begins enrolling the people who have not signed up at all.

The short version

  • From September 2026, HMRC starts signing you up itself. Its press release of 12 August 2026 says it will begin enrolling customers who should be using Making Tax Digital for the 2026/27 tax year but have not signed up. It will happen in stages over the coming months.
  • 570,000 signed up, 436,000 filed. Both figures are in the same HMRC release. The difference is 134,000 people, 23.5% of everyone signed up, who registered and then missed the 7 August deadline anyway.
  • The threshold is turnover, not profit. HMRC's own example: £25,000 of rent plus £27,000 of self-employment is £52,000 of qualifying income. Your expenses do not come off first. This is the single most misunderstood rule in the whole regime.
  • £50,000 applied from 6 April 2026. £30,000 applies from 6 April 2027, and it is judged on your 2025/26 tax return, for a tax year that already ended on 5 April 2026. That decision is already made.
  • There are no penalty points for late quarterly updates in 2026/27. None. Confirmed by HMRC. Points start from 6 April 2027, and four of them cost £200.
  • The Commons Public Accounts Committee put the average setup cost at £330, rising to £460 for smaller businesses and close to £1,000 at the top end.

1. What actually changes this month

On 12 August 2026, HMRC published a press release headlined "436,000 sole traders and landlords make their tax digital". It was written as a good news story, and most coverage repeated it that way.

Two sentences in the same release matter more.

The first: "Over 570,000 customers have now signed up to the service." Set that against the 436,000 who actually sent a quarterly update and you get a gap of 134,000 people. They registered, chose software, connected it to HMRC, and then did not file by the 7 August deadline. That is 23.5% of everyone signed up, and it is our arithmetic on HMRC's own two numbers, not a figure HMRC published.

The second: "From September 2026, HMRC will begin signing up customers who should be using MTD for the 2026 to 2027 tax year but have not yet done so." That is this month. It happens in stages, and HMRC says new guidance was due in late August explaining what to do if you get one of those letters.

Widely reported HMRC estimates put around 780,000 people in scope for April 2026. Take that at face value and roughly 210,000 have not signed up at all. Treat the 780,000 as an estimate rather than a hard count, because HMRC did not repeat it in the August release.

Being signed up by HMRC is not a penalty. It is worse than that in a quieter way: someone else picks the moment, and you find out by post.

If you sign yourself up first, you choose your software, you check your details are right at the start, and you do it in your own time. If you wait, HMRC does it and you catch up afterwards.

2. Whether you are in it, and the turnover trap

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax applies if you are registered for Self Assessment, you have income from self-employment or property or both, and your qualifying income is over the threshold. The dates, from HMRC's guidance last updated 26 March 2026:

  • Over £50,000 in 2024/25, you should have started on 6 April 2026.
  • Over £30,000 in 2025/26, you start on 6 April 2027.
  • Over £20,000 in 2026/27, you start on 6 April 2028.

Now the part that catches people. HMRC defines qualifying income as "your total income from self-employment and property. This is the amount before expenses (also known as turnover)." Its own worked example is £25,000 of rental income plus £27,000 of self-employment income, giving qualifying income of £52,000.

Nothing comes off first. Not your letting agent's fees, not your stock, not your mileage. A landlord grossing £31,000 in rent and clearing £5,000 after costs is over the April 2027 threshold. A trader turning over £33,000 and making £9,000 is over it too.

What does not count is also worth knowing, because it is where the panic usually comes from. Employment income taxed through PAYE does not count. Neither do dividends, including from your own company, nor a State Pension, nor a private pension, nor your share of profit from a partnership. Only self-employment and property turnover.

Two more details HMRC states plainly and almost nobody repeats. If you jointly own a property, only your share counts. And if you have been trading less than a full year, HMRC will annualise the figure, so six months of trading gets doubled.

3. Worked example: the Begum household in Stevenage

Nasima Begum is a freelance graphic designer who also rents out a one bedroom flat. Her 2025/26 figures:

Rental income: £1,200 a month, so £14,400 for the year. Letting agent at 10% £1,440, landlord insurance £320, repairs £900, service charge £1,100. Expenses £3,760, so a property profit of £10,640.

Freelance turnover: £17,400. Software and subscriptions £780, equipment £1,100, travel £620, simplified home working allowance £312. Expenses £2,812, so a trading profit of £14,588.

Qualifying income: £31,800. Actual profit: £25,228.

Nasima is £1,800 over the £30,000 threshold on the measure HMRC uses, and £4,772 under it on the money she actually keeps. She is in Making Tax Digital from 6 April 2027.

Her filing load goes up more than she expects, because quarterly updates are per business, not per person. Self-employment and UK property are two separate businesses. So she sends four updates for the design work, four for the flat, and one tax return: nine submissions a year, against one today.

The thing she cannot do is change it. The threshold for April 2027 is measured on the 2025/26 tax return, and 2025/26 ended on 5 April 2026. The income is already earned. The only lever left is the accuracy of the return she files by 31 January 2027, and HMRC has said it will look at amendments, so a correction counts and a rearrangement does not.

4. What the software actually costs

You cannot do this with a spreadsheet alone and a form on GOV.UK. You need software on HMRC's recognised list that creates digital records, sends quarterly updates and submits your return. HMRC publishes a filter tool for it, and there is no HMRC-built free product.

For the honest number on cost, the best source is not a vendor. It is the Commons Committee of Public Accounts, reporting on 24 November 2023 on HMRC's own 2021 research. It found that HMRC expected the average upfront cost of complying to be £330, that this rose to £460 for businesses with Self Assessment income between £10,000 and £30,000, and that HMRC's highest estimate was close to £1,000. The committee's actual complaint was that HMRC had left £1.5 billion of these transitional costs out of one business case and £640 million out of another.

Those figures are from 2021 research reported in 2023, so treat them as an order of magnitude rather than a quote for 2026. They are still the most independent numbers available, and they are considerably higher than the "a few pounds a month" framing you will see on software landing pages.

Free tiers do exist on HMRC's list, and they are real. They also come with conditions that are easy to miss: a turnover ceiling, a cap on the number of properties, a single income source only, or free for an introductory period and then not. Before you commit, check the specific limit against your own numbers, and check the price after the free period ends. We are not publishing a price table here because these terms change monthly and a stale table is worse than none.

5. Four things in the small print

Quarterly updates are cumulative. Each one runs from 6 April to the end of that quarter, not just the latest three months. HMRC says this outright, and the practical effect is good news: you can correct an earlier mistake in the next update instead of resubmitting.

You must file a nil update. If you had no income and no expenses in the period, you still have to send the update to say so.

Volunteering early costs you the 30-day grace period. This is the one that genuinely surprised us. In your first year under the new penalty rules you get 30 days from the payment due date to pay or set up a plan before late payment penalties start, instead of the usual 15. HMRC's guidance states that you only receive the 30 day period once, and that if you volunteered and are now required to use Making Tax Digital for Income Tax, you will continue to have 15 days. Volunteering burns it. Volunteers also hit a £200 late return penalty at 2 points rather than 4.

One point per deadline, not per business. Nasima's nine submissions do not mean nine ways to be fined. HMRC states you can only get one penalty point per deadline even if you run more than one business and file more than one late update.

The honest verdict

There is nothing to buy today and no reason to rush a software decision this week. The pressure in this story is manufactured by the people selling the software, not by HMRC, and 2026/27 carries no penalty points for late quarterly updates at all.

What is worth doing is fifteen minutes of arithmetic. Take your 2025/26 turnover from self-employment and property, before a single expense, and add it up. If it is over £30,000 you are in from 6 April 2027 and you have seven months to choose software calmly. If it is over £50,000 you were in from April 2026 and HMRC will write to you this autumn if you have not signed up.

If you are under the thresholds, this does not apply to you at all and you can stop reading. That is most people with a side income, and no amount of software marketing changes it.

Frequently asked

Does this replace my Self Assessment tax return? No. HMRC is explicit that quarterly updates are not tax returns and do not replace Self Assessment. The 31 January deadline stands. You will file the return through your software instead of the HMRC website, and you cannot file it until your quarterly updates are in.

I am employed and rent out one flat. Am I affected? Only your rental turnover counts. Your salary is irrelevant to the threshold no matter how large it is. One flat at £1,000 a month is £12,000 of qualifying income, which is under every announced threshold including the £20,000 one for April 2028.

What if my income drops below the threshold later? You cannot leave immediately. HMRC's rule is that qualifying income must fall below the relevant threshold for three tax years in a row before you can opt out.

Can I get out of it entirely? There are exemptions, including for people who are digitally excluded. They are not automatic in every case and some must be applied for. If you are exempt you still file a normal Self Assessment return.
