Twelve Weeks as an Agency Worker, and the Six Week Break That Wipes It Out

The 12 week rule is one of the few employment rights that arrives on a specific, countable date. What almost nobody explains is that three different lists govern the counting, and the difference between a six week gap and a seven week gap is the difference between keeping your progress and starting again.

The short version

  • From day one you already have worker's rights, plus the same access as permanent staff to shared facilities such as a canteen, a workplace creche or mother and baby room, and car parking or transport services.
  • After 12 weeks in the same role you get equal treatment: the same pay as a permanent colleague doing the same job, automatic pension enrolment, and paid annual leave.
  • The 12 weeks do not have to be consecutive. Count from your first day at work.
  • A break of 6 weeks or less PAUSES the clock. So does sickness or injury leave for up to 28 weeks, annual leave, a workplace closure such as Christmas or industrial action, and jury service for up to 28 weeks.
  • Pregnancy, adoption and paternity leave do not pause it, they CONTINUE it. If the leave runs longer than 12 weeks you qualify on your return.
  • A break of MORE than 6 weeks at the same workplace sends you back to zero. So does a new job at a different workplace, or a role at the same workplace that is substantively different.

1. What you have from day one

The 12 week rule gets all the attention, which leaves people assuming they have nothing until it arrives. That is wrong, and it matters, because the day one entitlements are the ones most often quietly withheld.

From your first day, GOV.UK states you have a worker's employment rights: the minimum wage, protection from unlawful deductions, statutory paid holiday, rest breaks and working hour limits, and protection from discrimination.

You also have, from day one, the same right as permanent colleagues to use shared facilities and services. GOV.UK gives three examples:

  • a canteen, or food and drinks machines
  • a workplace creche, or a mother and baby room
  • car parking or transport services, such as a local pick-up service or transport between sites

These are not courtesies. If permanent staff use the canteen at staff prices, or park in the staff car park, an agency worker is entitled to the same from the first shift. It is the most commonly ignored part of the rules and it costs nothing to ask about.

One boundary worth stating plainly. You are an agency worker if you have a contract with an agency but work temporarily for a hirer. You are not one if you simply used an agency to find permanent or fixed term work, in which case you are the hirer's employee and different rules apply.

2. What 12 weeks actually buys

After 12 weeks in the same role you qualify for equal treatment. GOV.UK lists three things:

  • Equal pay. The same pay as a permanent colleague doing the same job.
  • Automatic pension enrolment, subject to the usual earnings and age criteria.
  • Paid annual leave. On the same basis as a comparable permanent employee.

Now the part that stops people being disappointed. Equal treatment covers pay and basic working conditions. It is not becoming the hirer's employee. Twelve weeks gives you no unfair dismissal rights against the hirer, no statutory redundancy rights from the hirer, and does not convert your agency contract into a permanent job.

Twelve weeks changes what you are paid. It does not change who employs you.

In practice nothing happens automatically. The agency should apply it, but the realistic step is to work out your own date, then ask in writing what a directly employed colleague doing your job is paid.

3. Pause, continue, reset: the three lists

Count from your first day at work. You do not need 12 weeks in a row. What a gap does to the clock depends on which of three lists it falls into.

List one, the clock PAUSES. The days do not count, but your progress is kept. GOV.UK lists: a break of 6 weeks or less; leave due to sickness or injury for up to 28 weeks; annual leave you are entitled to; the workplace closing, for example for Christmas or for industrial action; and jury service for up to 28 weeks.

List two, the clock CONTINUES. The one people get backwards. Time off for pregnancy, and up to 26 weeks after childbirth, keeps counting, as does adoption leave and paternity leave. If that leave runs longer than 12 weeks, you qualify on your return.

List three, the clock RESETS TO ZERO. Three triggers. A new job at a different workplace. A break of more than 6 weeks between jobs at the same workplace. Or staying at the same workplace but taking a role that is substantively different.

Substantively different means completely new, different work. GOV.UK says it could be a combination of different skills or new training, a different pay rate, a different location, or different working hours. Days to nights on the same line at the same rate is not obviously a new role. Picking to forklift driving on a new rate with a new qualification probably is.

So six weeks and one day is the difference between keeping eleven weeks of progress and holding none. If a hirer proposes a seven week gap, that is worth a polite question first.

4. Worked example: Aneta in Peterborough

Aneta works through an agency at a Peterborough distribution centre, picking orders.

Weeks 1 to 5. She works five weeks. Clock at 5.

Weeks 6 and 7. Off sick with a back injury. Sickness of up to 28 weeks pauses the clock. Still 5, progress kept.

Weeks 8 to 11. Back at work, four weeks. Clock at 9.

Weeks 12 to 15. The site closes for a four week shutdown. A workplace closure pauses the clock. Still 9.

Weeks 16 to 18. Three more weeks picking. Clock reaches 12. Equal treatment applies: her rate should match a directly employed picker, and pension enrolment and paid annual leave follow.

Now the damaging version. Suppose after week 9 there had been no shutdown, just no shifts for seven weeks. More than six, so the clock does not pause, it resets to zero. Aneta returns with nine weeks of work behind her, no progress, and needs a further 12 from scratch. A third version: she reaches week 11 and is moved to a forklift role on a different rate needing a new qualification. Likely substantively different, so the clock resets without her leaving the building.

The lesson is not that anyone is acting improperly. It is that the date is calculable, and only the worker has any reason to calculate it.

The honest verdict

Keep your own record. A phone note of every week worked, every gap and its length, and any change of role takes a minute a week and is the only evidence on your side.

Use the day one rights straight away, because facilities and parking are worth real money and need no qualifying period. Then work out your 12 week date and, when it arrives, ask in writing what a directly employed colleague doing the same job is paid.

Be clear what this does not do. It does not make you the hirer's employee, and it gives you no unfair dismissal or redundancy rights against the hirer. It changes your pay and basic conditions, which is worth having, and is not a permanent job.

If gaps or role changes look designed to keep resetting the clock, that is what Acas and Citizens Advice exist for, both free.

Frequently asked

Does the 12 week rule apply if I work through an umbrella company? The Agency Workers Regulations turn on whether you are supplied temporarily to work for a hirer, not on how you are paid. Umbrella arrangements are a separate question about pay and tax, and the government has listed regulation of umbrella companies for 2027.

What if the agency says the 12 weeks restarted and I disagree? Ask, in writing, which of the three reasons applies: a different workplace, a break of more than six weeks, or a substantively different role. If it is the last one, ask which of the factors changed. A written answer is useful whatever it says.
