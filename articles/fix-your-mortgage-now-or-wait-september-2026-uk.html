Waiting for Thursday Will Not Get You a Better Fixed Rate, and Here Is Why

The Monetary Policy Committee decides on Thursday 17 September 2026 and a lot of people with a mortgage deal ending soon have decided to sit on their hands until then. It is an understandable instinct and it is usually the wrong one, because a fixed rate is not priced off the rate the Committee sets.

The short version

  • Bank Rate is 3.75%, held on a 6 to 3 vote on 30 July 2026. The next announcement is 12pm on Thursday 17 September 2026.
  • Fixed mortgage rates are priced off what the market expects to happen, not off today's Bank Rate. By the time a decision is announced it is largely already in the price.
  • A tracker or a standard variable rate is the product that moves on the day. If you are on one of those, the decision matters to you directly.
  • Most lenders let you reserve a new rate about six months before your current deal ends, and let you swap to a cheaper one if rates fall before completion. That option costs nothing and removes the guessing.

1. What the 17 September decision actually moves

Bank Rate is 3.75%. It was held there on 30 July 2026 by a 6 to 3 vote, with three members of the Monetary Policy Committee voting for an immediate rise to 4%. The next decision is announced at 12pm on Thursday 17 September 2026, the morning after the August inflation figures land at 7am on 16 September.

Here is the split that matters to you:

  • If you are on a tracker, your rate is contractually linked to Bank Rate. A change on Thursday feeds through to your payment, usually within a month or two depending on your lender's terms.
  • If you are on a standard variable rate, your lender decides whether to pass a change on, and by how much. It is not automatic, though in practice most do pass on rises.
  • If you are on a fix, absolutely nothing happens on Thursday. Your rate is your rate until your deal ends.
  • If you are shopping for a new fix, the decision has already been largely absorbed into the rates being quoted to you today.

That last point is the one that costs people money, so it deserves its own section.

2. Why fixed rates are already priced

A lender offering you a five year fix has to fund that promise for five years. It does that in the money markets, at a price driven by what those markets collectively expect Bank Rate to average over the period. That expectation is a live, continuously updating number.

So by the time 12pm on Thursday arrives, the outcome the market considers most likely is already baked into the fixed rates on offer. If the Committee does what is expected, fixed rates barely twitch. They move when the Committee, or the accompanying language about what comes next, surprises the market. Nobody sitting at a kitchen table can forecast a surprise.

This also cuts the other way, and it is worth being honest about it. If you were hoping a cut on Thursday would suddenly deliver cheaper fixes on Friday, that is not how the mechanism works. A widely expected cut was in the price weeks ago.

3. The free option most borrowers do not use

Most UK lenders will let you reserve a new fixed rate somewhere around six months before your current deal ends. The important part is what happens next: with most lenders, if rates fall between the day you reserve and the day the new deal starts, you can switch to the cheaper product without penalty.

Read that again, because it converts an agonising forecast into a free option. You lock in a ceiling today and keep the upside if rates improve. The cost of using it is an application and some paperwork. The cost of not using it is that you find out what rates are on the day your deal expires, with no room to move.

Two things to check before you rely on it, because terms vary by lender: whether the switch to a lower rate is allowed and up to what point in the process, and whether any product fee is refundable if you do not complete. Ask those two questions in writing.

4. Worked example: Owen and Marta in Chesterfield

Owen and Marta have 180,000 pounds left on their mortgage with 22 years to run. Their two year fix ends in November. They are trying to decide whether to reserve something now or wait to see what happens on Thursday.

Take two rates half a percentage point apart, 4.3% and 4.8%, on a repayment basis over 22 years:

  • At 4.3%, the monthly payment is 1,055.56 pounds.
  • At 4.8%, the monthly payment is 1,105.27 pounds.

The gap is 49.71 pounds a month. Over a year that is 596.52 pounds. Over a five year fix it is 2,982.60 pounds. Those are our calculations on those two illustrative rates, not quotes from any lender.

Now the point. Owen and Marta cannot influence which side of that gap they land on by waiting four days. They can influence it by reserving a rate now, keeping the right to switch down, and shopping more than one lender. The 2,982 pounds is decided by their loan to value band, their credit file and how many lenders they actually ask, not by Thursday lunchtime.

There is one exception worth naming. If their deal does not end for another eight or nine months, they are outside most lenders' reservation windows anyway, and there is nothing to decide yet. That is waiting because the door is not open, which is different from waiting for news.

5. How to actually decide

Work through these in order.

  1. Find your deal end date. It is on your mortgage offer or your annual statement. Everything else follows from it.
  2. If it is within six months, start now. Reserve a rate, confirm in writing whether you can switch down, and diarise the date the reservation expires.
  3. Work out what you would fall onto. Your lender's standard variable rate is the default if you do nothing, and it is almost always the most expensive option on the table. Knowing that number makes the decision less abstract.
  4. Decide the term on your own circumstances, not on a forecast. A longer fix buys certainty and costs flexibility. If you might move, or repay a lump sum, early repayment charges over a long fix are a real cost.
  5. Check the fee against the rate. A lower rate with a 1,499 pound fee can be worse than a higher rate with no fee on a smaller balance. Compare total cost over the fixed period, not the headline rate.

If the payment itself is what is worrying you, the broader squeeze is not only about the mortgage rate. Income tax thresholds have been frozen for years, which quietly raises the tax on every pay rise, and we have set out what frozen tax thresholds are doing to take home pay separately.

The honest verdict

If you are on a tracker or a standard variable rate, Thursday matters to you and it is worth watching. If you are on a fix, nothing happens to you on Thursday at all. If you are choosing a new fix, the decision is already in the price you are being quoted today, and waiting four days to find out is not a strategy.

The lever that genuinely moves the number is the one nobody talks about, because it is dull: reserve a rate inside your lender's window, keep the right to switch to something cheaper, and get quotes from more than one lender. That is worth thousands on a typical balance. The Monetary Policy Committee is not.

Frequently asked

If the Bank of England cuts on 17 September, will fixed mortgage rates fall on 18 September? Not by much, if the cut was expected. Fixed rates are priced off market expectations for the whole fixed period, and an anticipated move is already reflected. Rates move most when the decision or the accompanying guidance surprises the market.

How early can I lock in a new fixed rate? Most UK lenders allow you to reserve a new rate roughly six months before your existing deal ends, though the exact window varies. Ask your lender for their figure rather than assuming it.

Can I switch to a cheaper rate if one appears after I have reserved? With most lenders, yes, up to a point in the process. It is not universal and it is not always up to the day of completion, so get the answer in writing from your specific lender before you rely on it.

Is it worth paying a product fee to get a lower rate? It depends entirely on your balance. On a large mortgage the interest saving usually outweighs the fee. On a small remaining balance it often does not. Compare the total cost over the whole fixed term, including the fee, rather than comparing headline rates.
