The Payslip Trick Most UK Employees Never Ask About

Salary sacrifice lets you pay into a pension before income tax and National Insurance are taken, which is why the same contribution costs you less. It is offered by most large employers, and a surprising number of staff have never been told. Here is how it works, the four traps, and the change coming in 2029.

The short version

  • You agree to a lower salary, and your employer pays the difference into your pension. Because it comes off before tax and National Insurance, the same pension contribution costs you less take-home.
  • It is most valuable near a threshold, because it reduces the income assessed for income tax, so it can pull you back under £50,270 or £100,000.
  • Your employer usually saves National Insurance too, and some pass part of that saving into your pension. Always ask, because many do not advertise it.
  • From 2029, sacrificed pension contributions above £2,000 a year are set to attract National Insurance, so the advantage narrows on larger amounts.
  • Four things to check first: mortgage affordability, statutory pay, minimum wage floor, and any salary-linked benefits.

1. How it actually works

Normally your pension contribution comes out of pay you have already been taxed on, or gets tax relief added back afterwards. Either way, National Insurance has usually already been taken.

With salary sacrifice, you formally give up part of your contractual salary, and your employer pays that amount directly into your pension instead. Because your gross salary is genuinely lower, both income tax and National Insurance are calculated on the smaller figure.

The pension gets the same amount. Your take-home falls by less than the contribution. That difference is the National Insurance you no longer pay, and it is the entire point.

It is not a loophole. It is a formal change to your employment contract that the tax system deliberately treats differently, and most employers already have the scheme set up.

Worked example: the same contribution, less cost

Priya earns £45,000 and wants to put an extra £200 a month into her pension.

Without salary sacrifice: the £200 comes from pay that has already had National Insurance taken. Her take-home falls by roughly the full amount after basic-rate relief is applied.

With salary sacrifice: her contractual salary drops by £200 a month. She pays no income tax and no National Insurance on that £200, so her take-home falls by noticeably less, while the pension still receives the full £200.

Same pension. Smaller hit to her wages. If her employer also passes on part of its own NI saving, the pension receives more than £200.

The effect is larger for a higher-rate taxpayer, and larger again for anyone whose income sits just above £50,270 or £100,000, because the sacrifice can pull assessed income back below the threshold.

2. Who it suits, and who it does not

Suits you if: you are employed, your employer offers it, you can manage on slightly less take-home, and especially if you are just above £50,270 or approaching £100,000. See our guide to frozen tax thresholds for why those two numbers matter so much.

Does not suit you if: you are close to the National Minimum Wage, since sacrifice cannot take you below it. Or if you are applying for a mortgage soon. Or if you genuinely need every pound of current take-home, because pension money is locked until pension age.

Not available if you are self-employed. There is no salary to sacrifice. Pension contributions still get tax relief, but through a different route.

3. Four traps to check before you sign

  1. Mortgage affordability. Lenders assess your reduced contractual salary. If you are buying or remortgaging within a year, check with a broker first, because a lower gross salary can reduce how much you can borrow.
  2. Statutory pay. Maternity, paternity, sick pay and redundancy pay can be calculated on the lower salary. If any of those are foreseeable, get it in writing how your employer handles it.
  3. The minimum wage floor. Sacrifice cannot reduce your pay below the National Minimum Wage, so lower earners may be limited or excluded.
  4. Salary-linked benefits. Life cover, income protection and bonus calculations are sometimes based on reduced salary. Good schemes use "notional" or pre-sacrifice salary. Ask which yours uses.

4. What changes in 2029

From 2029, salary sacrifice pension contributions above £2,000 a year are set to attract National Insurance. Below that level the treatment is unchanged.

What this means in practice: the income tax advantage remains either way, and the National Insurance advantage continues on the first £2,000 a year. It is a narrowing of the benefit on larger contributions, not an abolition of the scheme.

It is also three years away, and policy can change again before then. It is not a reason to avoid setting this up now if it suits you today.

5. How to ask your employer

Most people never ask because they assume it is complicated or unavailable. Two sentences to HR or payroll usually settles it:

"Do we operate salary sacrifice for pension contributions, and if so does the company pass on any of its National Insurance saving into the employee's pension?"

That second half is the part worth asking. Some employers keep the whole NI saving, some share it, and a few pass on all of it. It costs nothing to find out, and the answer can be worth hundreds a year.

Also ask whether the scheme uses notional salary for benefits and statutory pay, and how often you are allowed to change your contribution level.

Frequently asked

Does it reduce my State Pension? Your State Pension depends on your National Insurance record of qualifying years, not the exact amount paid, so for most people it is unaffected. If your earnings are low enough that sacrifice could take you under the threshold for a qualifying year, that is a genuine concern worth checking.

Can I change my mind? Usually yes, but only at set points, often annually or on a life event, because it is a contractual change. Ask before signing.

Is it only for pensions? No. Cycle to work, electric car schemes and workplace nurseries commonly use it too. Pensions are simply where the numbers are largest.

Is it legal? Yes. It is a long-established, HMRC-recognised arrangement, provided the contract is genuinely varied.

6. The honest verdict

If you are employed, your employer offers it, and you can live on slightly less take-home, salary sacrifice is one of the few genuinely free improvements available on a payslip. The same pension contribution simply costs you less.

Check the four traps first, particularly if a mortgage or parental leave is on the horizon. Ask whether the employer shares its National Insurance saving, because that question alone can be worth a few hundred pounds a year.

Then set it and forget it. It is dull, and dull is usually where the reliable money is.
