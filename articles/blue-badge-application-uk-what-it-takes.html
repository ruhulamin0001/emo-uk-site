A Blue Badge Costs Up to 10 Pounds. The Expensive Part Is the Twelve Weeks

The fee is small, capped and set by your council. The real cost of a Blue Badge application is time, and the real risk is applying on the wrong route. One quirk of the rules means a lower Personal Independence Payment score can automatically qualify you where a higher one does not.

The short version

  • The badge costs up to £10 in England and £20 in Scotland, and it is free in Wales. Northern Ireland applies separately. Source: GOV.UK.
  • Badges usually last up to 3 years and you must reapply before the current one expires.
  • Assessment can take 12 weeks or longer. DfT says the council cannot even start until it has all the evidence, so an incomplete application does not queue, it waits.
  • Exactly 10 points under PIP descriptor E automatically qualifies you. DfT states that any other score, including a higher score of 12, does not automatically qualify.
  • The automatic list has six routes, including higher rate DLA mobility, 8 or more points for moving around, and being registered severely sight impaired.
  • A refusal is not the end. If the council did not take account of all the facts you can ask it to look again, and you can reapply if your mobility gets worse.

1. What it costs and how long it lasts

GOV.UK: "A Blue Badge costs up to £10 in England and £20 in Scotland. It's free in Wales." The words to notice are "up to". The English figure is a ceiling, not a price, and individual councils set their own charge beneath it. Some charge nothing. How you pay also depends on the council.

Badges usually last up to 3 years, and GOV.UK is explicit that you must reapply before the current one expires. A badge that has run out is not a badge, and the bays it would have opened are enforced by the same civil penalties as any other parking restriction.

If a badge is lost, stolen or damaged and it is not expiring in the next three months, that is a different service, not a new application.

Northern Ireland applies through nidirect, and Scotland and Wales have their own eligibility guidance even though the application starts on GOV.UK. The detailed eligibility document quoted throughout this guide says on its face that it applies to England. That matters more than it sounds, because the thing most often copied between websites is the English list.

2. The six automatic routes, in full

The Department for Transport guidance says you automatically qualify if you are aged 3 or over and at least one of the following applies:

  • you receive the higher rate of the mobility component of Disability Living Allowance
  • you receive PIP because you cannot walk more than 50 metres, which means a score of 8 points or more under the moving around activity of the mobility component
  • you are registered blind, that is severely sight impaired
  • you receive a War Pensioners' Mobility Supplement
  • you have received a lump sum within tariff levels 1 to 8 of the Armed Forces and Reserve Forces Compensation Scheme and are certified as having a permanent and substantial disability causing inability to walk or very considerable difficulty walking
  • you receive the mobility component of PIP and have 10 points specifically for descriptor E under the planning and following journeys activity, on the grounds that you cannot undertake any journey because it would cause overwhelming psychological distress

The minimum automatic qualification age was changed from 2 to 3 years old on 10 August 2021, which is the most recent substantive update logged on that guidance. Children under 3 are covered by the discretionary route instead, and there are two specific grounds for them, set out in section four.

3. The 10 points that beats 12 points

This is the part of the scheme that catches people, and the guidance states it in plain words rather than leaving it to be inferred:

"If you have any score other than 10 points under descriptor E, in the 'planning and following journeys' activity of PIP you may still be eligible for a Blue Badge, but you do not automatically qualify. This includes if you have a higher score of 12."

Read that twice. In almost every other benefit interaction in the UK, a higher score is better or at worst neutral. Here it is not. Automatic qualification is tied to one specific descriptor at one specific score, not to a points total. Someone awarded 12 points under a different descriptor in the same activity is, on the automatic route, in a worse position than someone awarded 10 under descriptor E, even though the higher score reflects a greater assessed difficulty.

What this means practically is simple and worth acting on. Before you apply, get the last page of your PIP decision letter out and look at the descriptor letter, not just the total. DfT tells you exactly which pages the council wants: the front page showing entitlement, the second to last page showing assessment scores, and the last page showing mobility scores.

And to be clear about what the rule does not do: a score other than 10 under descriptor E does not disqualify you. It moves you from the automatic route to the assessed route, where the council decides on evidence. That is slower and less certain, not impossible.

4. The route for everyone else

The discretionary list is long and covers both walking difficulty and non-visible conditions, which were brought into the scheme on 30 August 2019. You may be eligible if, among other grounds, you cannot walk at all, cannot walk without help or aids, find walking very difficult because of pain, breathlessness or the time it takes, or walking is dangerous to your health.

The non-visible grounds include being constantly a significant risk to yourself or others near vehicles and in car parks, struggling severely to plan or follow a journey, finding it difficult or impossible to control your actions with a lack of awareness of the impact on others, regularly having intense and overwhelming responses causing temporary loss of behavioural control, and frequently becoming extremely anxious or fearful of public or open spaces.

Two grounds exist specifically for children under 3: a medical condition meaning the child must always be accompanied by bulky medical equipment, or must always be kept near a vehicle in case emergency treatment is needed.

There is also a ground people forget: a severe disability in both arms, where you drive regularly but cannot operate pay-and-display machines.

On timing, the guidance is blunt: "They cannot start the assessment process until they have all the necessary evidence. It may take 12 weeks or longer to assess your application." An application missing a document does not sit in a queue making progress. It sits.

5. Worked example: Bernard in Scunthorpe

Bernard is 64 and has a heart and lung condition. He receives PIP. His mobility award is 10 points under the moving around activity.

Does he automatically qualify? No. The automatic route on moving around requires 8 points or more, so on the face of it 10 points clears it. But the wording ties the automatic route to the reason for the award, walking no more than 50 metres, so the descriptor matters as much as the number. Bernard checks the last page of his DWP letter and confirms which descriptor produced his points before he assumes anything.

What his application actually costs him. His council charges the full £10. That is the entire financial outlay. Against that, the assessment may take 12 weeks or longer. If he is applying because a hospital course starts in six weeks, the fee is irrelevant and the calendar is the whole problem. The practical answer is to apply the day the need becomes foreseeable, not the week it becomes urgent.

What he gathers before applying, in one sitting: proof of identity from the list, which is a birth or adoption certificate, passport, driving licence, marriage or civil partnership certificate, or divorce or dissolution certificate. Proof of address, which is a recent council tax bill, a recent letter from a government department such as DWP, or a driving licence. A recent head and shoulders digital photo. His National Insurance number. Contact details. And the three specific pages of his PIP letter.

If he is refused. GOV.UK says the council should tell him why, and that he can ask them to reconsider if he does not think all the important information he provided was taken into account. He can also reapply if his mobility problems become more serious. Neither of those is an appeal to a tribunal, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. It is a request to the same body to look again, which is why the quality of the evidence on the first attempt is worth the effort.

The honest verdict

For most people the Blue Badge is the cheapest significant thing their council will ever issue them, and the fee is not where the difficulty lies. Up to £10 in England, £20 in Scotland, free in Wales, up to three years, and an assessment that may run beyond twelve weeks.

The single most useful thing you can do before applying is to read your own PIP decision letter properly. The automatic route depends on which descriptor produced your points, and DfT says outright that a higher score of 12 does not automatically qualify where exactly 10 under descriptor E does. That sentence is on a government page and almost nowhere else.

Where this page does not help you: it will not make a discretionary application succeed, and it cannot tell you what your council will decide. If the assessed route is where you end up, the useful spend is not money, it is gathering medication, treatment and clinician details before you submit rather than after a request for more information restarts the clock.

Frequently asked

How much will my council actually charge? GOV.UK sets a ceiling of £10 in England, not a fixed price, and says how you pay depends on your local council. Some councils charge less and some charge nothing. Check your own council's Blue Badge page before assuming £10.

Does a Blue Badge work across the UK? The badge is recognised across Great Britain, but the rules on how it can be used differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, and DfT publishes four separate rights and responsibilities documents for that reason. Read the one for the place you are parking, not the one for where you live.

I get PIP but not for mobility. Can I still apply? Yes, through the assessed route rather than the automatic route. The council will want evidence of medication, treatments and the professionals involved in your care, and will ask how your condition affects either walking or the journey between the vehicle and your destination.

Can an organisation hold a badge? Yes, if it both cares for or assists people who need a Blue Badge and transports them. DfT gives residential care homes, hospices and council social services as common examples, and says taxi, private hire and community transport operators are unlikely to qualify because they are not usually concerned with the care of disabled people.
