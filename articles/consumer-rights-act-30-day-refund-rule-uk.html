The Shop Policy Saying 14 Days for Faulty Goods Is Not Binding on You

Everyone knows there is a 30 day window to reject something faulty. Almost nobody knows when it starts, that agreeing to a repair freezes it, or that an agreement shortening it is void by statute. All three are in one section of the Consumer Rights Act 2015.

The short version

  • 30 days, and a shop cannot shorten it. Section 22(2): an agreement under which the right would be lost before the time limit passes is not binding on the consumer.
  • The clock starts the day after three things have all happened: ownership or possession transferred, goods delivered, and any installation the trader had to do notified as done.
  • Agreeing to a repair stops the clock for the whole waiting period, from the day you ask or agree to the day you get the goods back.
  • If the repair fails you get more time, either 7 days after the waiting period ends or the original limit extended by the waiting period, whichever is later.
  • Perishable goods get a shorter window, set by how long they can reasonably be expected to last.
  • You can reject before delivery. Subsection (5) says the start conditions do not prevent it.
  • A trader can give you longer. Subsection (1) allows an agreement to exercise the right later. Longer is allowed, shorter is not.

1. When the 30 days actually starts

Section 22(3) of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 says the time limit for exercising the short-term right to reject is the end of 30 days beginning with the first day after all of these have happened:

  • ownership, or in the case of hire, hire purchase or a conditional sale, possession of the goods has been transferred to the consumer
  • the goods have been delivered, and
  • where the contract requires the trader to install the goods, or take other action to enable you to use them, the trader has notified you that the action has been taken

All three, not any one. The practical effect is that the purchase date on the receipt is often not the start date. Order a washing machine on 20 May, take delivery and installation on 1 June with the trader confirming installation is done, and your 30 days begins on 2 June and ends on 1 July.

Two qualifications sit alongside it. Subsection (4) gives a shorter window for goods of a kind that can reasonably be expected to perish after a shorter period, which is how food is handled without needing a separate rule. And subsection (5) confirms that none of this prevents you rejecting before delivery or installation has happened.

The section is in force from 1 October 2015, and legislation.gov.uk records it as up to date with all changes known to be in force on or before 9 January 2026.

2. Why a shop policy cannot shorten it

Subsection (2) is one sentence and it does a great deal of work. An agreement under which the short-term right to reject would be lost before the time limit passes is not binding on the consumer.

So a returns policy giving you 14 days for faulty goods does not reduce your statutory 30. Neither does a line in the terms you ticked at checkout. The right is not something you can be made to trade away by accepting a policy, and the statute says so directly rather than leaving it to argument.

It works the other way too. Subsection (1) says you lose the right if the time limit passes without you exercising it, unless the trader and the consumer agree that it may be exercised later. A trader can give you longer. They just cannot give you less.

That asymmetry is the single most useful thing to know in a shop. Extended goodwill windows are real and worth having. Shortened faulty goods windows are not enforceable against you.

One thing to be careful about, because it is where arguments start. A returns policy for goods that are not faulty, where you have simply changed your mind, is a different subject. That is goodwill in a shop, and separate distance selling rules online. Section 22 is about goods that do not conform to the contract.

3. The repair that pauses the clock

Subsection (6) is the part that almost nobody knows. If you request or agree to the repair or replacement of goods, the 30 day period stops running for the length of the waiting period.

The waiting period is defined in subsection (8). It begins with the day you request or agree to the repair or replacement, and it ends with the day you receive the goods supplied in response.

Then subsection (7) handles the case where the repair does not fix it. If the goods supplied in response still do not conform to the contract, the time limit becomes either 7 days after the waiting period ends, or, if later, the original time limit extended by the waiting period.

4. Worked example: Yvonne in Ipswich, with the dates

Yvonne takes delivery and installation of a washing machine on 1 June and is notified installation is complete the same day. Her 30 days runs from 2 June to 1 July.

On 20 June it develops a fault and she agrees to a repair. The clock stops that day, with 11 days of her window unused.

The machine comes back on 4 July. The waiting period is 20 June to 4 July, which is 15 days.

It is still faulty. Her new limit is the later of two dates. Seven days after the waiting period ends is 11 July. The original limit extended by the waiting period is 1 July plus 15 days, which is 16 July. The later of those is 16 July, so that is her deadline.

What the pause is worth to her: without subsections (6) to (8), agreeing to a repair on 20 June would have quietly burned her remaining window while the machine sat in a workshop, and by 4 July her right to reject would have expired three days earlier. Instead she has twelve more days after getting it back.

The behaviour this should change: agreeing to a repair is not the surrender it feels like. Refusing a repair to protect your clock is the wrong move, because the statute already protects it.

The verdict

Know three sentences and you will win most of these conversations. The 30 days starts the day after delivery and installation are complete. A shop cannot give you less than 30 days. Agreeing to a repair pauses the clock rather than ending it.

This also reframes the extended warranty question. A warranty sold at the till is being sold against a statutory position that already covers the first 30 days outright, and covers faults beyond that through other sections of the same Act. Whether the warranty is worth it depends entirely on what it adds after those rights run out, and that is the comparison the seller is not making for you.

Where this does not help you: section 22 covers the short-term right to reject only. What happens after the 30 days, including repair, replacement, price reduction and the final right to reject, sits in later sections of the Act, and we have deliberately not summarised them here rather than compress them badly.

Frequently asked

Does the 30 days start on the day I paid? Not necessarily. It starts the day after ownership or possession has transferred, the goods have been delivered, and any installation the trader had to do has been notified as complete.

The shop says I only have 14 days for faults. Is that right? No. Section 22(2) says an agreement under which the right would be lost before the time limit passes is not binding on the consumer.

If I agree to a repair, do I lose my 30 days? No. The period stops running for the waiting period, which runs from the day you request or agree to the repair until the day you receive the goods back.

What about changing my mind rather than a fault? That is a different thing. Section 22 is about goods that do not conform to the contract. Change of mind returns in a shop are goodwill, and online purchases have their own separate cancellation rules.

Does this apply across the whole UK? Section 22 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 has UK extent on legislation.gov.uk. How you enforce it in practice still differs by nation.
