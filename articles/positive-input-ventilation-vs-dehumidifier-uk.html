A PIV Unit Costs About £25 A Year To Run. A Dehumidifier Costs About £154

Both machines claim to end condensation and mould. One runs a very small fan all the time, the other runs a compressor some of the time, and the gap in running cost is a factor of six. Whether either is the right purchase depends entirely on what kind of damp you have.

The short version

  • A PIV fan draws 5 to 15 watts continuously. Take 10W: that is 87.6 kWh a year, or £23.06 at the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026.
  • A 200W dehumidifier run eight hours a day costs £153.79 a year. Run it round the clock and it is £461.37.
  • Difference: about £131 a year in the PIV's favour. A PIV at £700 fitted therefore pays back in about 5.3 years against a daily dehumidifier.
  • Beware the heated PIV. Models with a heater element draw around 500W when it engages, which can reach about £1 a day and destroys the whole argument. Buy the unheated version unless the loft is genuinely freezing.
  • Neither fixes rising or penetrating damp. If the wall is wet at the bottom, or wet after rain, you have a building fault and a machine is money burned.

1. Work out which damp you actually have, before spending anything

Three completely different problems get called damp, and only one of them is fixed by a machine.

Condensation. Moisture in the air meets a cold surface and turns back to water. The signs are water on the inside of windows in the morning, black spotted mould in corners, behind wardrobes and along the cold edge of an external wall, and a musty smell in a room that is closed up. It is worst in bedrooms and bathrooms and worst in winter. This is the one a PIV or a dehumidifier addresses.

Penetrating damp. Water coming through the fabric from outside. The tell is that it appears or worsens after rain, it is often a defined patch rather than a spread, and it can be at any height. Causes are a cracked render, a blocked gutter, a failed pointing joint, a leaking downpipe or a slipped tile. No machine fixes this. A ladder and a bag of mortar might.

Rising damp. Water drawn up from the ground through the wall. It appears as a tide mark, usually under about a metre from the floor, often with salting on the plaster. It is genuinely rarer than the damp proofing industry implies, and it is a building repair. No machine fixes this either.

Before you spend anything, do the free test. Tape a square of kitchen foil tightly over the damp patch, sealed on all four edges, and leave it a few days. Water on the room side of the foil means condensation. Water on the wall side means moisture is coming through the wall. That one piece of foil can save you several hundred pounds of the wrong purchase.

2. How each machine works, and why the running costs differ so much

A dehumidifier removes water from the air in one room. A compressor or a desiccant wheel pulls moisture out and drips it into a tank you empty. It is fast, it is portable, and it works. It also draws real power, typically 150 to 300 watts for a domestic unit, and it only treats the room it is standing in.

A PIV unit changes the air in the whole house. A small fan in the loft pushes gently filtered air down through a ceiling diffuser, usually on the landing, at a low continuous rate. The slight positive pressure displaces the moist indoor air out through gaps and trickle vents, taking the humidity with it. It does not remove water. It replaces wet air with drier air.

That difference is why the running costs are not close. A dehumidifier has to do thermodynamic work on water. A PIV only has to turn a fan slowly. The fan motor draws 5 to 15 watts, which is less than a single LED bulb.

There is one catch and it is important. The air a PIV pushes in is cold in winter, and your heating has to warm it. Manufacturers argue the drier air feels warmer and costs less to heat, and there is something in that, but be honest that a PIV imposes a small heating cost that does not appear on the fan's wattage. Some units add a heater element to temper the incoming air, drawing around 500 watts when it runs, and at that point the electricity bill rises by up to about £1 a day and the comparison below no longer holds.

4. When a PIV is the wrong buy

You have no loft. A standard PIV mounts in a loft space and drops through the landing ceiling. Flats and top-floor conversions need a wall-mounted or cupboard-mounted unit, which is a different product at a different price, and the airflow arrangement is fussier.

The house is very airtight. A PIV works by pushing air in and letting it leave through the fabric. In a well-sealed modern house there is nowhere for it to go, and the unit either does nothing or makes doors difficult to close. In that case a heat recovery ventilation system is the right product and it costs considerably more.

The damp is not condensation. Covered above. Do the foil test.

The moisture source is fixable and enormous. Drying laundry indoors on a radiator puts several litres of water into the air every load. A tumble dryer venting into the room, an unvented bathroom, a kettle boiled with the lid off, an unflued gas heater. Fixing the source is free and beats managing the symptom. Our comparison of heated airers against tumble dryers covers the laundry side, which is the biggest single indoor moisture source in most homes.

You are renting. A PIV is a fitted alteration and needs the landlord's consent and an electrician. A dehumidifier and a habit of ventilating are the sensible renter's answer, and both move house with you.

5. Worked example: Hyacinth in Stoke-on-Trent

Hyacinth has black mould in two bedroom corners and streaming windows every winter morning. She has been running a 250W dehumidifier in the worst bedroom for about ten hours a day from November to March.

That is 2.5 kWh a day for 150 days, so 375 kWh, or £98.70 a year at 26.32p. It also only helps the room it is in, and she has been carrying it between rooms.

She is quoted £780 for an unheated loft PIV, fitted, with the diffuser on the landing. Its fan draws 9W continuously: 78.8 kWh, or £20.74 a year.

Saving on running cost: £77.96 a year. £780 divided by £77.96 is 10.0 years. On running cost alone, that is a poor deal.

What changes her mind is not the electricity. It is that the dehumidifier treats one room and the PIV treats the whole house, and that she has been repainting the same two corners every eighteen months at about £60 a time in materials and a lost weekend. Counting the paint, the payback drops to about seven years, and counting the weekends it is shorter still.

Before committing, she does two free things: she starts drying washing in the bathroom with the door shut and the extractor running, and she opens the bedroom windows for ten minutes every morning. By February the windows are dry and the mould has stopped spreading. She decides not to buy the PIV at all.

That is a legitimate outcome and it is the reason this page exists.

6. The honest verdict

Do the foil test and fix the source first. Ventilate properly, dry laundry where the extractor can take the moisture, and check the bathroom fan actually shifts air. That costs nothing and it is enough for a large share of British houses.

If the condensation persists across several rooms, a PIV is the right machine. At £23 a year to run against £154 for a daily dehumidifier, it pays back in roughly five years at a £700 fitted price, and it needs almost no attention afterwards.

If the problem is one room, or you rent, buy a dehumidifier instead. It is a fifth of the price, it needs no installation, and it comes with you when you move.

Do not buy a heated PIV unless someone has demonstrated why your loft needs one. The heater element costs more to run in a winter than the whole unit costs to buy.

Frequently asked

Does a PIV make the house colder? Slightly, and honestly. It introduces outside air, and your heating pays to warm it. Manufacturers argue drier air is cheaper to heat and there is truth in that, but expect a small net heating cost rather than none. It is still far less than the difference in electricity between the two machines.

How often do PIV filters need changing? Typically every two to five years depending on the model and how dirty the air is. Budget £20 to £40 a change and add it to the running cost. It is small but it is not nothing.

Will a dehumidifier heat the room? A compressor dehumidifier does put nearly all of its electricity back into the room as heat, so a 250W unit is roughly a 250W heater. That is a genuine consolation in winter and a real nuisance in summer.

My landlord says the mould is my fault. Is it? It can be both. Lifestyle moisture is real, and so are cold uninsulated walls and missing extractors. Under the current rules landlords in England must investigate and address damp and mould hazards within set timescales. Report it in writing and keep photographs with dates.
