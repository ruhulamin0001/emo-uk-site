Everyone Told You to Go Limited at £50,000. For 2026/27, the Sums Say Stay a Sole Trader.

Two changes landed quietly this year. Dividend tax went up on 6 April 2026 and Companies House doubled its fees on 1 February 2026. Run the 2026/27 arithmetic on a one-person business that takes all its profit out, and the sole trader wins at every level we tested, from £30,000 up to £120,000.

The short version

  • Dividend tax rose 2 percentage points on 6 April 2026. The ordinary rate went from 8.75% to 10.75% and the upper rate from 33.75% to 35.75%. That is the change that broke the old advice.
  • Companies House fees roughly doubled on 1 February 2026. Digital incorporation went from £50 to £100, and the annual confirmation statement from £34 to £50 digital, £110 on paper.
  • On profits of £45,000, a sole trader keeps £36,568. The same person through a limited company keeps £35,247. The sole trader is £1,321 ahead before a penny of accountancy fees.
  • At £80,000 the gap widens to £1,947 in the sole trader's favour, because corporation tax plus 35.75% dividend tax now costs more than 40% income tax plus 2% National Insurance.
  • The company still wins on four things, and none of them are tax on money you take out: limited liability, leaving profit inside the business, staying outside Making Tax Digital, and clients who will not contract with a sole trader.
  • There is nothing to buy in this article. Incorporating costs money. Not incorporating costs nothing.

1. What actually changes when you incorporate

As a sole trader you and the business are the same legal person. You pay income tax and Class 4 National Insurance on your profit whether you spend it or not. For 2026/27 that is 20% income tax between £12,570 and £50,270 and 40% above, plus Class 4 National Insurance at 6% up to £50,270 and 2% on everything above.

A limited company is a separate legal person. It pays corporation tax on its profit, 19% up to £50,000 and 25% above £250,000 with marginal relief sliding between the two. Then you pay yourself, usually a small salary plus dividends, and you pay personal tax again on the dividends.

That second layer is the whole story. For years it was cheap enough that the two-step route beat the one-step route once profits passed roughly £30,000 to £40,000. On 6 April 2026 it got 2 percentage points dearer, and the arithmetic changed sides.

2. The running costs nobody quotes you

Most comparison articles still quote Companies House fees that stopped being correct on 1 February 2026. Here is what a company actually costs to run now, before you pay anyone for advice.

Confirmation statement, paper (every year)£34£110
Voluntary strike off, digital£13

Those are the visible costs. The invisible ones matter more. A company must file annual accounts and a corporation tax return, run PAYE if it pays a salary, and keep the two sets of money genuinely separate. Most one-person companies pay an accountant between £60 and £150 a month for this. A sole trader with tidy records can often file their own return for nothing.

There is also a step that did not exist two years ago. Since 18 November 2025, identity verification at Companies House is mandatory. Every new director and person with significant control must verify through GOV.UK One Login or an authorised provider and supply a personal code at registration. Existing directors do it at their next confirmation statement. It is free, but the days of setting up a company on a whim in ten minutes are over.

3. The tax comparison, run properly

Below is the same profit figure run two ways for the 2026/27 tax year. The sole trader column is income tax plus Class 4 National Insurance. The company column assumes the most common efficient structure: a £12,570 salary, employer National Insurance at 15% on the slice above £5,000, corporation tax on what is left, and the remainder taken as dividends with the £500 dividend allowance applied.

£120,000£80,911£74,654Sole trader +£6,257

Two details drive this and both are easy to miss.

Employer National Insurance is not free. A single-director company paying a £12,570 salary owes 15% employer National Insurance on the £7,570 above the £5,000 secondary threshold, which is £1,135.50. The £10,500 Employment Allowance that would normally wipe this out is not available to a company whose only employee earning above the threshold is the sole director. Guides written for two-person businesses quietly assume the allowance applies. For most freelancers it does not.

The upper dividend rate is now brutal. Above £50,270 of total income you pay 35.75% on dividends that have already had corporation tax taken off. Stack an effective corporation tax rate of about 21% under 35.75% and the combined bite on that slice is roughly 49%. A sole trader on the same slice pays 40% income tax plus 2% National Insurance, so 42%. That is why the gap grows rather than shrinks as profits rise.

The £60,000 row is the odd one out. Around that level marginal relief on corporation tax and the point where dividends cross into the upper rate briefly cancel out. Nobody should restructure a business over £20.

4. Worked example: Nadia in Luton

Nadia is a freelance graphic designer. Her business made £45,000 profit in 2026/27 and she has no other income. She needs all of it to live on. An online company formation advert has told her she would be better off limited.

As a sole trader. Her personal allowance covers the first £12,570, leaving £32,430 taxed at 20%, which is £6,486. Class 4 National Insurance at 6% on that same £32,430 is £1,945.80. Total tax and National Insurance: £8,431.80. She keeps £36,568.20.

Through a limited company. The company pays her a £12,570 salary, which triggers £1,135.50 of employer National Insurance. That leaves £31,294.50 of company profit, taxed at 19% corporation tax, which is £5,945.96. She can pay out £25,348.54 as dividends. Her personal allowance is already used by the salary, so after the £500 dividend allowance she pays 10.75% on £24,848.54, which is £2,671.22. She keeps £35,247.32.

The sole trader route leaves Nadia £1,320.88 better off. Then subtract the company's costs: £100 to incorporate, £50 a year for the confirmation statement, and say £80 a month for an accountant to handle accounts, corporation tax and payroll. On those assumptions the company costs her roughly £2,370 in year one to be £1,321 worse off on tax. The real gap is closer to £3,690.

5. When a limited company still wins

None of the above means companies are pointless. It means the reason to have one is no longer the tax on money you take out. There are four reasons that still hold.

Limited liability. If the business could plausibly be sued or could owe money it cannot pay, a company puts a wall between the business and your house. This is the reason that actually matters and it is worth paying for. A sole trader is personally liable without limit.

You do not need all the profit. The table above assumes every pound comes out. If you can leave money in the business, the comparison inverts. A sole trader on £80,000 pays 42% on the top slice whether or not they touch it. A company pays about 26.5% marginal corporation tax and the rest stays inside, available for equipment, a lean year, or an employer pension contribution that is deductible with no dividend tax and no National Insurance. That is a timing advantage, not a free lunch, because dividend tax still lands whenever you take it out.

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. From 6 April 2026, sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year must keep digital records and file quarterly updates instead of one annual return. The threshold drops to £30,000 from 6 April 2027 and £20,000 from 6 April 2028. Qualifying income is gross turnover before expenses, not profit, so it catches people well below £50,000 of take-home. Limited companies are not in scope. For a lot of freelancers this will be the first genuine push towards incorporating, and it has nothing to do with tax rates.

Clients who require it. Some agencies and larger firms simply will not engage a sole trader. If that is your market, the decision is made for you. Our guide on setting freelance rates in the UK covers how to price that overhead back in.

6. Two things people wrongly believe

A company does not give you more expenses. The test is wholly and exclusively for the business in both cases. If a cost is not allowable as a sole trader, it is very unlikely to become allowable inside a company.

Incorporating does not reset the VAT threshold. Registration is compulsory once taxable turnover passes the threshold in any rolling 12 months, and HMRC has anti-avoidance rules for businesses that split or transfer to stay below it. The honest verdict

If you are a one-person business taking all your profit out to live on, the sole trader route is now cheaper across the range we tested, and the gap gets wider as you earn more, not narrower. The rule of thumb that told you to incorporate above £40,000 or £50,000 was written for a dividend tax regime that ended on 5 April 2026.

Incorporate because you need liability protection, because you can genuinely leave profit in the business, because Making Tax Digital is about to catch you, or because your clients demand it. Those are good reasons. Do not incorporate because someone told you it saves tax on money you are going to spend, because for 2026/27 it does not.

If you are near the £60,000 line, or you have a spouse who could genuinely work in the business, that is a conversation for an accountant with your actual figures. The cost of that hour is small next to getting it wrong for three years.

Frequently asked

I already have a limited company. Should I close it? Not on the strength of one tax year. Closing has costs, and if you have retained profit inside the company there may be a more sensible way to extract it than dividends. Also weigh the liability protection you would be giving up. Get advice with your own numbers before you file a strike off.

Does putting my partner on the payroll change the answer? It can, in two ways. A genuine second employee paid above the £5,000 secondary threshold restores eligibility for the £10,500 Employment Allowance, and splitting dividends across two personal allowances and two basic rate bands can beat one. Both only work if the work and the shareholding are real. HMRC has settled law on arrangements that are not.

Can I stay a sole trader and avoid Making Tax Digital? Only if your qualifying income stays under the threshold for the relevant year, or you qualify for an exemption such as being digitally excluded. Qualifying income is gross income from self-employment and property combined, before expenses, so check it against turnover rather than profit.

Do these figures apply in Scotland? Not exactly. Scottish taxpayers pay Scottish rates and bands on earned and self-employment income, which changes the sole trader column. Dividend tax, corporation tax and National Insurance are UK-wide. The direction of travel is the same but the numbers differ, so run yours against the Scottish bands.
