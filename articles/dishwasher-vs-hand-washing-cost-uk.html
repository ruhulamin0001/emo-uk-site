A Dishwasher Does Not Save You Money If You Wash Up In A Bowl. It Does If You Rinse Under The Tap.

The claim that a dishwasher is cheaper than washing up is repeated everywhere and is only true for some kitchens. We took the energy and water figures off two Bosch labels, priced them at the Ofgem cap for October 2026, and set them against the cost of heating a washing-up bowl by gas and by immersion heater. Then we worked out what the machine has to save to pay for itself, and for whom it never will.

The short version

  • A Bosch SMS4HKW00G uses 0.836 kWh and 9.5 litres per eco cycle on its label. At 26.32p that is 22p of electricity per full load of 13 place settings. The cheaper SMS2ITW08G is 0.923 kWh and 10.5 litres, 24p.
  • A 10 litre bowl of hot water from a gas combi costs about 3p to heat at 7.97p per kWh. Wash and rinse bowls together, about 6p. Heated by an electric immersion instead, 9p a bowl and 18p for two.
  • A careful gas-heated bowl washer spends less than the dishwasher does. Three washing-up sessions a day is about 18p; one eco cycle is 22p. The dishwasher cannot pay back on energy alone in that home.
  • Rinsing under a running hot tap changes everything. Three minutes of hot tap at 6 litres a minute is 18 litres, about 5p by gas and 15p by immersion, on top of the bowl. Tap-runners on electric hot water spend 40p to 70p a day and the machine pays back in under three years.
  • The energy class premium does not pay back. The D-rated and E-rated Bosch machines are 8 kWh per 100 cycles apart, about £2 a year at one cycle a day.
  • Water is 9.5 litres a cycle either way, less than two full bowls, and far less than a running rinse. On a meter, that is where the dishwasher's real saving sits.

1. What the dishwasher label actually says

Every dishwasher sold in the UK carries an energy label with two figures that matter: energy per 100 eco cycles and water per eco cycle. They are measured the same way for every machine, on the eco programme, at full load, so they are the fairest numbers available. We took two current Bosch freestanding machines from the manufacturer's own product pages, read on 4 September 2026.

ModelClassEnergy per 100 eco cyclesPer cycleWater per cyclePlace settingsEco programme length

Priced at the Ofgem price cap unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p per kWh for Direct Debit customers, the eco cycle costs 22p on the D-rated machine and 24p on the E-rated one. That is the whole of the electricity for a full load of 12 or 13 place settings, which for most households is a full day's crockery, pans and cutlery.

Two warnings on those figures. First, they are for the eco programme, which runs for four and a half to five hours at a lower temperature. The normal or auto programme most people actually use is faster, hotter, and uses more energy; Bosch does not publish a per-cycle figure for it, and neither will we guess one, but expect it to be above the eco number. Second, the label assumes a full load. A half-full machine costs the same to run and doubles the cost per plate.

The Energy Saving Trust's own comparison, updated 3 August 2026, puts an A-rated 12-place machine at about £35 a year to run in Great Britain and a G-rated one at £70. Those are its figures at its assumed usage, and they bracket ours.

2. What a bowl of hot water costs, by gas and by immersion

Nobody publishes a label for washing up by hand, so this is our arithmetic, and the assumptions are stated so you can change them.

A standard washing-up bowl holds about 10 litres when it is usefully full. Cold mains water in winter arrives at around 10 to 15 degrees; a bowl you can put your hands in is about 45 degrees. Heating 10 litres by 30 degrees takes 10 times 4.186 times 30 kilojoules, which is 1,256 kJ, or 0.35 kWh of heat.

By gas combi boiler. A modern condensing combi turns roughly 90 per cent of the gas it burns into hot water, so 0.35 kWh of heat needs about 0.39 kWh of gas. At the October 2026 cap rate of 7.97p per kWh, that is 3.1p a bowl. A wash bowl and a separate rinse bowl, about 6p.

By electric immersion heater or instant electric water heater. Electricity converts to heat at essentially 100 per cent, so 0.35 kWh of heat is 0.35 kWh of electricity, 9.2p a bowl at 26.32p. Two bowls, about 18p. This is the number for anyone in an all-electric flat with a hot water cylinder, and it is three times the gas figure.

The running tap. This is where the money goes. A kitchen mixer tap typically delivers around 6 litres a minute on hot. Rinsing plates under it for three minutes across a washing-up session uses 18 litres of hot water, which is 0.63 kWh of heat: about 5.6p by gas or 16.5p by electricity, every session, on top of the bowl.

Water itself is a separate line. A dishwasher eco cycle uses 9.5 litres. Two bowls use 20 litres, and two bowls plus a three-minute rinse use 38 litres. If you are on a water meter, the dishwasher uses a quarter of the water a tap-rinser does, and you can price that from the per-cubic-metre rate on your own bill; we have not quoted a national figure because there is not one.

Read across the gas column first, because most UK homes heat water by gas. The careful bowl-washer spends £68 a year against the dishwasher's £80. The machine loses. It only loses by twelve pounds, and it wins on water and on time, but it does not win on energy, and any article that says it does is assuming you leave the tap running.

Now read the electric column. The same careful washer on an immersion heater spends £202 a year, and the dishwasher saves them £122 a year. The tap-runner on electric hot water spends £382, and the machine saves them £302 a year. That is not a small number, and it is the household the dishwasher was made for.

Payback is the purchase price divided by the annual saving. We do not quote purchase prices, because they change weekly and a stale one is worse than none; assume your own figure and use the table.

The energy class premium is a separate question and the answer is short. The D-rated and E-rated Bosch machines above are 8 kWh per 100 cycles apart. At one cycle a day, that is 29 kWh a year, or about £7.70. At the more typical four cycles a week it is under £5. If the D-rated machine costs £50 more, the payback on the better class is seven to ten years. Buy the class up for the quieter motor or the better drying if you value those; do not buy it for the electricity.

The Energy Saving Trust's wider spread, £35 for an A-rated 12-place machine against £70 for a G-rated one, is bigger because it spans the whole scale. Almost nothing on sale in the UK in 2026 sits at G, so the realistic choice is between C, D and E, and there the gap is pounds, not tens of pounds.

Warranty is where retailers differ and prices do not. Bosch's standard guarantee is two years; extended cover is sold separately and is rarely worth it on a machine this cheap to repair. Compare the guarantee length and whether the retailer will collect the old machine before you compare the sticker.

5. Worked example: Sunita and Raj in Luton

Three-bed semi, two adults and two children, gas combi, water meter fitted in 2024

Sunita washes up after breakfast and dinner in a bowl; Raj does lunch things and rinses everything under the running tap first because he was brought up that way. Call it two careful sessions and one tap-running session a day. Their kitchen is being refitted and there is a gap for a dishwasher.

Current cost, our arithmetic. Two careful sessions at 6p is 12p; one tap session at 6p plus 5.6p is about 12p. Roughly 24p a day, £88 a year in gas, and about 78 litres of water a day.

With a D-rated dishwasher, one eco cycle a day. 22p a day, £80 a year in electricity, 9.5 litres of water. Energy saving £8 a year. Water saving about 25,000 litres a year, or 25 cubic metres, which they can price from their own bill.

Payback on the machine on energy alone: 40 to 60 years. On energy plus metered water it comes down, but it does not come down to anything that would justify the purchase as a saving.

What they actually decide. They buy it, because forty minutes a day of washing up is worth more to them than £8 a year is, and because the kitchen has the gap. That is the honest reason most people buy one. They also stop pre-rinsing under the tap, which is where the only real energy saving in their house was hiding.

6. The honest verdict

If you heat water by gas and wash up in a bowl, a dishwasher will not save you money on energy. It costs about the same to run, saves a great deal of water, and buys you time. Buy it for the time or the water, and do not let anyone sell it to you as an energy saving.

If you heat water electrically, or you rinse under a running hot tap, the dishwasher genuinely wins: £120 to £300 a year at the October 2026 rates, and a payback of one to four years. In that home it is one of the better purchases you can make. Whichever you are, run the eco programme and fill it, because a half load at 22p is the most expensive way to wash a plate there is.

Frequently asked

Is it cheaper to use a dishwasher or wash by hand? On energy, a dishwasher eco cycle costs about 22p at the October 2026 cap, and a bowl of hot water by gas about 3p. A careful bowl-washer on gas spends slightly less than the machine; anyone rinsing under a running tap, or heating water electrically, spends more and the dishwasher saves them money.

How much does a dishwasher cost to run per cycle in the UK? The Bosch SMS4HKW00G label gives 0.836 kWh per eco cycle, which is 22p at 26.32p per kWh. Normal and intensive programmes use more; half loads cost the same as full ones.

Does the eco programme really save money? Yes. It is the programme the label figure is measured on and it uses less energy than the normal programme by running longer at a lower temperature. Bosch's eco cycle runs four and a half to five hours.

Is an A-rated dishwasher worth the extra? Rarely on energy alone. One class step is around 8 kWh per 100 cycles, about £2 a year at one cycle a day. The Energy Saving Trust's £35 against £70 figure spans the whole scale from A to G, and very few G-rated machines are on sale.
