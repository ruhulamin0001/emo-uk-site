The Cheapest Rewire Quote Is Usually the One That Costs You Most

Rewiring a house is notifiable work under the Building Regulations, which means somebody has to certify it. Quotes vary by thousands, and the gap is almost never the cable. It is who signs the certificate and who fills the holes back in. Get those two wrong and the bill arrives later, sometimes years later, on the day you try to sell.

The short version

  • A full rewire runs about £3,000 to £8,000 for a typical UK house, and the spread is driven by the number of points, the age of the building and how much plaster comes off.
  • It is notifiable work. GOV.UK lists replacing fuse boxes and connected electrics, and changing electrics near a bath or shower, as work that needs building regulations approval.
  • A competent person scheme registration is what saves you the paperwork. If your electrician is registered, they self-certify and you do not have to apply to building control yourself.
  • Without approval you will not have the certificates of compliance you may need when you sell. That is GOV.UK's own wording, and it is the sentence the cheap quote is quietly betting you will not read.
  • Making good is the hidden line. Plastering, redecorating and floor relaying after the chasing is often £1,000 or more, and a quote that excludes it is not cheaper, it is shorter.
  • New from 1 October 2026: building control applications and initial notices for certain residential buildings may attract the Building Safety Levy. Ask whether your quote allows for it.

1. When a house actually needs rewiring

Start here, because a good number of people who are quoted for a full rewire do not need one.

Age on its own is not a reason. A 1985 installation that has been tested, has a modern consumer unit and shows no faults may be perfectly serviceable. What triggers a genuine rewire is condition, not date. The things that do matter:

  • Rubber, lead or fabric insulated cable. If the cable sheathing crumbles when you touch it, that is a rewire, not a repair. This is broadly pre 1970 wiring.
  • No earthing to lights, or no earth at the sockets. Common in installations from the 1950s and 60s.
  • Round pin sockets or a rewireable fuse box with fuse wire. A fuse board on its own can sometimes be changed without a full rewire, which is a much cheaper job, and any electrician who refuses to price that option separately is worth questioning.
  • A recent Electrical Installation Condition Report with C1 or C2 codes. C1 means danger present, C2 means potentially dangerous. Those are the findings that justify work. A report full of C3 codes, meaning improvement recommended, does not.

The useful move before you accept any rewire quote is to pay for an Electrical Installation Condition Report from someone who is not bidding for the rewire. It typically costs a low hundreds of pounds and it tells you whether you are buying safety or buying somebody's van payment.

2. Notifiable work, and why it decides the price

This is the part that turns a cheap quote expensive, and almost nobody explains it.

GOV.UK's building regulations guidance lists work that needs building regulations approval, and electrical work is on it. Specifically named are replacing fuse boxes and connected electrics, changing electrics near a bath or shower, and installing a bathroom that will involve plumbing. A full rewire touches all of these.

There are two lawful routes:

Route one, the normal one. Your electrician is registered with a competent person scheme. They do the work, they self certify it, and the scheme notifies your local authority on your behalf. GOV.UK is explicit: you do not need to get approval yourself if you use someone registered with a competent person scheme.

Route two, the expensive one. Your electrician is not registered. You must apply to a building control body yourself, pay their fee, and have the work inspected. This costs money and time, and it has to happen before the work, not after.

What actually happens in the real world is a third thing: nobody does either, the work goes in unnotified, and everyone forgets about it until a conveyancing solicitor asks for the certificate. GOV.UK spells out the consequence in one sentence, and it is worth quoting exactly because it is the whole argument of this article:

Without approval you will not have the certificates of compliance you may need when you want to sell your home.

GOV.UK also notes that the person doing the work could be prosecuted and fined if they do not comply, and that the building control body could make you pay to fix faulty work. But the realistic risk for a homeowner is not prosecution. It is a stalled sale, a retention on the price, or paying for indemnity insurance and a fresh inspection years later at your own cost.

One new thing to ask about. GOV.UK now states that from 1 October 2026, applications for building control approval and initial notices for certain residential buildings may be subject to a tax called the Building Safety Levy. If your job is going down route two, ask whether the quote allows for it before you sign.

4. Making good, the line that is always missing

A rewire is destructive. Cables have to get from the consumer unit to every socket, switch and light in the house, and in a solid walled property that means chasing channels into the plaster, lifting floorboards, and cutting into ceilings.

Electricians are not plasterers and most do not want to be. So a great many quotes stop at what the trade calls first fix and second fix, and leave the building exactly as the cable left it: open chases, lifted boards, holes above the doorways. That is not a dishonest quote. It becomes dishonest only when nobody says so out loud.

The three questions that make the quotes comparable:

  1. Does the price include plastering the chases back? Not skimming the whole wall, just filling what you cut.
  2. Does it include relaying and refixing floorboards or carpet? Carpet that has been lifted usually needs a fitter to restretch it.
  3. Does it include any decorating? Almost never, and that is fair, but you should know before you budget.

On a typical three bedroom house, making good and redecorating after a rewire is commonly £1,000 to £2,500. A quote that is £2,000 cheaper and excludes all of it is not cheaper at all. It is the same price with the awkward part moved onto your weekend.

5. Worked example: the Whelan house in Stoke

A 1962 three bedroom semi in Stoke. Original consumer unit with rewireable fuses, no earth to the lighting circuits, sockets on a ring that has been extended twice by previous owners. They get three quotes.

Quote A: £2,900. Sole trader, not scheme registered, no mention of notification, making good excluded.

Quote B: £4,800. Scheme registered, self certification included, making good excluded.

Quote C: £6,900. Scheme registered, self certification included, chases plastered and boards relaid.

Making good priced separately: plasterer quotes £1,400, decorating they will do themselves.

So the real comparison is: A at £2,900 plus £1,400 plus no certificate = £4,300 and a problem. B at £4,800 plus £1,400 = £6,200 and a certificate. C at £6,900 and a certificate, with one contractor to chase if anything is wrong.

The gap between A and B is not £1,900 of profit. It is the certificate, and the certificate is the thing a buyer's solicitor will ask for. On a house worth a couple of hundred thousand pounds, a sale delayed by six weeks over a missing electrical certificate costs far more than £1,900 in mortgage offer expiry, removal rebooking and negotiating position.

The Whelans took B and hired the plasterer separately, which is the answer most households land on. C is the better answer if you are not confident managing two trades, and the £700 difference is the price of that being somebody else's problem.

Frequently asked

How do I check an electrician is registered? Ask which competent person scheme they belong to, then check on the scheme's own register rather than taking a logo on a van at face value. GOV.UK maintains a list of the authorised schemes.

Can I do any of it myself? Adding a socket to an existing circuit outside a bathroom or kitchen is generally not notifiable, but a rewire is, and a rewire done by an unregistered person requires you to notify building control in advance. The practical answer for almost everyone is no.

How long does it take and do I have to move out? A three bedroom house is commonly five to ten working days. You can usually stay, with power off in sections, but it is dusty, disruptive work and it is not pleasant with small children in the house.

Does a rewire add value? Not in the way a kitchen does. What it does is remove an obstacle. A buyer's survey flagging dangerous wiring costs you far more in price negotiation than the rewire costs. Treat it as clearing a liability rather than making an investment.

The honest verdict

Get an Electrical Installation Condition Report from someone who is not quoting for the work, before you accept any rewire quote. A few hundred pounds spent there is the only spend on this page that might save you the entire job.

If you do need it, only compare quotes from electricians registered with a competent person scheme, and make every quote state in writing whether making good is included. Those two rules make the quotes comparable, and without them the numbers mean nothing.

Do not take an unnotified rewire to save two thousand pounds. GOV.UK is explicit that without approval you will not have the certificates you may need when you sell, and that bill lands at the worst possible moment, with the least possible negotiating room.
