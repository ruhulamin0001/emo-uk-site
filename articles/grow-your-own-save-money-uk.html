Grow Your Own in the UK: What Actually Cuts the Food Bill

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Checked August 2026

The short version
  • Most people lose money in year one, because the setup costs land before the first harvest does.
  • The saving comes from growing what is expensive to buy and cheap to grow. Herbs, salad leaves, runner beans, courgettes, tomatoes, raspberries.
  • Potatoes, onions and carrots are the classic mistake. They are cheap in every supermarket and they eat your space for months.
  • An allotment is usually £30 to £125 a year depending on the council, but the waiting list is often the real barrier.
  • A realistic first-year saving from a small plot or a few containers is £100 to £300, not the thousands you will see quoted.

The honest arithmetic

There is a well-worn claim that a small vegetable plot returns many times what you put in. It can, eventually. It usually does not in the first year, because year one is when you buy the compost, the seed, the netting, the canes, the watering can and the pots, and harvest perhaps six weeks of produce from it.

The saving is real, but it comes from a narrow slice of what people actually plant. Two square metres of salad leaves and herbs will save you more than a whole bed of maincrop potatoes. That is the entire trick and most beginners do the opposite.

The way to think about it: you are not growing food, you are replacing specific supermarket purchases. Work backwards from your own receipt. If you never buy fresh coriander, growing coriander saves you nothing.

What is not worth the space

None of these are bad plants. They are simply a poor use of a small plot when the goal is saving money.

  • Maincrop potatoes. Cheap all year round, and they occupy a large area for months. Early new potatoes are a fairer argument, because those are genuinely dearer in the shops.
  • Onions. Very cheap to buy, slow to grow, and they need the ground all season.
  • Carrots. Cheap, fussy about soil, and carrot fly will punish you unless you net properly.
  • Sweetcorn. Needs a block of plants to pollinate at all, takes up serious room, and yields a handful of cobs.
  • Brassicas such as cabbage and cauliflower. Long season, and pigeons and caterpillars treat them as a buffet. Netting is not optional and it costs money.

If you have a large allotment and enjoy growing them, grow them. If you have four square metres and want to cut the food bill, they are the wrong choice.

The costs nobody counts

The reason the "grow your own saves thousands" claim falls apart is that it counts the harvest and not the inputs. A realistic first-year list:

  • Compost. The largest recurring cost and the one that surprises people. Peat-free multipurpose has not got cheaper. Buying in bulk in early spring is meaningfully cheaper than bags in July.
  • Seed. Individually cheap. Collectively, a first order is easily £30 and most packets contain far more seed than you will use in three years. Split orders with a neighbour.
  • Water. If you are on a meter, a hot summer of hand watering is not free. A water butt pays for itself.
  • Protection. Netting, fleece, slug defences, canes, string. Small amounts that add up fast.
  • Losses. Slugs, pigeons, blight, a fortnight away in August. Budget for losing some of everything, because you will.
  • Your time. Not a cash cost, but be honest that a plot is several hours a week from March to September.

Year two is where the economics improve sharply, because the pots, canes, netting and water butt are already paid for and only compost and seed recur.

Allotments: cost, waiting lists, rules

Allotment rent in the UK is typically somewhere between £30 and £125 a year for a standard plot, and it varies enormously by council. Many sites offer concessions for pensioners and people on certain benefits. Water is often included, sometimes not.

The real cost is the wait. Popular urban sites have lists measured in years, and demand rose sharply after 2020 and has not fallen back. Some practical points:

  • Put your name down now even if you are not ready. It costs nothing and the clock starts.
  • Ask about half plots. They come up more often, and a full plot is more work than most beginners expect.
  • Check the tenancy rules before you commit. Many sites require a minimum level of cultivation, restrict sheds, ban bonfires, and can serve notice if a plot is neglected.
  • Some sites let you share a plot officially. Half the work, half the rent, and someone to water while you are away.

No garden? What still works

A windowsill, a balcony or a doorstep is enough for the highest-value crops, which is convenient, because the highest-value crops are also the smallest.

  • Windowsill: basil, chives, chillies, mint, salad leaves in a shallow trough.
  • Balcony or doorstep: add tomatoes in a large pot, spring onions, a dwarf French bean, and strawberries in a hanging basket.
  • Buy the supermarket herb pot and rescue it. Those pots contain many seedlings crammed together. Split it into three or four pots of fresh compost and you turn a £2 herb that dies in a fortnight into plants that last months. It is the cheapest possible entry to this whole subject.

Container growing needs more watering and more feeding than ground, because the plant has no reserves to draw on. That is the trade-off for needing no garden.

The glut problem

Everything ripens at once. That is not a failure, it is how growing works, and it is why so many first-year plots end with a bin full of courgettes.

Three fixes, in order of usefulness:

  1. Sow in succession, not in one go. A short row of salad every three weeks instead of a whole packet in April. Same seed, four times the useful harvest.
  2. Freeze what freezes well. Beans, soft fruit, chillies, herbs in oil. This is where the actual money is saved, because it displaces winter shopping rather than summer shopping.
  3. Grow fewer plants of more types. Two courgette plants, not five. Nobody has ever regretted planting fewer courgettes.

Questions people ask

How much can I realistically save? On a few containers and a small bed, focused on herbs, salad and soft fruit, roughly £100 to £300 in year one after costs, and more in year two when the setup is paid for. A well-run full allotment can do considerably better, but it is also a serious time commitment.

Is it cheaper to grow from seed or buy plants? Seed is far cheaper per plant and gives you variety choice. Small plants from a garden centre are better if you only want two of something, which is usually true of tomatoes and courgettes.

Do I need a greenhouse? No. It extends the season and helps with tomatoes and chillies, but it is a large cost that takes years to repay in produce. A cheap plastic growhouse against a sunny wall does most of the job.

Is peat-free compost worse? It behaves differently rather than badly. It dries out faster and needs feeding sooner. Water more often and less deeply, and add a liquid feed from about six weeks.

The verdict

Grow the expensive, perishable things and buy the cheap, storable ones. Herbs, salad, beans, courgettes and soft fruit will genuinely take money off your shopping bill. Potatoes and onions will not. Do it because you enjoy it, and treat the saving as a real but modest bonus rather than the reason.
