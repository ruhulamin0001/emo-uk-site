Professional Floor Insulation Takes 25 To 45 Years To Pay Back. Lift The Boards Yourself And It Is Under Eight. Fill The Gaps And It Is One Winter.

About ten per cent of the heat in an uninsulated house leaves through the ground floor, and if that floor is timber on joists over a void, the cold comes up through every gap between the boards. The Energy Saving Trust prices the professional fix at £1,400 to £2,500 and the saving at £55 a year for a semi, which is a payback most people would not accept. But this is the one insulation job that is genuinely DIY, and the DIY sum is a different sum.

1. The figures: £1,400 to £2,500 for £40 to £100 a year

The Energy Saving Trust's floor insulation page, updated 19 May 2026 and read again on 5 September 2026, says a typical suspended floor installation could cost between £1,400 and £2,500 depending on house type, and that solid floor insulation could cost considerably more. Its embedded savings table, at July 2026 fuel prices for a gas-heated home, gives £85 a year for a detached house, £55 for a semi, £40 for a mid-terrace and £100 for a detached bungalow. The average it quotes is £55 in Great Britain and £70 in Northern Ireland. No figure is given for a flat, because a flat above another flat has no cold floor to insulate.

Divide the cost range by the saving and a semi pays back in 25 to 45 years, a terrace in 35 to 62, a detached house in 16 to 29 and a bungalow in 14 to 25. The bungalow is the outlier for a good reason: every square metre of its floor is ground floor over a cold void, so it loses more heat that way and gains more from stopping it. A two-storey house loses proportionally more through walls and roof, which is why cavity wall insulation and loft insulation come first in every sensible order of works.

That puts professional floor insulation alongside double glazing: a comfort or renovation job, not a bills one. What makes floors different is that the Energy Saving Trust says it may be possible and more affordable to insulate a suspended timber floor yourself. For walls it says hire an installer. That changes the sum.

3. Which floor you have, and why concrete changes everything

Houses built from the 1850s to the 1950s, which is most Victorian, Edwardian and interwar stock, usually have suspended timber ground floors: boards on joists over a void, ventilated by airbricks in the outside walls below floor level. If you can see airbricks, you almost certainly have one. If you have a cellar or a basement you can get into, look up: joists and the underside of boards confirm it.

Houses built from the 1950s onwards mostly have solid concrete ground floors, and anything built since the early 2000s will already have insulation in the slab under building regulations. Some older houses have stone flags, tiles laid on earth or limecrete, which are solid, uninsulated and need specialist advice, especially in listed buildings where the floor is part of what is listed.

No airbricks and no cellar? Lift a corner of carpet downstairs. Boards with gaps: timber. A flat grey screed: concrete. The next section applies to timber only.

4. Doing it yourself: from below, from above, and the £20 version

From below, if you can get under. A cellar or a crawl space with room to work is the easy case. Check the joists first for damp, wet rot or dry rot; if any joist is soft, dark or smells of mushrooms, stop and get a professional to fix the moisture before any insulation goes near it. Sound joists: push mineral wool up between them, cut slightly over-width so it holds by friction, and staple netting across the underside of the joists to keep it there. Rigid foam boards cut to fit are the alternative, and need to fit tight, because the Energy Saving Trust is clear that gaps trap moisture and dramatically reduce the effect. Leave the airbricks and the space below them completely clear.

From above, if you cannot. Lift the boards carefully, numbering them with chalk so they go back where they came from. Drape netting or a breathable membrane over the joists into the gaps, lay the mineral wool in, and relay the boards. This is the version that takes a weekend per room, breaks a few boards, and is why professionals charge £1,400 and up. The Energy Saving Trust says only attempt it if you are confident, and that is fair; the skill is not in the insulation, it is in getting old boards up and down without splitting them.

The £20 version. Most of what you feel from a timber floor is not conducted heat loss, it is air moving up through the gaps between the boards and under the skirting. A flexible decorator's caulk or a purpose-made floorboard gap filler along the skirting line and in any gap you can see daylight through stops that for about £15 to £30 a house. It has to flex, because floors move; a hard filler cracks out in a season. The Energy Saving Trust counts floor gaps within its £55 a year whole-house draught proofing saving, and our draught proofing article puts the floor share of that at perhaps £5 to £10 a year. On £20 of filler, that is one winter.

Do the £20 version this autumn regardless. Do the full DIY version if the joists are sound and you have a cellar or the patience. Pay a professional only for a bungalow, or when the floor is up for rewiring or plumbing anyway.

5. Ventilation, damp and the building regulations

The void under a suspended timber floor is ventilated for a reason: the airbricks keep the joists dry. Insulating between the joists changes how air and moisture move down there, and the Energy Saving Trust warns that plasterboarding the underside of the joists to make a tidy cellar ceiling can trap moisture against the timber unless it is fully dry and well ventilated. Keep the airbricks clear, keep the netting or membrane vapour-permeable, and if the void is already damp, fix that first.

If you replace half or more of a floor, building regulations require you to insulate it to the current standard: a U-value of 0.25 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland and 0.18 in Scotland, which in practice means at least 70 mm of high-performance rigid foam or around 150 mm of mineral wool. That applies to a professional and to you, and the legal responsibility is the homeowner's. For a straightforward retrofit between existing joists you will not usually need approval, but the local building control office will tell you for free, and photographs of the insulation before the boards go back are worth keeping for the next EPC, because once the floor is down nobody can see what you did.

6. Worked example: Owen in Cardiff

Two-bed 1900s mid-terrace, suspended timber ground floor with a coal-cellar hatch under the stairs, gas central heating, loft insulated to 270 mm, cavity check done: solid walls

Owen's living room floor is cold enough that the dog will not lie on it in January, and the curtains move on still days. A local firm quotes £1,650 to lift and insulate the two downstairs rooms and the hall.

The professional sum. Energy Saving Trust saving for a mid-terrace: £40 a year. £1,650 divided by £40 is 41 years. No.

The cellar. The coal cellar gives him access to the underside of the living room and hall, about 22 square metres. He checks the joists with a screwdriver: sound, dry, no smell. He buys 100 mm mineral wool, 30 metres of netting, a box of staples and a tube of flexible filler: £140. One Saturday from below for the front rooms.

The kitchen. No cellar access and a tiled floor on boards. He leaves it, and runs filler along the skirting there instead.

The result. About two thirds of the ground floor insulated, all the gaps sealed. He credits himself with perhaps £30 of the £40 a year plus the floor share of the draught saving, call it £35 to £40 a year for £140: about four years. The dog goes back on the floor in the second week of November. He keeps the airbricks clear and photographs the netting before he loses access to the cellar behind the new boiler.

7. The honest verdict

Do not pay £1,400 to £2,500 to insulate a suspended timber floor for the bills; at £40 to £100 a year it takes 14 years in the best case, a bungalow, and up to 60 in a terrace. Do seal the gaps between the boards and along the skirting this autumn, for about £20 and a payback of one winter. And if you have a suspended floor with sound joists and either a cellar or a free weekend, insulate it yourself for £250 to £450 in materials and a payback of four to eight years, which is better than most things on this site.

Concrete floors are a renovation job, not an energy one: insulate them when they are being replaced, not before. Whatever you do, leave the airbricks alone.

Frequently asked

How much does floor insulation cost in the UK? The Energy Saving Trust, page updated 19 May 2026, puts a typical suspended timber floor installation at between £1,400 and £2,500 depending on house type, and says solid concrete floor insulation could cost considerably more. Doing a suspended floor yourself typically costs £200 to £600 in mineral wool, netting and fixings.

How much does floor insulation save per year? At July 2026 fuel prices for a gas-heated home the Energy Saving Trust puts it at £100 a year for a detached bungalow, £85 for a detached house, £55 for a semi and £40 for a mid-terrace, with a GB average of £55 and £70 in Northern Ireland. Flats above other flats have no cold ground floor and save nothing.

Can I insulate my floor myself? A suspended timber floor, yes, if the joists are sound and you are confident lifting boards or working from a cellar; the Energy Saving Trust says it may be possible and more affordable to do it yourself. Concrete floors need rigid boards on top or under a new slab and are a renovation job. Always keep airbricks clear and fix any damp before insulating.

Do I need building regulations approval to insulate a floor? If you replace 50 per cent or more of a floor you must insulate it to the current standard, a U-value of 0.25 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland and 0.18 in Scotland, roughly 70 mm of rigid foam or 150 mm of mineral wool. Retrofitting between existing joists usually does not need approval, but your local building control office will confirm, and the legal responsibility is yours as the homeowner.
