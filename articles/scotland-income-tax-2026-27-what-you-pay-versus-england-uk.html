Six Scottish Tax Bands Against Three: Where the Crossover Actually Sits

Scotland sets its own income tax rates on earnings and has done for years, but the shape of the difference is widely misunderstood. It is not that Scottish taxpayers always pay more. On lower salaries they pay slightly less. The crossover point matters, and so does the size of the gap once you are past it.

The short version

  • Scotland has six bands for 2026-27: 19%, 20%, 21%, 42%, 45% and 48%.
  • England, Wales and Northern Ireland have three: 20%, 40% and 45%.
  • On £30,000 a Scottish taxpayer pays about £35 less a year. The starter rate is genuinely cheaper.
  • On £50,000 they pay about £1,496 more, because the 42% higher rate starts far earlier.
  • On £80,000 the gap is about £2,300 a year.
  • The personal allowance is UK-wide at £12,570, and so is National Insurance. Only the rates and bands on earnings differ.

1. The bands, in salary terms

HMRC publishes the Scottish bands as amounts above the PAYE threshold, which is how payroll uses them but not how anyone thinks about their salary. Converted into salary terms, using the UK-wide personal allowance of £12,570, the 2026 to 2027 Scottish bands are:

Starter rate 19%: £12,571 to £16,537

Basic rate 20%: £16,538 to £29,526

Intermediate rate 21%: £29,527 to £43,662

Higher rate 42%: £43,663 to £75,000

Advanced rate 45%: £75,001 to £125,140

Top rate 48%: above £125,140

Against that, England, Wales and Northern Ireland run three bands above the same personal allowance: 20% on the first £37,700 of taxable income, 40% from £37,701 to £125,140, and 45% above £125,140. In salary terms the 40% rate starts at £50,271.

That last comparison is the whole story in one line. Scotland's 42% rate starts at £43,663. The English 40% rate starts at £50,271. There is a £6,608 stretch of salary where a Scottish taxpayer is paying 42% and someone on identical pay elsewhere in the UK is paying 20%.

2. Three salaries through both systems

Income tax only. National Insurance and student loan deductions are identical across the UK and are excluded so the comparison is clean.

On £30,000. England £3,486. Scotland £3,451. Scotland is £35 a year cheaper.

On £50,000. England £7,486. Scotland £8,982. Scotland is £1,496 a year more.

On £80,000. England £19,432. Scotland £21,732. Scotland is £2,300 a year more.

Two observations. The saving at the bottom is real but small, a consequence of the 19% starter band applying to a narrow slice of income. The cost at the top arrives quickly and then grows steadily rather than explosively, because above £43,663 the Scottish taxpayer is simply paying two percentage points more on a band that started earlier.

3. Where the crossover sits, and why

Below roughly £30,000 a Scottish taxpayer pays marginally less. Between £30,000 and £43,662 the difference is small in both directions, because the 21% intermediate rate is only a point above the English basic rate. From £43,663 the gap opens sharply, and that one threshold does almost all the work.

The practical effect is a band of salaries where a pay rise is worth noticeably less in Scotland. Crossing £43,663 there means a marginal income tax rate of 42%, and with employee National Insurance at 8% up to the upper earnings limit of £50,270, the combined marginal deduction on that stretch is 50%. The same salary in England is taxed at 20% plus 8%.

That is worth knowing before you conclude a promotion did not land properly, and it is the strongest argument in Scotland for looking at pension contributions through salary sacrifice, which reduce the earnings the higher rate applies to.

4. Worked example: Fiona in Dundee and Mark in Carlisle

Fiona, Dundee, and Mark, Carlisle. Same employer, same job title, both on £50,000. Their payslips differ because Fiona is a Scottish taxpayer and Mark is not.

Mark's income tax. Taxable income £37,430, all within the £37,700 basic rate band. £7,486.

Fiona's income tax. The same £37,430 split across four bands: 19% on the first £3,967, 20% on the next £12,989, 21% on the next £14,136, and 42% on the final £6,338. £8,982.

Difference: £1,496 a year, about £125 a month, on identical gross pay.

The part that surprises them both. Their National Insurance is identical, because NI is not devolved. So is any student loan deduction. The entire difference sits in one line of the payslip.

What Fiona can do about it. A £6,338 salary sacrifice pension contribution would take her exactly out of the 42% band. She would be saving at 42% rather than the 40% Mark saves at, so pension contributions are worth more to her than to him, which is the flip side of the same rule.

5. What is not devolved, and catches people out

The Scottish rates are narrower in scope than most people assume. Four things to keep straight:

  • The personal allowance is UK-wide. £12,570 for 2026 to 2027, and it tapers away above £100,000 on the same basis across the UK.
  • National Insurance is UK-wide. 8% between the primary threshold and the upper earnings limit, 2% above, wherever you live.
  • Savings and dividend income are taxed at UK rates, even for a Scottish taxpayer. The Scottish rates apply to earnings, pensions and property income, not to interest or dividends.
  • It depends on where you live, not where you work. Scottish taxpayer status follows your main residence. Commuting across the border does not change it, and moving does, which is a real payroll issue people forget to report.

The marker for it is your tax code. A Scottish taxpayer's code begins with S. If you have moved in either direction and your code has not changed, that is worth raising, because the correction arrives eventually and it arrives as a lump.

The honest verdict

The honest summary is that Scotland is slightly cheaper at the bottom, roughly neutral in the middle, and clearly more expensive above £43,663, with the gap around £1,500 at £50,000 and £2,300 at £80,000.

What is worth acting on is narrow. If you are a Scottish taxpayer earning between £43,663 and £50,270, your marginal rate on that slice is 50% once National Insurance is counted, which makes pension contributions unusually efficient and makes it worth understanding before you evaluate a pay rise. If you have moved across the border in either direction, check your tax code starts with the right letter. Beyond those two things, this is a difference to understand rather than to plan around, and it is not a reason on its own to live anywhere.

Frequently asked

Does it depend on where I work or where I live? Where you live. Scottish taxpayer status is based on your main place of residence, not your employer's location. If you move across the border you should tell HMRC, because your tax code needs to change and corrections applied later arrive as a lump sum.

Is my National Insurance different in Scotland? No. National Insurance is not devolved and is identical across the UK: 8% between the primary threshold of £12,570 and the upper earnings limit of £50,270, and 2% above. Student loan thresholds are also UK-wide, though the Scottish student loan plan, Plan 4, has its own threshold.

Do the Scottish rates apply to my savings interest? No. Scottish rates apply to earnings, pensions and property income. Savings interest and dividends are taxed at UK-wide rates regardless of where you live.

How do I know if I am being taxed as a Scottish taxpayer? Your tax code begins with the letter S. If it does not and you live in Scotland, or it does and you have moved away, that needs correcting with HMRC.
