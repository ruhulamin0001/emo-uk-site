Side Hustles

The £1,000 Allowance Is on Sales, Not Profit. That One Word Catches More People Than Any Other Rule.

You sell a few things on Vinted. You do some freelance work at weekends. Maybe you walk dogs for neighbours. At some point one question shows up, and the answer is simpler than most people fear, provided you understand the difference between clearing your wardrobe and running a business.

1. The £1,000 rule, and the word that catches people

Every person in the UK gets a trading allowance of £1,000 per tax year. The tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April.

If your total side income stays at £1,000 or below, you owe no tax on it and you do not need to tell HMRC anything. No forms, no registration, nothing.

Two details catch people out, and the first one is the single most misunderstood rule in UK personal finance.

First, the £1,000 means your total sales, not your profit. If you sold £1,200 worth of candles and spent £500 on wax and jars, you are over the limit. Your sales were £1,200. Your profit of £700 is what gets taxed, but the threshold that decides whether you must report at all is measured on the £1,200.

Second, all your side hustles share one allowance. Freelance work, market stalls, tutoring, online selling, dog walking, it all adds up together. You do not get £1,000 per activity.

Gross, not net. The threshold is measured on money in, before you have paid for a single thing. That one word is the difference between "I made £300, I am fine" and a Self Assessment return.

2. Trading or decluttering? The distinction that decides everything

This causes the most needless panic, so here it is plainly.

Clearing out your own possessions is not trading. Selling your old wardrobe, your old phone, or unwanted gifts on Vinted is not income. You are selling personal possessions, usually for less than you paid. There is no tax on that, no matter the amount, and the £1,000 limit is not relevant to it at all.

Trading means you buy things to resell, or you make things to sell, with the aim of profit. Buying ten pairs of trainers to flip is trading. Selling your own trainers because they no longer fit is not.

What you didTrading?Does the £1,000 apply?
Cleared your wardrobe and sold 40 items for £600NoNo. Not taxable at any amount
Sold your old sofa and a bike for £450NoNo
Bought stock at car boots to resell on eBayYesYes, from the first sale
Made and sold candles, jewellery or printsYesYes
Did freelance design work at weekendsYesYes
Walked neighbours' dogs for paymentYesYes
Sold one personal painting for £8,000NoNo, but Capital Gains rules can apply above £6,000

The distinction is intent, and for a reseller it starts from the first item you deliberately sourced. If you began by selling your own things and then started buying stock, the trading starts at that point, not retrospectively.

One exception exists for single valuable items. If you sell a personal possession for more than £6,000, such as a painting, jewellery or an antique, Capital Gains Tax rules can apply. For everyday clearouts this almost never comes up.

3. Three allowances people confuse

These are separate. Using one does not consume another, and merging them in your head is how people either over-report or miss a threshold.

AllowanceAmountCovers
Trading allowance£1,000Self-employment and casual trading income of any kind, combined
Property allowance£1,000Property income such as renting a driveway, garage or storage space. Entirely separate from trading
Rent a Room scheme£7,500Letting furnished accommodation in your own main home to a lodger

So someone earning £900 from a side hustle, £800 from renting their driveway and £6,000 from a lodger is inside all three and has nothing to report. Add those three figures together and it looks like £7,700 of untaxed income, which is exactly why people get confused.

One important limitation: if you claim the trading allowance, you cannot also deduct expenses. You choose one or the other. Below about £1,000 of costs the allowance is usually better. Above it, deducting properly wins, and that crossover arrives fast once you buy equipment.

4. Over the line: registering for Self Assessment

Once your gross side income passes £1,000 in a tax year, you need to register for Self Assessment with HMRC. It takes about ten minutes on GOV.UK. You will need a Government Gateway account and your National Insurance number.

The deadline to register is 5 October after the end of the tax year in which you passed the limit. So if you crossed £1,000 during the year ending 5 April 2026, you must register by 5 October 2026.

Registering does not always mean paying. If your profit is small and your total income sits under the Personal Allowance of £12,570, you may owe nothing at all. The return still has to be filed. Note also that the Personal Allowance is frozen until at least 2031, so more people cross into tax each year without any rate changing, as covered in our guide to frozen thresholds.

The key dates once you are in the system:

DateWhat happens
5 AprilTax year ends
5 OctoberDeadline to register for Self Assessment for the year just ended
31 OctoberPaper return deadline
31 JanuaryOnline return deadline, and the tax is due
31 January and 31 JulyPayments on account, if your bill was over £1,000

The last row surprises people in year two. If your bill is over £1,000, HMRC asks for next year's tax in two instalments on top of this year's. Your first January as a self-employed person can be a bill you did not budget for.

5. What Vinted, eBay and Airbnb already tell HMRC

Since January 2024, digital platforms in the UK must report seller information to HMRC. This covers eBay, Vinted, Etsy, Depop, Airbnb, Uber, Deliveroo and similar sites.

A platform reports you when you pass either of these in a calendar year: 30 sales, or roughly £1,700 in earnings (the rule is set at 2,000 euros). They send your name, address, total earnings and transaction count. Reports go in by 31 January for the previous year.

This is not a new tax. It is a visibility tool, and reporting is not the same as taxation. Being reported does not mean you owe anything. If you were selling your own belongings, the honest answer to any HMRC letter is exactly that, and it is a complete answer.

What it does mean is that HMRC can now compare what platforms say you earned against what you declared. If those numbers do not match and you were trading, expect a letter. Guessing is no longer a strategy.

6. The £3,000 change everyone talks about

You may have seen headlines saying the threshold is rising to £3,000. Here is the accurate version.

The government has announced a plan to raise the reporting threshold from £1,000 to £3,000. When it takes effect, people earning between £1,000 and £3,000 would use a simple online service instead of a full tax return. Around 300,000 people would stop filing returns.

Two things to note, and both are routinely got wrong:

  • The change is not yet law, so the £1,000 rule still applies right now.
  • The tax-free amount stays at £1,000 either way. Between £1,000 and £3,000 you may still owe tax, you would just report it in a simpler way.

Check the current status on GOV.UK before you rely on it.

7. What you can actually deduct

If you are past the allowance and deducting expenses instead of claiming it, the test is that a cost must be wholly and exclusively for the business. Common allowable costs for a small side business:

  • Stock and materials, including the wastage you did not sell
  • Postage, packaging and courier fees
  • Platform and payment fees, such as Etsy's cut or card processing. Our Etsy fees guide breaks those down per sale
  • Travel to buy stock or see clients, at the approved mileage rate or actual cost
  • A proportion of your phone and broadband, based on genuine business use
  • Software, hosting, domains and professional subscriptions
  • Insurance such as public liability or professional indemnity
  • Use of home, either the simplified flat rate or a calculated proportion

What is not allowable: your entire laptop if you also use it personally, ordinary clothing even if you wear it for work, client entertaining, or fines and penalties. And a company structure does not widen this list, as our sole trader versus limited company guide explains.

8. Worked example: three people, three answers

Same tax year, very different positions

Leah cleared her flat. She sold 63 items on Vinted and eBay for a total of £840: old clothes, a games console, a bookcase. Vinted reported her to HMRC because she passed 30 sales. She owes nothing and needs to do nothing. These were her own possessions, so it is not trading, and the £1,000 threshold does not even apply. If HMRC writes, one sentence answers it.

Marcus started reselling. He bought stock at car boots and resold it, taking £1,450 in sales and spending £590 on stock, fuel and postage. His profit was £860. He is over the threshold on the £1,450, not the £860, so he must register by 5 October following the end of that tax year. Because his profit is small and his employed salary already uses his Personal Allowance, he will pay tax on the £860 at his marginal rate. He deducts expenses rather than claiming the allowance, because £590 of costs is more than the £1,000 allowance would shelter once you account for the sales figure.

Priya did three things at once. Tutoring brought £700, selling handmade prints brought £450, and she rented her driveway for £600. She assumed she was fine on all three. She is not. The tutoring and the prints are both trading and share one £1,000 allowance, so £1,150 of trading income puts her over. The driveway sits in the separate property allowance and is fine. She must register, on the strength of two hobbies she thought were too small to count.

The pattern: the person selling the most money's worth of goods owed nothing, and the person who felt smallest is the one who had to register. Gross versus net, and one allowance versus several, decide it.

9. What happens as it grows: NI, VAT and MTD

Three further thresholds arrive later, and it helps to know they exist before you meet them.

  • National Insurance. Class 4 is charged on profits above £12,570. Class 2 is £3.65 a week for 2026 to 2027 and is voluntary for most self-employed people above the small profits threshold, though paying it protects your State Pension record.
  • VAT. Registration is compulsory once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in any rolling 12 month period. Not per tax year, rolling, so it can arrive mid-month if you are growing. One important exception: if you dropship low-value consignments direct to GB customers, you may need to register from your first sale regardless of turnover, as our dropshipping guide explains.
  • Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. Live since 6 April 2026 for sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000. The threshold drops to £30,000 from April 2027 and £20,000 from April 2028. Qualifying income is gross turnover before expenses, the same trap as the £1,000 rule. 10. A 60 second self check

  1. Am I selling my own used possessions? If yes, relax. No tax, no forms, whatever the amount.
  2. Am I making or buying things to sell, or doing paid work on the side? If yes, add up your total sales for the tax year, across every activity, before costs.
  3. Is that total over £1,000? If yes, register for Self Assessment by 5 October following the end of that tax year. If no, you are done.
  4. Is any of it property income? That has its own separate £1,000, and a lodger has a separate £7,500.

Keep simple records either way. A spreadsheet with dates, amounts and costs takes minutes to maintain and settles any question HMRC ever asks. Reconstructing two years of it in January is miserable, and it is the single most common regret among people who started a side hustle that worked.

The honest verdict

Most people worrying about this owe nothing. If you are clearing your own clutter, you are not trading, the platforms reporting you changes nothing, and you can stop reading.

If you are actually trading, the rule is simpler than the fear: add up what came in, before costs, across everything. Over £1,000, register by 5 October. Under it, do nothing. Then open a separate account for the money and put a share of every payment aside from day one, because the bill arrives in January whether you saved for it or not.

Frequently asked

I sell on Vinted and got an HMRC letter. What do I do? If you were selling your own used possessions, reply saying so. Reporting is triggered by sales volume, not by any judgement that you were trading. Keep a note of what you sold and roughly what you originally paid.

Does my employer find out? No. Self Assessment is between you and HMRC. Your tax code can change if HMRC collects side-hustle tax through PAYE, which an employer could in principle notice, but they are not told why. You can ask HMRC to collect it separately instead.

I made a loss. Do I still need to register? If your gross sales passed £1,000, yes. Registering also lets you record the loss, which can be set against future profits, so it is worth doing properly rather than ignoring.

What if I missed the 5 October deadline? Register as soon as you realise. Penalties are generally based on tax owed and lateness, so acting quickly limits them. Deliberately not registering is treated very differently from being late.

Do I need an accountant? Not for a small, simple side hustle. Once you are dealing with VAT, MTD, employees or a limited company, an hour with an accountant costs far less than the mistake.

Is this the same in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland? The allowances, National Insurance and VAT rules are UK-wide. Scotland sets its own income tax bands and rates, so what you pay on the profit differs there.
