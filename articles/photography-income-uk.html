Making Money From Photography in the UK: The Real Routes

21 July 2026 · 6 min read · Checked July 2026

The short version
  • The UK average wedding photography spend is £1,484, with most full day bookings landing between £1,500 and £2,500. London and the South East run £200 to £500 higher.
  • Stock photography pays in pennies. A Shutterstock download averages about £0.60, and Adobe Stock subscription downloads pay roughly £0.26 to £0.78. A 1,000 image portfolio typically returns £80 to £300 a month.
  • Weddings are where the money is and where the risk is. One Saturday can be a month of stock income, and one botched Saturday can be a lawsuit.
  • The gap between a £600 photographer and a £2,400 photographer is almost never the camera. It is insurance, backups, a second shooter, editing hours and the ability to survive a complaint.
  • If you want money this quarter, do local commercial work, not weddings and not stock.

Notice the shape. The routes that pay quickly are the ones nobody dreams about. The routes people dream about, weddings and stock, are the slowest to start and the most oversupplied.

2. Weddings: the numbers behind the number

The headline is good. Bridebook puts the UK average wedding photography spend at £1,484 for 2026, and for a conventional full day with 80 to 150 guests the realistic figure is £2,200 to £2,800. Full day packages typically run £1,500 to £2,500.

Now the part the courses skip.

A wedding is not a day of work. Enquiry and consultation, contract and admin, venue recce, the day itself at ten to twelve hours, then culling and editing. A realistic total is 35 to 45 hours per wedding. At £2,000 that is £44 to £57 an hour before costs, which is good, not extraordinary.

It is seasonal. Roughly 70% of UK weddings fall between May and September. You will have five very hard months and a January with no income at all. Price for twelve months, earn in five.

You cannot reshoot it. This is the whole risk. A corrupted card, a failed body, an illness on the morning, and you have destroyed something irreplaceable. That is why professionals shoot to dual card slots, carry two bodies, back up to two locations before sleeping, and carry professional indemnity and public liability cover. All of that is cost, and all of it is the difference between a £600 photographer and a £2,400 one.

Start by second shooting. Assist an established photographer for £120 to £250 a day. You get real conditions, real timelines and portfolio images, and you find out whether you actually like it before anyone is depending on you.

3. Stock: the honest arithmetic

Stock photography is the most commonly recommended and most commonly misrepresented route.

Converted from published US dollar contributor rates at roughly 0.79 to the pound, August 2026. Rates change and both platforms have cut them historically.

A 1,000 image portfolio realistically returns £80 to £300 a month, and getting to 1,000 accepted images is perhaps 300 to 500 hours of shooting, editing, keywording and uploading. So the build is a year of unpaid work for a return around minimum wage, which then continues without further effort.

It works as a by-product, not a plan. If you are already shooting, uploading the outtakes costs you keywording time only. Deliberately shooting for stock, in 2026, competing with an effectively unlimited supply of generated images, is a poor use of a photographer's time.

4. Local commercial work, the underrated one

This is where most working photographers actually earn, and almost nobody writes about it because it is unglamorous.

  • Estate agents. £60 to £150 per property, an hour on site. Agents shoot constantly and value reliability over artistry.
  • Restaurants and cafes. £200 to £500 for a menu shoot. Every independent needs images for delivery apps and social.
  • Small business product photography. £15 to £40 per product on a white background, or £300 to £800 for a half day. Anyone selling on Etsy or Amazon needs this.
  • Headshots. £80 to £200 per person, or £600 to £1,500 for a team day. Solicitors, estate agents, recruiters and accountants all refresh these.
  • Local sport and school events. Reliable, repeatable, and nobody is competing hard for it.

The route in is straightforward and unpleasant: walk into twenty independent businesses with a printed sample and a price. Email works less well than turning up.

6. What kit you actually need

Less than you think for everything except weddings, where the requirement is not quality but redundancy.

  • Portraits, product, estate agents: any modern body, one sharp prime, one wide zoom, a tripod and a couple of speedlights. A used full frame body from five years ago is entirely sufficient.
  • Weddings: two bodies, both with dual card slots, two lenses that cover the range, two flashes, and enough batteries and cards that a failure is an inconvenience rather than a catastrophe. This is not preference, it is the professional minimum.
  • Everything: a colour calibrated screen, and a backup routine you follow the same night, every time. Two copies in two places before you sleep.

You can browse used bodies and lenses on Amazon UK, though for used gear the specialist dealers usually offer better condition grading and a warranty. As an Amazon Associate we earn from qualifying purchases.

7. Insurance, contracts and copyright

  • Public liability insurance is required by most venues before they will let you work. £60 to £150 a year for a solo photographer.
  • Professional indemnity covers you when the work itself goes wrong. Essential for weddings.
  • Equipment cover is separate. Your home contents policy almost certainly excludes commercial use.
  • You own the copyright in the UK as the photographer, unless you sign it away or you are an employee. Clients get a licence, not ownership, and that distinction should be written into every contract.
  • Model releases are needed for stock and for most commercial use of recognisable people. Property releases for private buildings.
  • Photographing children at schools and clubs needs written parental consent and often a DBS check.
  • Data protection. You are holding personal data. Have a retention policy and a way to delete images on request.

Myths that cost people money

"I need better gear before I can charge." Clients cannot tell what body shot the image. They can tell whether you turned up on time, whether the files arrived when promised and whether the light was any good. Buy gear when a specific job requires it.

"Do a few free shoots to build a portfolio." Two, maybe. Beyond that you are training a local market to expect free photography, and free clients are consistently the most demanding. Charge £40 instead of nothing. The relationship changes completely.

"Stock photography is passive income." At roughly £0.30 to £0.78 a download, a month's income needs hundreds of downloads, which needs a portfolio that took you a year to build. It is passive in the same sense that a pension is passive.

The honest verdict

This helps you if you will do unglamorous commercial work, you can sell to local businesses face to face, and you treat weddings as something to grow into via second shooting rather than start with. On that path a part time £900 to £1,200 a month is realistic inside a year.

This does not help you if your plan is stock libraries, or shooting only what you find beautiful. The market pays for solving a business problem, and a landscape at golden hour is not a business problem.

There is genuinely nothing to buy to get started here. If you own a camera and can drive, you have everything you need for the first £500.

Frequently asked

Do I need to register as self-employed? Yes, once your income from it passes the £1,000 trading allowance in a tax year. Register with HMRC by 5 October after the end of that tax year.

Should I do a photography course? A short business of photography course is often worth more than a technical one. The technical side is free on YouTube. Pricing, contracts and client management are what people actually get wrong.

What should I charge for my first paid job? Work out your minimum acceptable hourly figure, multiply by the realistic total hours including editing, and do not go below it. For a first headshot session that is usually £80 to £120, not £25.

Is AI killing photography work? It has hollowed out generic stock badly. It has barely touched anything requiring a real person, a real place or a real event, which is where the paying work is. Weddings, headshots, property and food are all safe for now.
