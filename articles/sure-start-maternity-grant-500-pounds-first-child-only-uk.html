£500 for a new baby: the grant nobody claims, and the test everyone gets wrong

The Sure Start Maternity Grant is a one-off £500 you never have to pay back and that does not touch your other benefits. Almost every guide describes it as being for your first child. That is not what the rule says, and the difference decides real claims.

The short version

  • £500, once, and you do not pay it back. GOV.UK also confirms it "will not affect your other benefits" (Sure Start Maternity Grant, read 6 September 2026).
  • The real test is that you have no other children under 16, not that this is your first baby. An older child of 16 or over does not block a claim.
  • You or your partner must get income-related ESA, Pension Credit or Universal Credit, or a Support for Mortgage Interest loan. That list is shorter than most guides still print.
  • Twins and triplets have their own table. Already have a child and expecting triplets, and it is £1,000. Already had twins and expecting twins, and it is nothing.
  • Claim from 11 weeks before the due date until 6 months after the birth. Miss it and there is no late route.

1. What it is, and what it is not

The Sure Start Maternity Grant is a one-off payment of £500 to help with the costs of having a child. GOV.UK's own summary: "You do not have to pay the grant back and it will not affect your other benefits."

Read that second half again, because it removes the objection most people raise. The grant is not counted as income, it does not reduce your Universal Credit, and it does not come off anything else. It is not a loan and there is no clawback.

It is not available in Scotland. If you live in Scotland you apply for the Pregnancy and Baby Payment through mygov.scot instead, which is a separate Scottish Government scheme with its own rules and its own amounts. Do not spend an afternoon on the SF100 form if you live in Glasgow.

The claim window is fixed and short. You must claim within 11 weeks of the baby's due date or within 6 months after the birth. There is no discretion to accept a late claim because you did not know the grant existed, and not knowing it existed is the single most common reason it goes unclaimed.

You claim on form SF100, and part of it has to be signed by a health professional: a midwife, a doctor or a health visitor. That is worth planning for, because it means the form cannot be finished in one sitting at the kitchen table. Take it to an appointment.

2. The test is not "first child"

Almost every guide, including the plain English summary on GOV.UK's own overview page, describes this as a grant for your first child. The eligibility page is more precise, and the precise version is the one that decides claims.

GOV.UK, Sure Start Maternity Grant eligibility: "Usually, to get a Sure Start Maternity Grant you must have no other children under 16."

That is a different test, and it is more generous than "first child" in one very common situation. If your eldest is 17 and you are expecting a baby, this is not your first child, but you have no other children under 16, so on that limb you qualify. A family with a gap of sixteen years or more between children is not unusual and is routinely told, wrongly, that the grant is not for them.

It is also worth being clear about what the test is not. It is not about whether you have given birth before. It is about the children currently in your household who are under 16.

If you do have children under 16, GOV.UK lists several routes back in. You may still get a grant if you or your partner get one of the qualifying benefits and any of these apply:

  • you are expecting a multiple birth, such as twins
  • the child you are caring for is someone else's, but not your partner's, and was over 12 months old when the arrangement started
  • you have refugee status or humanitarian protection, or you came to the UK from Afghanistan or Ukraine
  • you are claiming for a family member who is under 16, or 16 to 19 and in certain education or training

That third route has specific tests attached. GOV.UK says you can get a grant for your first child born in the UK if you have refugee status or humanitarian protection, or you left Afghanistan because of the collapse of the Afghan government on 15 August 2021, or you were resident in Ukraine immediately before 1 January 2022 and left because of the Russian invasion on 24 February 2022.

The fourth route is the one people never think of. If a family member who lives with you is having their first child, and that family member is under 16, or is 16 to 19 and in approved education or training, you can claim for them. Approved education means more than an average of 12 hours a week of supervised study or course related work experience, and covers A levels, T levels, NVQs and other vocational qualifications up to level 3, home education that started before they turned 16, and traineeships in England. Courses paid for by an employer do not count, and neither do advanced courses such as a degree or a BTEC Higher National Certificate.

And if you are not giving birth at all. You may still qualify if you are adopting, have a parental order for a surrogate birth, have been appointed guardian, or have an adoption or residence order. In those cases the baby must be less than 1 year old on the date you claim.

3. The qualifying benefits, and what dropped off the list

You or your partner must already be getting one of these:

  • income-related Employment and Support Allowance
  • Pension Credit
  • Universal Credit

You may also qualify if you are getting a Support for Mortgage Interest loan.

That is the complete list on GOV.UK as read on 6 September 2026, and it is shorter than the version still printed on a lot of parenting sites. Child Tax Credit and Working Tax Credit used to be on it. Tax credits have ended, and with them that route in. Income Support and income-based Jobseeker's Allowance are not on the current list either.

The practical consequence is straightforward: for most families the way in is Universal Credit. If you are on a low income, pregnant, and not claiming Universal Credit, a Universal Credit claim is the thing that unlocks this grant, along with several others. It is worth running a benefits calculator before the 11 week window opens rather than after the six month one closes.

Notice what is not on the list of tests. There is no savings limit specific to this grant, no separate income assessment, and no requirement to have worked. The means test is done once, by the qualifying benefit. If you have that benefit and you pass the children test, you get the £500.

4. Twins, triplets and the table nobody prints

If you already have children under 16 and you are expecting a multiple birth, the amount depends on what you already have. GOV.UK publishes it as a table, and it is worth reproducing exactly because it is not intuitive.

Children under 16 you already haveGrant if you have twinsGrant if you have triplets
1 or more, and none of them from a multiple birth£500£1,000
You have already had twins£0£500
You have already had triplets£0£0

The logic underneath it is that the grant is meant to cover the equipment you do not already own. A household that has already had twins already owns two of everything, so a second set of twins brings nothing. A household that has already had twins and is now expecting triplets is one baby further along than last time, and gets £500 for that one.

Two things follow.

  • £1,000 is possible. One existing child, none from a multiple birth, expecting triplets. That is the maximum the table produces.
  • A nil answer is a real answer. If you have already had triplets, expecting triplets again produces nothing, and no amount of appealing changes the table.

5. Farida in Oldham: the claim she nearly did not make

Farida is 38 and lives in Oldham with her husband and her son, who is 17 and in the second year of a T level. The household claims Universal Credit. She is expecting a baby in December.

Every summary she read said the grant was for a first child. This is her second. She assumed she was out.

What the rule actually asks is whether she has any other children under 16. Her son is 17. She does not. On that limb, she qualifies.

The benefit test: the household gets Universal Credit, which is on the list.

The window: her due date is 14 December, so she can claim from 11 weeks before that, which is 28 September, and at the latest until 14 June the following year, six months after the birth.

The outcome: £500, paid into the account her benefits go into, not repayable, and with no effect on her Universal Credit.

Now change one fact. If her son were 15 rather than 17, she would have another child under 16 and the ordinary route would close. She would then need one of the exceptions: a multiple birth, a refugee or humanitarian protection route, caring for someone else's child, or claiming for a family member. On the facts as they are, none of those apply, and the answer would be no.

That is a single year of a teenager's age deciding £500, which is exactly the kind of detail worth checking properly rather than taking from a summary.

One more thing Farida should do while the form is out. The grant is a one-off, but pregnancy opens several other doors at the same time: Healthy Start if she is on Universal Credit and pregnant or has a child under four, free NHS prescriptions and dental treatment during pregnancy and for a year after the birth, and Maternity Allowance if she does not qualify for Statutory Maternity Pay. None of them are automatic and all of them have their own forms.

The verdict

If you are pregnant, on Universal Credit, income-related ESA or Pension Credit, and you have no children under 16 at home, this is £500 you are entitled to and it takes one form. Do not rule yourself out because you have had a child before: the test is other children under 16, not first birth. Get the SF100 signed at a midwife or health visitor appointment rather than trying to finish it at home, and put the deadline in your phone now, because eleven weeks before the due date to six months after the birth is the whole of it and there is no late claim. If you are in Scotland, ignore all of this and apply for the Pregnancy and Baby Payment instead.

Frequently asked

Is the grant means tested on my savings? There is no savings test specific to this grant. The means test happens through the qualifying benefit, so if you already get Universal Credit, income-related ESA or Pension Credit, that part is settled. What can block a claim is not having one of those benefits at all.

Do I get anything if I already have a child under 16? Usually not, on the ordinary route. You may still qualify if you are expecting a multiple birth, if the child you care for is someone else's and was over 12 months old when the arrangement started, if you have refugee status or humanitarian protection or came from Afghanistan or Ukraine, or if you are claiming for a family member who is under 16 or 16 to 19 and in approved education.

What if I miss the six month deadline? There is no late claim route on the GOV.UK guidance. The window is 11 weeks before the due date to 6 months after the birth. If you are close to the end of it, get the form submitted and sort out any missing detail afterwards rather than waiting until it is complete.

Can I get it if I am adopting or using a surrogate? Yes, in defined circumstances. The baby must be less than 1 year old on the date you claim, you must be getting one of the qualifying benefits, and one of GOV.UK's listed situations must apply, such as the baby being placed with you for adoption, a parental order for a surrogate birth, or being appointed as guardian.
