The £2.99 Hosting Deal Wants Four Years Up Front. Here Is the Real Bill

Every guide to starting an affiliate site quotes a monthly hosting price. Almost none of them mention that the price is before VAT, that it only applies if you pay 48 months on day one, and that the free domain is free for exactly one year. This is the four-year total, worked out from the vendor's own published pages, and the amount of Amazon commission it takes to cover it.

The short version

  • The advertised price is before VAT. Hostinger's UK pages state plainly at the foot of every one of them that prices are listed without VAT. A UK consumer adds 20%. The £2.99 Premium plan is £3.59 a month in real money.
  • £2.99 a month means £143.52 in one payment. That rate only exists on a 48-month term. Including VAT you hand over £172.22 on day one, and Hostinger's refund window is 30 days.
  • Twelve months at the standard monthly rate costs almost exactly the same. Premium at £11.99 a month ex VAT is £172.66 including VAT for one year. That is 44p more than four years on the deal, for a quarter of the commitment.
  • The free domain is free for one year. A .co.uk is £0.01 in year one and £10.99 a year to renew, which is £13.19 with VAT. A .com renews at £16.99, or £20.39 with VAT.
  • Four-year total on the popular route: £211.79. That is £4.41 a month, not £2.99. At Amazon UK's 3% Home rate you need roughly £7,060 of tracked sales over those four years just to stand still.
  • The honest floor is about £13 a year. A static site on a free tier plus a domain comes to roughly £39.58 over four years. Amazon does not care where your site is hosted.

1. What the advertised price actually buys

Take the most commonly recommended budget host in the UK. On 29 August 2026 its UK hosting page advertises four shared plans. Here is what each headline price is attached to, taken directly from the small print on the same page.

Single, £1.49 a month. 48-month term, £71.52 in one payment. Renews at £7.99 a month. One website.

Premium, £2.99 a month. 48-month term, £143.52 in one payment. Renews at £10.99 a month. Three websites, domain free for one year.

Unlimited, £3.99 a month. 48-month term, £191.52 in one payment. Renews at £13.99 a month.

Cloud Startup, £7.99 a month. 48-month term, £383.52 in one payment. Renews at £20.99 a month.

Every figure above is excluding VAT, as stated at the foot of the page.

Three things are being compressed into that one number, and it is worth separating them out.

VAT. UK domestic consumers pay 20% on web hosting. The page is priced for a VAT-registered business that can reclaim it. If you are an individual starting a first site, you cannot, so every figure above needs multiplying by 1.2. £2.99 becomes £3.59. £143.52 becomes £172.22.

The term. The monthly figure is arithmetic, not a billing arrangement. The page says so: the monthly rate reflects the total plan price divided by the number of months. You are not paying £2.99 a month. You are paying £172.22 once and dividing it by 48 in your head.

The renewal. Premium renews at £10.99 a month ex VAT, which is £13.19 with VAT, or £158.26 for a year. That is 3.7 times the promotional rate. The promotion is not a discount on an ongoing price. It is a different price that lasts four years and then stops.

Twelve months at the standard monthly rate is £172.66 including VAT. Forty-eight months on the deal is £172.22. The saving is 44 pence, and the cost is a four-year commitment.

That comparison is the one nobody runs, and it is the most useful thing on this page. The 48-month deal is not saving you money against a one-year commitment. It is selling you three extra years at a price you would have paid anyway for the first one. If you are confident you will still be writing in 2030, that is a fine trade. If this is your first site and you have not yet published anything, it is not.

2. The domain is the cost that never goes away

The free domain is real, and it is free for one year. After that you own an annual bill that continues for as long as the site exists, and it continues even if you stop paying for hosting.

.co.uk — £0.01 for the first year, then £10.99 a year to renew. With VAT: £13.19 a year.

.com — £0.01 for the first year, then £16.99 a year to renew. With VAT: £20.39 a year.

Both figures are from the registrar's own published .co.uk and TLD pages on 29 August 2026, and both exclude VAT on the page.

Two practical notes. A .co.uk can only be registered for one to three years at a time, so you cannot lock a long price the way you can with hosting. And the plan that most beginners are pushed towards, the cheapest Single plan, is excluded from the free domain offer in the vendor's own FAQ, even though the plan card lists a free domain among its features. Those two statements sit on the same page and contradict each other. Read your basket total at checkout rather than the feature list.

3. Worked example: Priya in Watford, four years

Priya works in a garden centre in Watford and wants to build a review site about UK garden tools. She takes the advice she has read everywhere: the Premium plan on the 48-month term, with the free .co.uk.

Day one. £143.52 ex VAT for 48 months of hosting, which is £172.22 including VAT, taken as a single payment. Domain: £0.01. Total out of her account in week one: £172.22.

Year 2. Hosting already paid. Domain renewal £10.99 ex VAT = £13.19.

Year 3. £13.19. Year 4. £13.19.

Four-year total: £211.79. Divided by 48 months that is £4.41 a month, against an advertised £2.99. The advert understates her actual monthly cost by 48%.

And in month 49, hosting renews at £13.19 a month with VAT, so year five alone costs £158.26 plus £13.19 for the domain, which is £171.45 for one year.

Now the version nobody writes about. Suppose Priya publishes eleven articles, gets bored in month seven, and stops. She has consumed roughly £25 of hosting and paid £172.22 for it. The refund window closed in month two. That is not a scandal, it is how prepaid terms work, but it is the realistic outcome for most first sites and it belongs in the decision.

4. How much commission it takes to break even

This is the arithmetic that turns a hosting bill into a business decision. Amazon UK's Associates programme pays a percentage of the sale price, and across most UK categories that percentage sits between 1% and 4%. Home is 3%. We are using 3% throughout because it is the rate most general review sites will actually see.

Priya's four-year cost: £211.79. At 3%, she needs £7,060 of tracked qualifying sales over four years to cover it. That is roughly £1,765 of sales a year, every year.

A single year on the cheapest plan after renewal: hosting £115.06 plus domain £13.19 = £128.25. At 3% that is £4,275 of tracked sales in twelve months.

Per order. On a £40 basket, 3% is £1.20. Covering £128.25 takes 107 orders, which is about nine every month without a gap. The £40 basket is our assumption, not a published figure, and your niche will differ enormously.

Then there is the payment threshold. Amazon UK does not pay out until your balance passes £25, and payment arrives roughly 60 days after the end of the month the commission was earned. At £1.20 a sale that is 21 qualifying orders before any money reaches you at all, and then a two-month wait on top. A site that generates six sales a month is not a site earning £7.20 a month. It is a site holding a balance it cannot withdraw.

One more figure worth carrying. Amazon's cookie is 24 hours, so a click today that converts on Thursday pays you nothing. Every break-even number above assumes same-day conversion, which means the real requirement is higher than what is written here, not lower. We have deliberately not guessed at click-through or conversion rates, because those depend entirely on your niche and inventing them would make this page look more precise than it is.

5. The honest floor, and when paying more is right

Amazon does not inspect your hosting. Neither does Awin, nor any network we are aware of. What they check is that the site exists, has real content, and discloses the relationship. A static site served from a free tier satisfies all three.

The floor. A static site on a free hosting tier, plus a .co.uk. Year one £0.01, years two to four £13.19 each. Four-year total: £39.58.

Against the popular route: £211.79. The difference is £172.21 over four years, and it buys you WordPress, a control panel and email, not visibility.

Paying for managed hosting is a reasonable choice for one specific reason: if you are not comfortable with a build step or a Git push, a free static tier will cost you evenings, and evenings are the scarce resource when you have a job. That is a genuine trade and it is worth £170 to plenty of people. What it is not is a requirement, and nobody who tells you it is a requirement is disinterested. Almost every article recommending a host earns a commission on the sign-up, frequently £50 or more per sale, which is precisely why the 48-month plan is the one you keep seeing recommended.

We earn nothing from anything named on this page. There is no affiliate link in this article, because we are not in any of these programmes and adding one would defeat the point of the article.

Finally, tax. If your affiliate income is under £1,000 gross in a tax year the trading allowance means you generally have nothing to report. But if you claim the allowance you cannot also deduct your costs, and the two are alternatives, not extras. On £600 of income and £128 of hosting, the allowance wipes out all £600 and is clearly better. Once income passes roughly £1,128 in that example, deducting real costs starts to win. Check your own numbers on GOV.UK rather than following a rule of thumb.

The honest verdict

Starting an affiliate site in the UK genuinely is cheap. It is about £13 a year cheap, not £2.99 a month cheap, and the gap between those two statements is where the hosting industry makes its money.

If you have not published anything yet, do not buy 48 months of anything. Register a domain, put a static site on a free tier, and write twelve articles. That costs you a penny in year one and about thirteen pounds a year after. If you are still writing in twelve months and the free tier has become the bottleneck, buy hosting then, with a year of evidence behind the decision instead of a discount code.

And run the break-even before you spend. £128 of annual cost is 107 Amazon orders at a £40 basket. If you cannot picture where those 107 orders come from, the hosting plan is not the problem you should be solving this week.

Frequently asked

Is the free domain actually free? For twelve months, yes, on the plans that include it. From month thirteen you pay the standard renewal, which is £10.99 a year plus VAT for a .co.uk and £16.99 plus VAT for a .com. Set a calendar reminder for month eleven, because auto-renewal on a card you have forgotten about is how people end up paying a premium renewal they never chose.

Can I get a refund if I change my mind? The standard offer is a 30-day money-back guarantee on hosting. Domains are usually excluded from that, because registration is passed to the registry and cannot be undone. Check the specific refund policy before you pay, not after.

Do I need to register as self-employed to run an affiliate site? Not on day one. The trading allowance covers up to £1,000 of gross trading income in a tax year. Above that, the rules on registering and reporting apply and you should read the GOV.UK guidance in full rather than a summary.

Does cheap hosting hurt my Google rankings? Not directly. Page speed is a ranking signal and a badly overloaded shared server can hurt it, but a well-built static site on a free tier is typically faster than a WordPress site on entry-level shared hosting, not slower. The reason most new sites get no traffic is not the server.
