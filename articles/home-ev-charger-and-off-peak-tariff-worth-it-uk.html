A Home EV Charger Pays Back In Two And A Half Years. Not Because Of The Box, But Because Of The £8 Charge It Lets You Buy.

Nobody saves money by owning a wallbox. The saving comes from an off-peak EV tariff that charges the car overnight at a fraction of the daytime rate, and most of those tariffs need a smart meter and a charger that can be scheduled. The Energy Saving Trust puts a 220-mile charge at about £17 on a standard rate and about £8 on an EV tariff. This article does the arithmetic on that gap, prices the box that unlocks it, and explains why the three-pin cable in the boot cannot.

1. £17, £8 and £45: what the Energy Saving Trust figures actually price

The Energy Saving Trust's electric vehicles guide, updated 21 August 2026 and read again on 5 September 2026, gives three costs for the same journey. A full charge in a fully electric car gives a typical range of around 220 miles and costs approximately £17 if charging at home. On an EV tariff or other time-of-use tariff, which the Trust says typically requires a smart meter, the same full charge costs approximately £8, which it puts at as low as 2p a mile. Driving a petrol or diesel car for the same 220 miles could cost up to £45.

The £17 implies a battery of roughly 65 kWh at about 26p a kWh, which is the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p to within rounding; the £8 implies the same battery at about 12p. Per mile that is 7.7p, 3.6p and 20.5p. Yours will differ with the car and the weather, but the ratio holds: the off-peak charge costs a bit under half the standard one.

This article is about the middle gap, £17 to £8, because that is the one a wallbox and a tariff can close. A household charging at home on a standard tariff pays roughly £9 more per full charge than it needs to; the question is what it costs to stop.

2. Payback by annual mileage, with and without the grant

Annual charging cost is the Energy Saving Trust's per-charge figure multiplied by annual miles divided by 220. The wallbox cost of around £1,000 installed is our estimate of a typical 7 kW untethered unit with a straightforward install and is not a live price; quotes run from about £800 to £1,500 depending on the cable run and whether the consumer unit needs work. The grant row uses the GOV.UK EV chargepoint grant for renters and flat owners: 75 per cent of the cost of buying and installing a socket up to £500. Running cost of the box itself is a few pounds a year of standby.

The payback is short at every realistic mileage, and it is entirely a function of the tariff. A household that fits a £1,000 wallbox and stays on a single-rate tariff saves nothing, because electricity costs the same through a wallbox as through a wall socket. The box earns the £409 only if it gets the household onto the EV tariff and charges in the window every night.

From April 2028 the eVED road charge of 3p a mile, covered in our pay-per-mile article, adds £300 a year at 10,000 miles to every EV regardless of how it is charged. It does not change the table, because it lands on both columns equally, but it is worth knowing that the £409 you keep from the tariff is roughly what the road charge will take back.

3. Why the three-pin cable cannot get you the £8 charge

Every EV comes with, or can be bought, a cable that plugs into an ordinary 13-amp socket. It draws about 2.3 kW, sometimes less on a long extension or an old circuit, and it is fine for a plug-in hybrid with a small battery or for topping up. On a 220-mile car it is not fine, for a reason of arithmetic: a battery of roughly 65 kWh at 2.3 kW takes 28 hours from empty, and even a half-full one takes 14. The off-peak windows on EV tariffs are typically five to seven hours long. A granny cable delivers 12 to 16 kWh in that window, about a fifth of the battery, and the rest of the charge lands at the daytime rate, which on some EV tariffs is higher than the standard cap rate.

A 7 kW wallbox fills the same battery in nine to ten hours from empty and puts 35 to 49 kWh in during a five to seven hour window, which for most drivers is a full day's driving and then some. It can also be scheduled, either by the box or by the car or by the tariff's own app, so the charge starts when the cheap rate starts without anyone going outside at half past midnight. Several EV tariffs require a compatible smart charger for exactly that reason; the ones that schedule through the car instead still need a charger that can deliver a meaningful charge inside the window.

There is a safety point too: 10 amps for 14 hours through a socket never rated for continuous load is how sockets melt. A wallbox is on its own protected circuit.

4. What the off-peak tariff needs from you, and the daytime catch

EV tariffs typically require a smart meter, the Energy Saving Trust says, because the supplier has to know when the electricity was used; your supplier fits one free, and that is the first step. Some tariffs then need a specific charger or a compatible car; others give a fixed overnight window and leave the scheduling to you. Read the tariff's conditions before buying the box.

The catch is what happens to the rest of the house. EV tariffs pair a cheap overnight rate with a daytime rate that is usually at or above the standard cap rate, so the household's other electricity gets a little dearer while the car gets a lot cheaper. For a typical household using the Ofgem typical figure of 2,700 kWh a year of non-car electricity, a daytime rate a penny or two above the cap costs £27 to £54 a year, which is small against the £409 but not nothing; and a household that also shifts its washing machine, dishwasher and tumble dryer into the window claws that back and more. The households that lose on an EV tariff are the ones with high daytime use, home all day, and low mileage; the table's 4,000-mile row is where that starts to matter.

Off-peak arithmetic also changes the case for smart home kit that can time the dishwasher, and for a home battery, which our solar article treated as a separate, tariff-dependent decision. The EV tariff is often the first domino.

5. Choosing the box: what matters and what is marketing

7 kW single-phase is the standard for a UK house; 22 kW needs a three-phase supply almost no home has. Tethered has its own cable and is more convenient; untethered is cheaper and works with whichever car comes next. Smart scheduling has been a legal requirement for new home chargers in Great Britain since 2022, so what varies is whether the app is any good and whether the box is on your tariff's compatible list.

The installer matters more than the box. Get the quote to include the cable run, any consumer unit work, the DNO notification and the electrical certificate; cheap headline quotes tend to leave one out. For the grant the installer must be on the approved list.

There is no Amazon link in this article on purpose. A wallbox is bought with its installation, the price you should compare is the installed quote, and the item that produces the saving is the tariff, which is free.

6. Worked example: Sam in Milton Keynes

Owns a three-year-old electric hatchback with a 60 kWh battery, drives about 11,000 miles a year, charges on a granny cable through the garage socket on a standard single-rate tariff, smart meter fitted in 2024

Sam's charging costs at the Energy Saving Trust's £17 per 220 miles: 50 charges a year, about £850. The granny cable takes most of a night and a day to fill the car and he has twice found the socket warm.

Step one, the tariff. He already has the smart meter. His supplier's EV tariff gives a six-hour overnight window at a rate a little under half the standard one, with a daytime rate about a penny above the cap. On the granny cable, six hours puts about 14 kWh in, a quarter of the battery, and the rest charges at the daytime rate. The tariff alone would save him perhaps £120 a year. Not enough.

Step two, the box. Two quotes for a 7 kW untethered smart charger: £940 and £1,120, both including the DNO notification and certificate. No grant: he owns the house. He takes the £940.

The sum. At £8 a charge, 50 charges: £400 a year instead of £850. Saving £450, less about £30 of dearer daytime electricity for the rest of the house: £420. £940 divided by £420 is 2.2 years. The box has a three-year warranty.

What he also does. Sets the dishwasher and washing machine delay timers to run after 11pm, which wins back the £30. And notes that from April 2028 the 3p-a-mile road charge will cost him £330 a year, which is most of the £420, so the tariff switch is what keeps his running cost where it is rather than what cuts it.

7. The honest verdict

If you charge an electric car at home and are on a standard tariff, get the smart meter, move to an EV off-peak tariff, and fit a 7 kW wallbox so the car can actually fill in the window. On the Energy Saving Trust's £17 and £8 figures that saves about £409 a year at 10,000 miles and pays back a £1,000 install in about two and a half years, or fifteen months with the renters' and flat owners' grant. It is one of the shortest paybacks on this site.

Do not fit a wallbox and stay on a single-rate tariff; the box saves nothing by itself. Do not rely on a granny cable to reach the cheap rate; it cannot deliver enough charge inside the window. And if you drive under 4,000 miles a year and are home all day using electricity, do the sum on your own bill first, because the dearer daytime rate can eat the smaller gain.

Frequently asked

How much does it cost to charge an electric car at home in the UK? The Energy Saving Trust, page updated 21 August 2026, puts a full charge giving around 220 miles at approximately £17 on a standard home rate and approximately £8 on an EV or time-of-use tariff, which typically requires a smart meter. That is roughly 7.7p and 3.6p a mile. Petrol or diesel for the same 220 miles could cost up to £45.

How long does a home EV charger take to pay for itself? At 10,000 miles a year the gap between the standard rate and the EV tariff is about £409 on the Energy Saving Trust's figures, so a wallbox installed for around £1,000 pays back in about 2.5 years, or about 15 months if the GOV.UK chargepoint grant for renters and flat owners covers 75 per cent up to £500. The payback depends entirely on moving to the off-peak tariff; the box alone saves nothing.

Can I use a three-pin granny cable instead of a wallbox? For a plug-in hybrid or occasional top-ups, yes. For a 220-mile battery, no: at about 2.3 kW it takes 25 hours or more to fill, delivers only about a fifth of the battery inside a typical five to seven hour off-peak window, and puts sustained load on a socket that was not designed for it. A 7 kW wallbox fills the same battery in nine to ten hours and can be scheduled to the cheap window.

Is there a grant for a home EV charger? For people who rent any residential property or own a flat, the GOV.UK EV chargepoint grant pays 75 per cent of the cost of buying and installing a socket up to £500, through an approved installer. Owner-occupiers of houses are not eligible. Scotland has separate grants and loans through Energy Saving Trust Scotland.
