The Average Pay Rise Was 3.4%. Inflation Took 3.3% of It

Most pay rise advice is about confidence and phrasing. The part that decides the outcome is arithmetic: what the market actually paid this year, what your employer has budgeted, and whether the number you ask for is a real increase or just a refund of inflation.

Ruhul Amin Updated 21 August 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked August 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • UK regular pay rose 3.4% in the year to January to March 2026, total pay 4.1%. The median basic pay award was 3.3%, and employers are budgeting around 3%.
  • With CPIH inflation at 3.3%, regular pay grew 0.1% in real terms. The average rise was almost exactly inflation, which means it was not a rise.
  • The sector gap is large. Public sector regular earnings grew 5.1% against 2.9% in the private sector, February to April 2026. Finance settled at 5.0%, hospitality at 2.8%.
  • Ask above the budget, not above inflation. If your employer has budgeted 3%, asking for 3.5% is a rounding argument. Asking for 8% with evidence is a different conversation.
  • The strongest case is a change in your job, not a change in prices. Nobody is persuaded by your bills. They are persuaded by what you now do that you did not do before.

Two things to take from that table before you walk into any meeting. Your employer almost certainly has a number in mind and it is close to 3%. And the spread between sectors is larger than the spread between good and poor performers within a sector, which is worth knowing when you decide whether to negotiate or to move.

2. Why the average rise was not a rise

CPIH inflation averaged 3.3% over the year to March 2026. Regular pay grew 3.4%. In real terms, that is 0.1% growth, which within measurement error is nothing at all. The later ONS figures, with vacancies down to 707,000 and private sector pay growth now below inflation, are worked through in our 2026 jobs market and pay rise guide.

This reframes the whole conversation. If you are offered 3%, you are being offered your existing salary in different numbers. Accepting it is not receiving a rise, it is declining to fall behind.

So separate the two things in your own head, and in the meeting. The cost of living adjustment is what keeps you level and it is not a reward. The rise is the part above inflation, and that is what you are actually negotiating for. Employers who conflate the two are not being dishonest, it is simply how the budget is presented, and it is your job to unbundle it.

If inflation is 3.3% and you are offered 3.3%, you have been offered the same job for the same money. That is the starting point of the negotiation, not the end of it.

3. Choosing your number

The most common mistake is asking for a figure just above the budget, because it invites a split down the middle and lands you back at the budget.

  • Find the market rate for your role, in your region, at your level. Real job adverts for your job today, not a salary survey average across the whole country. Five adverts is enough.
  • If you are below market, ask to be brought to market and say so plainly. This is the strongest possible position because it is a fact rather than an opinion, and losing you costs more than fixing it.
  • If you are already at market, you need a different argument. That argument is scope: you are doing more than the job you were hired for.
  • Ask for a specific number, not a range. A range is heard as its lowest value.
  • A realistic ask, with evidence, is 6 to 12%. Below 5% you are negotiating over the budget. Above 15% without a formal role change is usually a different job title, and it may be worth asking for that instead.

4. Building the case, in one page

Your manager may have to take this to someone else, so write it down and make it easy to forward.

  1. What you were hired to do, in one line.
  2. What you do now, and what has been added since. Scope is the whole argument.
  3. Three outcomes with numbers. Money saved, revenue brought in, time reduced, errors cut, people trained. Anything measurable beats anything adjectival.
  4. The market evidence. Three or four current adverts for equivalent roles with their advertised salaries.
  5. The ask. One number, and the date you would like it to take effect.

Leave out entirely: your rent, your energy bill, your mortgage, what a colleague earns, and how long you have been there. Length of service is the weakest argument in the room, and personal costs, however real, are not something your manager can take to a budget holder.

5. Timing, which matters more than wording

The same request succeeds or fails depending on when it arrives relative to the budget cycle.

  • Ask before the budget is set, not after. Most organisations decide pay two to four months before it takes effect. By the time you are told your increase, the money is already allocated and your manager genuinely cannot change it.
  • Find out when your organisation sets pay, and open the conversation a month before that. Your manager will usually tell you if you ask directly.
  • Ask after a visible win, while the evidence is fresh in everyone's mind.
  • Do not ask in the week of bad results, during a redundancy process, or in the same conversation as a complaint about something else.
  • Book a specific meeting for it. Raising it at the end of a one to one gives it five rushed minutes. A meeting titled "career and pay" gives your manager time to prepare, which is in your interest, because unprepared managers say no by default.

6. The conversation itself

It is shorter than people expect. Fifteen minutes is normal.

  1. Say why you are there in the first sentence. "I would like to talk about my salary" removes the awkward preamble that costs you confidence.
  2. Give the case, not the feelings. Scope, three outcomes with numbers, market evidence. Two or three minutes.
  3. State the number, then stop talking. The silence after your number is the single most important moment. People fill it by negotiating against themselves. Do not.
  4. Expect not to get an answer in the room. Almost no manager can approve on the spot. "When do you think you will be able to come back to me on this?" is the right closing question.
  5. Follow up in writing the same day. A short email with the one page attached, so it can be forwarded.

Kavita, 33, marketing executive in Birmingham on £31,000. Private sector.

Her position. The standard offer this year is 3%, which is £930 and roughly inflation. Five current adverts for equivalent roles in her city advertise £34,000 to £38,000. She is below market.

What she asks for. £36,000, a 16% increase. Large as a percentage, but it is the market midpoint rather than an aspiration, and she frames it that way: "I am asking to be brought in line with the market for this role."

Her evidence. Scope: she took on the email programme in January, which was previously an agency contract costing £900 a month. Outcomes: email revenue up 22% since she took it over, agency cost of £10,800 a year removed, and she trained two juniors on the platform.

The outcome. Her manager cannot approve £36,000. Two weeks later she is offered £34,500, an 11.3% increase, with a review in six months.

Why it worked. Not confidence, and not the phrasing. She removed £10,800 of cost, which is more than the £3,500 she was asking for, and she showed five adverts proving the company was underpaying for the role. The decision became cheaper than the alternative.

7. When the answer is no

A refusal is information, and you should collect it rather than absorb it.

Two things to price honestly if the refusal comes with a non-cash offer. Extra remote days are a lifestyle benefit, not a tax one, because the homeworking tax relief was abolished from 6 April 2026. And if what you actually want is flexibility rather than money, a statutory flexible working request is a separate process with its own eight refusal grounds, and it is a right to ask, not a right to receive.

  • Ask what would have to be true. "What would I need to be doing for this to be a yes in six months?" A manager who cannot answer that is telling you the answer is no permanently.
  • Get it written down with a date. Specific objectives and a review date. Vague encouragement without a date is a polite refusal.
  • Ask about non salary options. A four day week, additional leave, a training budget, a title change, or a bonus can be available when base salary is not, and a title change is often worth more at your next move than the money would have been.
  • Consider the sector numbers. Private sector regular earnings grew 2.9% against 5.1% in the public sector, and finance settled at 5.0% against hospitality at 2.8%. If you are in a low settling sector and already at market, the largest available increase is usually a move rather than a negotiation.
  • Do not threaten to leave unless you will. It is the last card and it can only be played once. If you would accept a counter offer, be aware that many employers treat a resignation as the start of a search for your replacement.

The honest verdict

With regular pay up 3.4% against 3.3% inflation, the default outcome this year is standing still. If you accept the standard offer, that is what you have chosen.

The version that works is unglamorous. Find out when pay is decided and ask a month before. Bring one page with scope, three numbers and five current job adverts. Ask for a specific figure, then stop talking. And if you are below market, say exactly that, because it is the only argument that is a verifiable fact rather than a matter of opinion.

If the answer is no and you are already at market in a sector settling at 2.9%, the honest conclusion is that the money is somewhere else. That is not a failure of the conversation, it is the conversation telling you something useful.

Frequently asked

How much should I ask for? Enough to be a real conversation, which usually means 6 to 12% with evidence. If you are below market, ask for the market rate regardless of what percentage that turns out to be.

Can my employer refuse to discuss pay? They can decline to increase it. Discussing your own pay is not something they can forbid, and in the UK terms preventing you from discussing pay for the purpose of establishing discrimination are unenforceable.

Should I mention another job offer? Only if it is real and you would take it. Bluffing works until it is called, and it is called more often than people expect.

What if I have only been here six months? Then the argument has to be scope rather than time. If the job has genuinely changed since you were hired, that is a legitimate case at any tenure. If it has not, wait for the review cycle.
