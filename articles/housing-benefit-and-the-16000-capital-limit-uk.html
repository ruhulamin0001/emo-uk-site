Housing Benefit: the £16,000 line, and the two groups who can still claim at all

Housing Benefit is being replaced by Universal Credit, and most people can no longer make a new claim. If you are one of the two groups who still can, your savings decide it, and the rule is a cliff rather than a slope.

Ruhul Amin Published 6 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • You can only make a new claim for Housing Benefit if you have reached State Pension age, or you are in supported, sheltered or temporary housing. Everybody else claims Universal Credit instead (GOV.UK, Housing Benefit eligibility, read 6 September 2026).
  • Savings over £16,000 and you will not usually get anything, unless you get Guarantee Credit of Pension Credit. That is a cliff, not a taper.
  • Savings over £6,000 start counting as income in the calculation, even though you have not actually earned anything from them.
  • The spare room rule takes 14% of your eligible rent for one spare bedroom and 25% for two or more, if you rent from a council or a housing association.
  • Eligible rent includes service charges like lift maintenance and a communal laundry, but not your heating or water. Renting privately, it is the lower of your actual rent or the Local Housing Allowance rate.

1. Who can still make a new claim

Start here, because for most people the rest of this article does not apply.

GOV.UK is blunt about it. Housing Benefit "is being replaced by Universal Credit", and you can only make a new claim if one of two things is true:

  • you have reached State Pension age
  • you are in supported, sheltered or temporary housing

If neither applies, you claim Universal Credit and the housing element inside it. There is no discretion here and no route round it.

If you are over State Pension age and single, you can make a new claim.

If you are over State Pension age and live with a partner, it is narrower. You can claim if you have both reached State Pension age, or if one of you reached it and started claiming Pension Credit as a couple before 15 May 2019, or if you are in supported, sheltered or temporary housing. A mixed age couple, where one has reached State Pension age and the other has not, cannot make a new claim unless they had an existing claim as a couple before 15 May 2019.

Supported, sheltered and temporary housing covers more situations than people expect. GOV.UK lists temporary accommodation such as a bed and breakfast arranged by your council, a refuge for survivors of domestic abuse, and sheltered or supported housing such as a hostel that provides "care, support or supervision". That last phrase does the work: sheltered housing without care, support or supervision does not qualify, and you would use Universal Credit for the housing costs instead.

One thing worth knowing if you already claim. An existing claim is not affected if, before 15 May 2019, you were getting Housing Benefit and had reached State Pension age. It does not matter if your partner is under State Pension age. But GOV.UK adds the sting: if your circumstances change and your Housing Benefit stops, you cannot start getting it again unless you are eligible to make a new claim. That is a reason to report changes carefully rather than letting a claim lapse.

2. The £16,000 line, and the £6,000 line beneath it

There are two savings figures and they do very different jobs.

£16,000 is the shutter. GOV.UK: you will not usually get Housing Benefit if "your savings are over £16,000 - unless you get Guarantee Credit of Pension Credit". Above that line the answer is normally no, whatever your rent is and however low your income.

The exception matters. Guarantee Credit is the part of Pension Credit that tops your weekly income up to a minimum level, and it does not have a savings limit of its own. So a pensioner with capital above £16,000 who qualifies for Guarantee Credit can still get Housing Benefit. Checking Pension Credit first is therefore not a side quest, it is the thing that decides whether the £16,000 line applies to you at all.

£6,000 is where counting starts. On the "what you'll get" page, GOV.UK lists what the amount depends on and includes "your household income - including benefits, pensions and savings (over £6,000)".

The important word is "including". Savings above £6,000 are treated as producing income for you, whether or not they actually do. Money sitting in a current account earning nothing is still assumed to be contributing. The precise conversion is done by your council when it works out your claim, so ask them to show you that line of the calculation rather than guessing at it.

Between £6,000 and £16,000, then, you are eligible but reduced. Below £6,000, savings are ignored entirely. Above £16,000, you are normally out.

Two practical consequences.

  • Do not assume a small inheritance, a pension lump sum or a compensation payment is invisible. Capital is capital, and going over £16,000 for a period can end a claim that is then hard to restart.
  • Report the change rather than waiting to be asked. Overpaid Housing Benefit is recoverable, and councils do recover it.

3. What counts as your rent, and the spare room rule

Housing Benefit is not calculated on the rent you pay. It is calculated on your "eligible rent", which is a different and usually smaller number.

Eligible rent is your actual rent plus any service charges you have to pay, such as lift maintenance or a communal laundry, but not things like heating or water costs for your own home. So a service charge line on your rent statement is not automatically included, and it is worth asking the council which parts of yours it accepted.

If you rent privately, your eligible rent is either the Local Housing Allowance rate for your area and household size, or your actual rent, whichever is lower. The LHA rate is set by where you live and how many bedrooms your household is entitled to, and you can look up both on the Valuation Office Agency's LHA site. If your rent is above the LHA rate, the gap is simply yours to find.

If you rent from a council or a housing association, the spare room rule applies instead. GOV.UK sets it out as a straight percentage cut to the eligible rent:

  • 14% for 1 spare bedroom
  • 25% for 2 or more spare bedrooms

Their own example: an eligible rent of £100 a week with one spare bedroom is reduced by 14% to £86, and the claim is calculated on £86.

What makes a bedroom "spare" is set by sharing rules, and these are where appeals are won. An adult couple are expected to share. Two children under 16 of the same sex are expected to share. Two children under 10 are expected to share regardless of sex. But a single adult of 16 or over can have their own room, and so can a couple or children who cannot share because of a disability or medical condition, and so can an overnight carer for you, your partner, your child or another adult, provided that carer does not live with you but sometimes has to stay.

Two more allowances people miss. An approved foster carer between placements keeps one spare bedroom for up to 52 weeks from the end of the last placement, and a newly approved foster carer keeps one for up to 52 weeks from approval even if no child is placed. And rooms used by students or by members of the armed or reserve forces are not counted as spare if they are away and intend to return home.

4. Pauline in Grimsby: a spare bedroom and £100 of savings

Pauline is 68, single, and rents a two bedroom flat from a housing association in Grimsby. Her rent is £110 a week and she pays a £6 weekly service charge for the communal laundry. Her spare bedroom is her spare bedroom: no carer, no foster placement, no student away at university.

Her eligible rent is £110 plus the £6 laundry charge, so £116 a week. Her heating and water are not included.

The spare room reduction is 14% of £116, which is £16.24. Her claim is calculated on £99.76 a week.

Over a year, that one spare bedroom costs her £844.48 of eligible rent before anything else is worked out.

Now the savings. Pauline has £9,400 in an ISA from selling a car and a small legacy. That is above £6,000, so the council treats part of it as producing income and her award is reduced accordingly. It is below £16,000, so she is still eligible.

Suppose instead she had £16,100. Unless she qualified for Guarantee Credit of Pension Credit, she would get nothing. The £100 that took her over the line would be standing between her and up to £5,187 a year of eligible rent being considered at all, on the arithmetic of £99.76 a week across 52 weeks.

That is the whole point of calling it a cliff. There is no version where £16,100 gets slightly less than £15,900. It gets nothing.

Which is also why the order of operations matters: check Pension Credit first. If Guarantee Credit is in play, the £16,000 line stops applying and the answer changes completely.

5. The other things that stop a claim dead

Savings get the attention, but GOV.UK's list of situations where you will not usually get Housing Benefit is longer, and several entries catch people out.

  • You are paying a mortgage on your own home. Housing Benefit is for rent. There is a separate scheme, Support for Mortgage Interest, which is a loan rather than a benefit.
  • You live in the home of a close relative. Paying rent to a parent or a sibling you live with does not create a claim.
  • You are already claiming Universal Credit, unless you are in temporary or supported housing.
  • You live with your partner and they are already claiming Housing Benefit. One claim per couple.
  • You are a full-time student. There are exceptions in the wider rules, so take advice rather than assuming.
  • Your immigration status excludes you. GOV.UK lists EEA jobseekers, asylum seekers, people sponsored to be in the UK, and anyone whose leave states they cannot claim public funds.
  • You are a Crown Tenant.

Two more things sit on top of any award.

The benefit cap limits the total amount of benefit you can get, and applies to most people aged 16 or over who have not reached State Pension age. If it bites, GOV.UK says it is your Housing Benefit that goes down to bring the total under the cap. So the cap does not reduce your other benefits, it reduces the one paying your rent.

How you get paid depends on your landlord. If you are a council tenant it goes into your rent account and you never see the money. If you are a private or housing association tenant it goes into your bank or building society account, and paying the rent from it is your job.

If you are refused or the amount looks wrong, you appeal to your council, not to DWP. Do it in writing and ask for the calculation. Citizens Advice will go through a Housing Benefit decision letter with you for free, and Shelter's helpline deals with the housing side. Both are better first calls than anyone charging a fee.

The verdict

For most working age households Housing Benefit is simply closed, and the question is really about Universal Credit. If you are at State Pension age or in supported, sheltered or temporary housing, then two numbers decide almost everything: £6,000, where your savings start being counted as income you are not actually getting, and £16,000, where the claim normally stops entirely. Before you accept a refusal at £16,000, check Guarantee Credit of Pension Credit, because that exception rewrites the answer. And if you rent from a council or housing association, check the spare room decision against the sharing rules, particularly the overnight carer allowance and the 52 week foster carer protection, because 14% of your eligible rent every week is worth an afternoon of arguing about.

Frequently asked

Does my partner's savings count towards the £16,000? Yes. Housing Benefit is assessed on the household, so a couple's capital is added together. That is one reason a couple can be refused where each of them individually would have been under the line.

I am over State Pension age and my partner is not. Can we claim? Not as a new claim, unless you are in supported, sheltered or temporary housing, or one of you reached State Pension age and started claiming Pension Credit for you as a couple before 15 May 2019. Otherwise the mixed age couple rule sends you to Universal Credit.

Is a room used by my son at university a spare bedroom? No, not if he intends to return home. GOV.UK says rooms used by students and by members of the armed or reserve forces are not counted as spare while they are away and intend to come back. The same protection does not automatically apply to an adult child who has moved out for good.

What if my rent is higher than the Local Housing Allowance rate? Your eligible rent is the lower of the two, so the difference is yours to pay. You can ask your council about a Discretionary Housing Payment, which is a separate short term fund and is not part of the Housing Benefit calculation.
