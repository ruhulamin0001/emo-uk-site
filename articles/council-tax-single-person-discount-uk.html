The 25 Per Cent Council Tax Discount Is Not Only for People Who Live Alone

Almost everyone knows the single person discount. Far fewer know that the rule is not about being alone, it is about who counts. A full bill assumes two adults, and a list of people published by GOV.UK do not count as adults at all, which puts households of two, three or four into the same 25 per cent as a single occupant.

The short version

  • A full bill assumes at least 2 adults live in the home. That is the baseline the discounts work from.
  • 25 per cent off if you live on your own, or if everyone else in your home is disregarded. Two different routes to the same discount.
  • 50 per cent off if everyone living there is disregarded, including you.
  • The disregarded list is long and includes student nurses, live-in carers, foreign language assistants registered with the British Council, and 18 or 19 year olds in full-time education.
  • Apprentices count only under a pay ceiling. Your employer must declare you will not be paid more than £195 a week and that the training leads to an accredited qualification.
  • You have to apply. GOV.UK says so even where you are clearly disregarded. Nothing happens automatically.

1. Why two adults is the baseline

GOV.UK states the structure in two sentences. "You'll usually have to pay Council Tax if you're 18 or over." And: "A full Council Tax bill is based on at least 2 adults living in a home."

That second sentence is the one everything else hangs off. Your band sets the amount, and then the bill assumes two adults are there to pay it. If fewer than two adults count, the bill comes down. If more than two adults live there, it does not go up, which is why a house of five working adults pays exactly the same as a house of two in the same band.

The word doing the work is count. GOV.UK calls it being disregarded. A person can be living in the property full time, sleeping there every night, and still not be counted for the purposes of working out the bill. Disregarded is not the same as absent, and it is not the same as exempt.

From that, the two discounts follow mechanically:

  • 25 per cent off if you pay Council Tax and either you live on your own, or everyone else in your home is disregarded.
  • 50 per cent off if everyone living in the household is disregarded.

2. The disregarded list, in full

GOV.UK's list of people who are disregarded is this. You are disregarded if you are:

  • under 18 years old
  • on certain apprentice schemes
  • 18 or 19 years old and in full-time education
  • a full-time student at college or university
  • under 25 years old and get funding from the Education and Skills Funding Agency
  • a student nurse
  • a foreign language assistant registered with the British Council
  • severely mentally impaired
  • a live-in carer for someone who is not your partner, spouse, or child under 18
  • a diplomat

Three entries on that list are worth pausing on, because they are the ones households miss.

Student nurse is listed separately from full-time student. People training on a nursing course often assume the general student rules either do or do not cover them and stop there.

Live-in carer, with a specific exclusion: the person being cared for must not be your partner, your spouse, or your child under 18. So a parent caring for an adult son or daughter, or an adult caring for a parent, can be disregarded where a spouse caring for a spouse cannot.

18 or 19 and in full-time education catches the year when a child stops being disregarded for being under 18 and before they start being disregarded as a university student. Households often assume the discount stops on the eighteenth birthday. It does not, if the young person is still in full-time education.

Severely mentally impaired is a defined status requiring a doctor's certificate and, usually, receipt of a qualifying benefit. It is the single most under-claimed entry on this list, and it is worth checking properly rather than dismissing on the wording.

3. The apprentice rule and the 195 pound line

The apprentice entry is the only one with a number attached, and the number is not widely known.

GOV.UK: to show you do not qualify as an adult for Council Tax, you need a declaration from your employer stating that you will not be paid more than £195 a week, and that the training leads to a qualification accredited by a body recognised by Ofqual, or by the Scottish Vocational Education Council.

Both limbs have to be satisfied, and both have to come from the employer rather than from you. An apprentice paid above £195 a week is not disregarded, however genuine the apprenticeship. An apprentice on a training scheme that does not lead to an accredited qualification is not disregarded either, however low the pay.

The action, if there is an apprentice in the house, is a single email to their employer asking for that declaration in writing. It costs nothing and it is the only evidence the council will accept.

4. Worked example: Yvonne in Ipswich

Yvonne works full time and lives with her 19 year old son, who is in the second year of a college course, and her mother, who moved in last year and whom Yvonne cares for.

The instinctive answer. Three adults in the house, so no discount. That is what Yvonne assumed for a year.

The actual position. Her son is 18 or 19 and in full-time education, so he is disregarded. Her mother's position depends on the facts, but if her mother is severely mentally impaired within the council tax meaning and holds the qualifying benefit, she is disregarded too. Yvonne herself is a live-in carer, but the carer entry disregards a carer of someone who is not their partner, spouse or child under 18, and her mother is none of those, so on the face of the list Yvonne may be disregarded as well.

The two possible outcomes. If her son and mother are disregarded and Yvonne is not, everyone else in the home is disregarded, so the bill falls by 25 per cent. If Yvonne also qualifies as a live-in carer, then everyone in the household is disregarded and the reduction is 50 per cent.

What that is worth. On a band C bill of £1,960 a year, 25 per cent is £490 a year and 50 per cent is £980 a year. Those are large numbers for a form, and the figure used here is an illustration, because your actual bill depends on your band and your council rather than on anything in this article.

What she has to do. Apply. None of the above happens by itself, and the council does not know her son's college enrolment status or her mother's medical position unless she tells them.

5. Claiming it, and the mistake that gets fined

GOV.UK is explicit: "You need to apply for a Council Tax discount or an exemption, even if you're disregarded." The application is through your own council, and the evidence is whatever fits the ground: a college letter, an employer declaration, a doctor's certificate, a benefit award letter.

Then the warning most people never read, and it matters as much as the discount. If you get a Council Tax discount by mistake, you must contact your local council. If you do not, you could get a fine, and the council may ask you to pay back the discount.

That is not an abstract risk. The common version is a single person discount that was correct in March and wrong in September because someone moved in, and it goes unreported because nobody thinks of council tax as a thing you update. Treat the discount as conditional on a set of facts, and tell the council on the day those facts change. Reporting a change yourself is an administrative correction. Being found out is a penalty plus repayment.

And if the household position is genuinely unclear, GOV.UK's own advice is to contact the council and ask, which costs nothing and creates a record of your having asked.

The honest verdict

The single person discount is misnamed and the misnaming costs households real money. The rule is about who counts, not about who is alone. If everyone else in your home is disregarded, you get the same 25 per cent as someone living by themselves, and if everyone including you is disregarded, it is 50 per cent.

The list is worth reading line by line rather than skimming, because the entries people miss are the specific ones: student nurse, live-in carer for a non-spouse, 18 or 19 and still in full-time education, and severely mentally impaired. Any one of those in your household is worth an application.

Where this does not help you: the discount is not means tested and it is not the same as Council Tax Reduction, which is a separate scheme for people on low incomes and is worth checking as well. And if your bill feels wrong for the property rather than the people, that is a band question, not a discount question.

Frequently asked

Does a lodger stop my single person discount? Usually yes, if the lodger is an adult who is not disregarded, because the home then has two counting adults. If the lodger falls into one of the disregarded categories, for example a full-time student, the discount can continue. Tell the council either way.

My adult child is unemployed and lives with me. Do I still get 25 per cent? Being unemployed is not on the disregarded list. An adult child who is over 18, not in full-time education and not otherwise disregarded counts as a second adult, so the discount would not apply on that ground. Council Tax Reduction is the scheme to look at instead if household income is low.

Is the discount backdated if I apply late? Councils often can backdate where the qualifying facts were true for the earlier period and you can evidence them, but it is a council decision rather than an entitlement set out on the GOV.UK page. Ask, and provide dated evidence such as a college enrolment letter.

Does this apply in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland? The 25 and 50 per cent structure applies in England, Wales and Scotland with their own administrative differences, and the GOV.UK page is written for England and Wales. Northern Ireland uses domestic rates rather than council tax, so this does not apply there at all.
