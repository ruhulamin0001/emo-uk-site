One Route Makes Your Donation Cheaper. The Other Makes It Bigger. They Are Not The Same Choice

Payroll Giving takes the money before Income Tax, so a £1 donation costs a basic rate taxpayer 80p and a higher rate taxpayer 60p. Gift Aid costs you the full pound and hands the charity an extra 25p from HMRC. Both are worth using, they suit different people, and a charity cannot claim Gift Aid on a Payroll Giving donation.

The short version

  • Payroll Giving: to donate £1 you pay 80p at basic rate, 60p at higher rate and 55p at additional rate. Relief happens at source, on your payslip.
  • Gift Aid: you pay the full £1 and the charity claims an extra 25p from HMRC. A higher rate taxpayer then claims the difference back themselves.
  • A charity cannot claim Gift Aid on a Payroll Giving donation. GOV.UK states this plainly. You get one or the other, never both.
  • Payroll Giving comes out after National Insurance, not before it, so you still pay NI on the amount you donate.
  • Gift Aid only works if you pay enough tax. Your donations must not be more than four times the tax you paid that year, and HMRC can ask you for the difference if they are.
  • You cannot use Payroll Giving for a community amateur sports club. Gift Aid does cover CASCs.

1. How Payroll Giving actually works

Payroll Giving is a way of giving to charity without paying tax on the money, and it has to run through PAYE from your wages or pension. Your employer sets the scheme up; you cannot do it on your own.

The mechanics are the whole point. The donation is taken from your pay before tax but after National Insurance. Because it comes out before Income Tax, you never pay Income Tax on it, which is why the cost to you depends on your tax rate.

GOV.UK states the cost of donating £1 directly. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland:

  • 80p if you are a basic rate taxpayer
  • 60p if you are a higher rate taxpayer
  • 55p if you are an additional rate taxpayer

Scotland has its own bands, so the figures differ. To donate £1 you pay 81p at starter rate, 80p at basic, 79p at intermediate, 58p at higher and 52p at top rate.

Three details that are easy to miss and all affect the answer.

You still pay National Insurance on the donation. The deduction happens after NI, so the relief is on Income Tax only. That is a real limit on how good Payroll Giving is, and it is stated on GOV.UK rather than hidden.

There is usually an admin fee. Payroll Giving agencies may charge one, and GOV.UK says they usually deduct it from employees' donations before passing them to the charity. So the charity may receive slightly less than you gave. Employers can choose to pay the fee themselves, in which case the charity gets the full amount, and the employer can deduct the cost from business profits before tax. It is worth asking your employer which of those two applies to you, because nobody will tell you unprompted.

You cannot give to a community amateur sports club this way. GOV.UK excludes CASCs from Payroll Giving. If your local cricket club is a CASC, Gift Aid is your only route.

2. How Gift Aid actually works

Gift Aid works from the other direction. You donate out of taxed income, and the charity reclaims the basic rate tax you already paid on that money.

GOV.UK puts it simply: donating through Gift Aid means charities and CASCs can claim an extra 25p for every £1 you give, and it will not cost you any extra. You make a declaration to each charity, which can cover current donations, future donations and all donations made in the last 4 years.

If you are a higher rate taxpayer there is a second step, and it is yours to take. GOV.UK's own example: you donate £100, the charity claims Gift Aid to make it £125, and because you pay 40% you can personally claim back £25.00, being £125 times 20%. You do that through Self Assessment, or by asking HMRC to amend your tax code.

If you do not file a return, you can still claim: £5,000 or less by phone, more than £5,000 in writing. For claims of £10,000 or more you also have to tell HMRC the date of the donation and who you gave it to.

There is one genuine trap, and it is the reason Gift Aid is not simply free money. Your donations must not be more than four times what you paid in tax that year, on income or capital gains. If the charity reclaims more tax than you actually paid, HMRC may ask you to pay the difference. You should only sign a declaration if you have paid enough tax to cover what will be reclaimed, and you must tell the charities you support if you stop paying enough tax. That catches people who retire, take a career break, or drop below the Personal Allowance.

One useful piece of flexibility: for Gift Aid you can claim relief on donations made in the current tax year up to the date you send your return, which helps if you want the relief sooner, or if you paid higher rate tax last year but will not this year. You lose that option if you miss the return deadline, 31 January online or 31 October on paper.

3. Which one leaves who better off

The two schemes optimise for different things, and the honest answer depends on which you care about.

Payroll Giving makes the donation cheaper for you. The charity receives what you gave, less any agency fee. Nothing is added.

Gift Aid makes the donation bigger for the charity. It costs you the headline amount, and the charity gets 25% more than you handed over.

Put concretely, with £100 leaving your pocket:

  • Gift Aid, any taxpayer: you are £100 down, the charity receives £125.
  • Gift Aid, higher rate taxpayer who claims: the charity receives £125 and you claim £25 back, so you are £75 down.
  • Payroll Giving, basic rate: £100 of donation costs you £80 of take home pay, and the charity receives £100 less any agency fee.
  • Payroll Giving, higher rate: £100 of donation costs you £60, and the charity receives £100 less any fee.

Read those four lines carefully, because the comparison people usually make is the wrong one. At higher rate, Gift Aid plus the claim delivers the charity £125 for a net £75, while Payroll Giving delivers £100 for a net £60. Gift Aid gives the charity £25 more; Payroll Giving leaves you £15 better off. Per pound you personally give up, Gift Aid with the claim is the more efficient route for the charity, at £1.67 delivered per net pound against £1.67 for Payroll Giving. They are remarkably close, which is why the tie breakers below matter more than the arithmetic.

Payroll Giving wins when: you would not claim higher rate relief anyway, and most higher rate taxpayers do not; you want it automatic and off your mind; you want to give without owning any paperwork; or your income might drop below the level where Gift Aid declarations are safe.

Gift Aid wins when: your employer has no scheme, which is most employers; you are self employed and so have no payroll to give from; you give to a CASC; you give in one off amounts rather than monthly; or you actually will do the Self Assessment claim.

The single most valuable thing in this article for most readers is not the choice at all. It is that higher rate relief on Gift Aid is not automatic and enormous amounts of it go unclaimed every year. If you pay 40% and have been Gift Aiding donations without claiming, you can go back through your returns. Our guide to Gift Aid higher rate relief sets out how.

4. Worked example: Simone in Gloucester, £40 a month

Simone earns £58,000 as a project manager in Gloucester, which makes her a higher rate taxpayer. She gives £40 a month, £480 a year, to a homelessness charity. Her employer runs a Payroll Giving scheme and the agency fee is deducted from donations.

Route one, Gift Aid by direct debit. She pays £480 from her bank. The charity claims 25% and receives £600. As a higher rate taxpayer she can claim back £600 times 20%, which is £120, through Self Assessment or a tax code change. Her net cost is £360 and the charity has £600.

Route two, Payroll Giving. To give £480 she gives up £288 of take home pay, because at higher rate £1 costs 60p. The charity receives £480 less the agency fee. Her net cost is £288 and the charity has just under £480.

The comparison. Gift Aid costs her £72 more and delivers the charity roughly £120 more. Per pound she gives up, Gift Aid delivers £1.67 to the charity and Payroll Giving delivers about £1.66. Almost identical.

So the deciding factor is not the maths, it is her behaviour. If she will reliably claim the £120 every year, Gift Aid is marginally better for the charity at a slightly higher cost to her. If she will not claim, which is the common outcome, Gift Aid costs her £480 net for the charity's £600, while Payroll Giving would have cost her £288 for £480. In that case Payroll Giving is clearly the better arrangement for her, and only £120 worse for the charity.

What she actually decides. She moves to Payroll Giving, because she has not filed a Self Assessment return in three years and knows she will not start. She also increases the monthly amount to £55, because at 60p in the pound her net cost of £33 a month is close to what £40 of Gift Aid donation was costing her. The charity ends up with £660 a year against the £600 it was getting, and she is paying less.

That last move is the one most people miss. If you switch to Payroll Giving and keep the donation the same, you have given yourself a pay rise rather than the charity a bigger donation. Deciding which of those you meant is the whole exercise.

The honest verdict

These two schemes answer different questions. Payroll Giving makes your donation cheaper to you. Gift Aid makes it larger for the charity. Per pound you actually give up they land within a penny of each other, so the arithmetic will not decide it for you.

What decides it is whether you will do the paperwork. Gift Aid's higher rate relief only exists if you claim it, and most people who are entitled to it never do. If you know yourself well enough to admit you will not file, Payroll Giving quietly gives you the same relief without asking you to do anything.

The trap on the other side is the four times rule. Gift Aid declarations only work if you pay enough tax to cover what the charity reclaims, and HMRC can come back to you if they do not. If you retire, take a break, or drop below the Personal Allowance, tell the charities you support.

And if you do switch to Payroll Giving, decide deliberately what to do with the difference. Keeping the donation the same size gives you the saving. Increasing it by about a quarter at basic rate, or two thirds at higher rate, gives it to the charity instead. Both are legitimate; drifting into one without noticing is not.

Frequently asked

Can a charity claim Gift Aid on a Payroll Giving donation? No. GOV.UK states that charities cannot claim Gift Aid on Payroll Giving donations. The relief has already been given to you at source, so there is nothing left for the charity to reclaim.

Do I save National Insurance with Payroll Giving? No. The donation is deducted after National Insurance, so you still pay NI on the amount you give. The relief is on Income Tax only, which is why £1 costs 80p at basic rate rather than less.

My employer does not offer Payroll Giving. Can I set it up myself? Not on your own. The employer has to contact an approved Payroll Giving agency and run the scheme through payroll. You can ask them to; GOV.UK publishes a list of approved agencies and the employer can deduct the running costs from business profits.

I am self employed. Which one applies to me? Gift Aid. Payroll Giving needs a PAYE payroll to deduct from. If you pay higher or additional rate tax you claim the difference on your Self Assessment return, which you are already filing.
