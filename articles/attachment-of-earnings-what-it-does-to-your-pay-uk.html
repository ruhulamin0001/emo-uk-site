There Is a Floor Under Your Wages, and Most People Facing an Attachment of Earnings Order Do Not Know It Exists

When a county court judgment is enforced by taking money from your wages, it is easy to assume the court has simply reached into your pay packet. It has not. It has set two numbers, and the second of them is a floor that your employer is not allowed to go below.

The short version

  • A CCJ enforced against wages is a non-priority order. The court sends the order to your employer, not to you to pay.
  • The court sets two figures: a normal deduction rate and a protected earnings rate. The first is what comes out, the second is what has to be left.
  • If the full deduction would take you below the protected earnings rate, they deduct less. The shortfall is not carried over to the next payday.
  • If your earnings are below the protected rate, nothing is deducted at all, and your employer must tell the CAPS office why.
  • Your employer may keep an extra £1 each time they make a deduction towards their own admin costs. They can take that even if it pushes you below your protected earnings rate, but never below the National Minimum Wage.
  • A creditor can only apply if you owe them more than £50.

1. What actually happens

If someone has a county court judgment against you and you have not paid it, one of the things they can ask the court to do is take the money directly from your wages. The form is N337, a request for an attachment of earnings order, and the creditor can only use it if you owe them more than £50.

If the court makes the order, it goes to your employer, not to you. Your employer is then legally obliged to make the deductions. GOV.UK is blunt about it: both you and your employer can be fined if the deductions are not made, or if either of you deliberately gives false information about your earnings.

Debts from a county court judgment are treated as a non-priority order. That matters, because the rules for non-priority orders are the ones set out below. Unpaid maintenance is handled under a different, stricter set of rules, and benefit debt and child maintenance run through separate processes entirely.

2. The two rates that decide everything

The order your employer receives contains two figures set by the court.

The normal deduction rate. This is the amount your employer takes out of each pay packet and sends to the court.

The protected earnings rate. This is the amount that must be left in your hands. Your employer must pay you at least this much.

The interaction of the two is the whole of the protection, and it works in three steps.

  • If the full normal deduction still leaves you at or above the protected rate, the full amount comes out. Nothing unusual happens.
  • If the full deduction would take you below the protected rate, your employer deducts only as much as they can while leaving you at the protected rate. And here is the important part: the unpaid difference is not carried over to the next payday. It is not a debt that accumulates in the background.
  • If your earnings that payday are already below the protected rate, nothing at all is deducted, again with no carry-over. Your employer has to email the Centralised Attachment of Earning Payments office to explain why no deduction was made.

That no-carry-over rule is the single most reassuring fact in this whole area and it is almost never mentioned. A bad month does not create a worse next month.

3. The extra pound

Your employer is allowed to keep an extra £1 each time they make a deduction, towards their own administrative costs. They do not have to, but most do.

The £1 has its own rules, and they are oddly specific. Your employer can still take the £1 even if doing so takes your income below your protected earnings rate. They cannot take it if doing so takes your income below the National Minimum Wage. And if no deduction can be made at all because your earnings are under the protected rate, they are not allowed to take the £1 either.

Over a year of monthly pay that is £12. It is small, and it is worth knowing about only because seeing an unexplained extra pound gone from a payslip is exactly the sort of thing that makes people assume the whole calculation is wrong.

4. Worked example: Tomasz in Peterborough

Tomasz has a CCJ enforced against his wages. The court has set a normal deduction rate of £40 and a protected earnings rate of £320 per weekly pay period. He is paid weekly and his hours vary.

A good week. Earnings £420. Deduct the normal £40 and he is at £380, above the protected rate. So the full £40 goes to the court, the employer keeps £1, and Tomasz is paid £379.

A short week. Earnings £345. The full £40 would leave him at £305, below the £320 protected rate. So the employer deducts only £25, leaving him at the protected £320, keeps £1, and pays him £319. The missing £15 is not added to next week.

A bad week. Earnings £300. He is already below the £320 protected rate, so nothing is deducted. The employer cannot take the £1 either, and must email the CAPS office to say why no deduction was made. Tomasz is paid the full £300.

Back to normal. Earnings £420. Full £40 to the court, £1 to the employer, £379 to Tomasz. The previous two weeks do not increase this week's deduction.

Across those four weeks the court receives £65 rather than the £160 it would have received if the deduction were fixed. That gap is the protection working exactly as designed, not a mistake.

5. What it does not do

It does not send bailiffs. An attachment of earnings order and a warrant of control are two different enforcement routes. A creditor chooses one, and the order to your employer does not put anyone at your door.

It does not touch your home. Securing a debt against property is a charging order under a separate application, and it is a different form and a different decision.

It does not run forever. Deductions stop when the debt is paid off, and your employer is required to stop them at that point.

It does not override Breathing Space. If you enter the Debt Respite Scheme, a creditor cannot enforce a judgment against you while that protection is running, and they are required to tell the court.

It does not follow you automatically to a new job. The order is served on a named employer. Changes of circumstance, including changing employer, have their own reporting rules, and you should not assume nobody will notice, but it is not a charge that travels with you invisibly.

The honest verdict

An attachment of earnings order is not the court taking whatever it likes. It is two numbers, and the second one is a floor. Below the protected earnings rate nothing comes out, and nothing is stored up to come out later.

The one thing genuinely worth doing is checking the two figures on the order against your actual pay pattern, particularly if your hours vary. If the protected earnings rate was set on an assumption about your income that is no longer true, that is a matter to raise with the court rather than something to absorb quietly. Free debt advice is available from National Debtline and Citizens Advice, and neither charges you anything to look at the numbers with you.

Frequently asked

How much can be taken from my wages? Whatever the court set as the normal deduction rate, but never so much that you are left below the protected earnings rate. Both figures are on the order sent to your employer.

If they take less one month, do they take more the next? No. GOV.UK is explicit that where the full deduction cannot be made because it would take you below your protected earnings rate, the unpaid difference is not carried over to the next payday.

Can my employer charge me for this? They can keep an extra 1 pound each time they make a deduction, towards their own costs. They cannot take it if doing so would put you below the National Minimum Wage, or if no deduction was possible at all that payday.

Will my employer find out about my debt? Yes, unavoidably, because the order is served on them and they have to operate it. What they receive is the order and the two rates, not a general account of your finances.
