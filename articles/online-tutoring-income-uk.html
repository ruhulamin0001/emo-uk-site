MyTutor Keeps 40%. On a £40 Lesson You See About £21

Tutoring is one of the few side hustles that clears minimum wage from your very first hour. The variable that decides whether it clears it by a little or by a lot is not your subject, your degree or your experience. It is the commission.

The short version

  • UK tutors charge roughly £28 to £55 for GCSE and £38 to £75 for A Level, averaging about £38 and £52 an hour.
  • Online typically pays £5 to £10 an hour less than in person at the same level, and that gap holds from primary to A Level.
  • MyTutor takes 40% plus VAT, leaving tutors roughly 52% of the lesson fee, with published terms allowing a maximum platform fee of 49%.
  • Tutorful charges the student a 35% service fee instead and pays tutors their full rate, though it is not accepting new tutor applications in 2026. PMT Education is among the lowest at 18%.
  • The same lesson can pay £21 or £40. The difference is entirely how the student found you.

1. What tutors actually charge

Online generally pays £5 to £10 an hour less than in person at the same level. In exchange you remove travel entirely, which for most people is worth more than the difference, because an hour of unpaid driving costs you more than £8.

What raises your rate, in rough order of effect: the subject, since maths and the sciences pay more than most; the level, since A Level pays more than GCSE and university level more again; examiner experience, which is the single most quotable credential; and results you can point to. Not your degree class, which almost no parent asks about.

2. What each platform takes

This is where most of your money goes or does not go, and the models differ in ways that are easy to miss.

PlatformModelOn a £40 lesson
MyTutorTakes 40% plus VAT from the tutor. Terms allow up to 49%You keep roughly £21
TutorfulAdds a 35% service fee on top, paid by the student. Tutor keeps their full rateYou keep £40, the student pays £54. Not accepting new tutors in 2026
PMT Education18% platform fee per lessonYou keep about £33
Direct studentNo platformYou keep £40, minus the time you spent finding them

The two models are genuinely different, not just different numbers. A commission taken from the tutor lowers your pay for the same price. A service fee added to the student raises the price without lowering your pay, which makes you look more expensive but leaves your rate intact.

Neither is dishonest, but you need to know which you are on before you set your rate, because on a tutor commission model you must price with the deduction in mind. A £40 rate on MyTutor is a £21 job.

Forty per cent is not a fee, it is a business partner. Whether that partner earns their share depends entirely on whether you could have found the student yourself.

3. The unpaid hours nobody counts

Tutoring looks like a pure hourly job, and it is not. A one hour lesson is rarely one hour of your time.

  • Preparation. 10 to 30 minutes for a new topic, less once you have taught it several times. Building a reusable bank of materials is the single biggest efficiency gain available.
  • Marking and feedback. If you set work between sessions, and you should, someone has to look at it.
  • Parent communication. Messages, progress updates, rescheduling. Small individually and significant in total.
  • Admin. Invoicing, chasing payment, keeping records for tax.

A realistic figure is 20 to 30% on top of billed time in your first year, falling towards 10% once your materials are built. So a nominal £38 an hour is closer to £29 in year one, which is still good, but it is not £38.

Two policies that protect you: a 24 hour cancellation policy, stated before the first lesson, and payment in advance for blocks of lessons. Late cancellations are the largest hidden cost in tutoring and almost every experienced tutor has learned this the expensive way.

4. Moving to direct students, legally

The obvious move is to take platform students off platform. Read your contract first, because most platforms prohibit this and can remove you, and in some cases pursue the fee.

The legitimate routes to direct students:

  • Referrals from families you already teach directly. The strongest source by a distance. Parents talk to other parents, and a recommendation converts far better than any advert.
  • Local school and community groups. Village and school Facebook groups, parish newsletters, notice boards. Unglamorous and effective.
  • Your own simple page. Subjects, levels, rate, DBS status, how to contact you. It does not need to be sophisticated, it needs to exist so people can check you are real.
  • Schools directly. Some will pass your details to parents asking for tutors, particularly for shortage subjects.

The sensible strategy is to use a platform for the first term and build direct students alongside it, not instead of it. The platform solves the hardest problem, which is your first students and your first testimonials. Once you have those, the 40% stops being good value.

5. A worked academic year

Tom, 29, maths graduate in Bristol. Tutoring evenings, term time only.

Autumn term. Six hours a week on a platform taking 40%. Nominal rate £40, so he receives about £21 an hour. Twelve teaching weeks. £1,512. Preparation adds roughly 30%, so his real rate is about £16 an hour.

Spring term. Two families found him locally through a school group at his full £40. Four platform hours plus three direct hours a week. Platform £1,008, direct £1,440, total £2,448 for one hour more per week than the autumn.

Summer term. Exam season, demand peaks. Two platform hours plus seven direct, and he raises his direct rate to £45 for A Level. £504 platform, £2,835 direct, total £3,339.

Academic year: about £7,299 for roughly eight teaching hours a week during term time.

The comparison that matters. Had he stayed entirely on the platform at £21 an hour, the same teaching hours would have produced about £4,000. Moving most of his hours direct earned him £3,300 more for the same work, and the only thing that changed was who introduced him to the family.

Seasonality worth planning for: demand collapses in July and August and peaks from January to May. Budget for a quiet summer rather than being surprised by it.

6. DBS, safeguarding and the boring essentials

  • A DBS check is not legally required for private tutoring, but almost every parent will ask and most platforms require one. An enhanced check through a platform or an umbrella body is the usual route, and it makes you far easier to say yes to.
  • Record your online lessons or keep the chat log, with the parent informed. This protects the student and it protects you.
  • Keep a parent in the loop. Communicate through the parent for under 18s rather than privately with the child.
  • Professional indemnity insurance is inexpensive and some platforms require it.
  • The £1,000 trading allowance is on gross income. At £30 an hour you pass it in 34 lessons, so most tutors will need to register for Self Assessment in their first term.
  • If you are a teacher, check your employment contract and your school's policy on private tuition, particularly regarding tutoring pupils from your own school.

The honest verdict

Tutoring is one of the best side hustles available in the UK, and it is one of the few that clears the £12.71 benchmark from the first hour with no equipment and no startup cost. If you can teach GCSE maths or A Level sciences, you can earn more per hour than in most part time employment.

The mistake is staying on a 40% platform for years. It is the right way to start, because it solves your first students and your first reviews, and it is a poor way to continue once you have both.

Plan for one academic year: platform in the autumn, build direct students through referrals in the spring, and by the summer exam season most of your hours should be direct. That single shift is worth more than any rate rise you will ever negotiate.

Frequently asked

Do I need to be a qualified teacher? No. Subject knowledge and the ability to explain it are what parents buy. Qualified teacher status helps you charge more, particularly examiner experience, but it is not required.

What should I charge as a beginner? Slightly below the average for your level, so around £30 for GCSE and £40 for A Level, then raise it after your first three students and a couple of testimonials. Do not start at £15, because it is very hard to climb from there and it signals inexperience.

Which platform is best for a new tutor? Compare the fee model rather than the brand. A tutor side commission lowers your pay for the same price. A student side service fee does not. And check whether they are accepting applications at all, since availability changes.

How many students can I realistically teach? Six to ten hours a week alongside a full time job is sustainable. Beyond that, evening teaching after a working day gets difficult to do well, and tired tutoring loses students.
