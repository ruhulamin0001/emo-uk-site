A Smart Meter Does Not Make Energy Cheaper. It Makes It Visible

Smart meters are sold on the promise of saving money and refused on the suspicion of costing money, and both positions miss what the device does. It does not touch your unit rate or your standing charge. It changes what you can see, how accurately you are billed, and which tariffs you can be offered.

The short version

  • A smart meter does not change your unit rate or your standing charge. The price cap applies either way.
  • Ofgem lists three benefits: near real-time usage information, an end to estimated bills, and access to a broader range of tariffs.
  • You are not charged separately for it. Meters and their maintenance are paid for through energy bills, as they always were.
  • You can refuse one, and request one later without being charged. Refusing may limit which tariffs you can get.
  • It stores your usage every 30 minutes. Whether that is shared half-hourly or daily depends on when it was installed.
  • The saving, if any, comes from what you do with the information, or from a tariff the meter unlocks. Not from the meter.

1. What it does not change

Start here, because both the marketing and the suspicion get it wrong. Installing a smart meter does not alter the price you pay for energy.

Under the cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, a direct debit household on a standard variable tariff pays an average 26.32p per kWh of electricity with a 54.83p daily standing charge, and 7.97p per kWh of gas with a 29.68p standing charge. The same whether your meter is thirty years old or was fitted this morning.

The standing charges alone. 54.83p plus 29.68p is 84.51p a day, or £308.46 a year, before you use a single unit. A smart meter does not reduce that by a penny.

Nor are you billed separately for the device. Ofgem is explicit: you pay for your meter and its maintenance through your energy bills, and this is the same for smart meters. The objection that suppliers are recovering the cost from you is half right in a way that does not distinguish smart meters at all, because metering has always been inside the bill and is one of the components Ofgem includes in the cap.

2. What Ofgem says it does change

Ofgem lists three benefits, and they are not equally valuable.

  • Near real-time information on your use. The in-home display shows consumption in pounds and pence. Whether this changes behaviour is down to you.
  • An end to estimated bills. The most reliably useful of the three and the least talked about. A meter that reports itself removes the estimate, and with it the catch-up bill.
  • Access to a broader range of tariffs. Some smart tariffs could be cheaper. Time-of-use tariffs need a meter that records when you used energy, not just how much.

Prepayment customers get a further set of practical benefits: flexible top up without visiting a shop, a credit balance on the display, automatic top up so you are not cut off overnight, and no meter change when moving between prepayment and credit tariffs.

So two of the three are administrative and certain. The headline one, saving money by watching a display, is conditional on behaviour.

3. Worked example: Denise in Rotherham

Denise, Rotherham. Three bedroom semi, gas and electricity, standard variable tariff, direct debit. She has been told a smart meter will cut her bills and wants to know by how much.

Before. Capped unit rates, standing charges of £308.46 a year.

After the meter is fitted, changing nothing else. Capped unit rates, standing charges of £308.46 a year. The bill is identical.

What could move it. Two things, neither the meter. If the display shows her something is costing real money and she changes it, the saving is the behaviour. If the meter lets her onto a cheaper off-peak tariff and she shifts her washing, the saving is the tariff plus the shift.

What it definitely gets her. Accurate bills. If she has been estimated too low for two years, that is not a saving, it is a bill arriving late. Estimated too high, and the correction is real money back.

4. The data question, and the date that decides it

This is the most common genuine objection and it usually gets reassurance rather than detail. Your smart meter stores data about your energy use every 30 minutes. What differs is how often it is shared, and the default depends on a date.

Installed before 3 November 2022, with no supplier switch or new contract since, you automatically share data at daily resolution. You can opt in to half-hourly, or opt out to monthly.

Installed on or after 3 November 2022, or you have switched supplier or agreed a new contract since, you automatically share at half-hourly resolution. You can opt out to daily.

So most people who have changed tariff recently are on half-hourly sharing and were never asked, because it is the default. You can change it in either direction by telling your supplier. Using your data for marketing requires your permission; data needed to bill you and for other regulated purposes does not.

5. Refusing one, renting, and when it stops being smart

  • You can say no. Decline an offer and request one later without charge. The trade off is a limited choice of tariffs. Suppliers must still install a smart meter when replacing a meter or fitting one for the first time, unless there is good reason not to.
  • If you rent and pay the bills, the choice is yours. Tell your landlord, because your tenancy may have rules about metering. If it requires their permission, they should not unreasonably refuse. If the landlord pays, it is their decision.
  • If it stops working in smart mode, nothing breaks. It still measures accurately and you take manual readings. First generation meters are being remotely upgraded to restore smart functionality.

The Citizens Advice Smart Meter Checker tells you what meter you have and whether it is in smart mode. If an installation goes wrong, Ofgem cannot investigate individual complaints. Citizens Advice and the Energy Ombudsman can.

The honest verdict

A smart meter is a measurement device, not a saving. It does not reduce your unit rate, it does not touch the £308.46 a year of standing charges at the current cap, and it is not billed separately. Anyone selling it as a way to cut your bill is describing the behaviour change they hope follows, not the hardware.

On what it actually does, it is worth having for most households, and the strongest reason is the dullest: accurate bills instead of estimates. The second, access to time-of-use tariffs, is worth real money but only to a household that can move usage into cheaper hours, which means washing and charging rather than heating or cooking. If neither appeals, declining is reasonable and you can change your mind later at no cost. What is not reasonable is expecting the meter to make anything cheaper.

Frequently asked

Will a smart meter make my bills cheaper? Not by itself. Your unit rates and standing charges are unchanged by the type of meter you have. Savings come from changing what you use after seeing it, or from a tariff the meter makes available, such as one with cheaper off-peak electricity.

Am I charged for the smart meter? Not separately. Ofgem states that you pay for your meter and its maintenance through your energy bills, as you always have, and that this is the same for smart meters. There is no separate charge for the meter or the in-home display.

Can I refuse a smart meter? Yes. You can choose not to accept an offer of one, and you can request one later without being charged. The trade off is a limited choice of tariffs, because some smart tariffs require a smart meter.
