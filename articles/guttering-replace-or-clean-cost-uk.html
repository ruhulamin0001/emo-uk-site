A Gutter Clean Is the Cheapest Thing You Can Buy for a House

Per year of service, a gutter clean beats almost every other line of home maintenance there is. It is also the one most people skip, and the consequence of skipping it does not land on the gutter at all. It lands on the wall behind it, where it costs many times more to put right.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • A clean is £80 to £160, a replacement is £700 to £2,000. Those are not comparable until you divide each by the years it buys.
  • Cleaning once a year is about £80 to £160 a year. A £1,200 replacement over 25 years is about £48 a year, plus you still have to clean it.
  • So replacement does not save you the cleaning. New gutters still fill with leaves. Anyone selling replacement as an alternative to maintenance is selling the wrong thing.
  • Replace when the gutter has failed, not when it is dirty. Sagging, split joints, plastic gone brittle and grey, or brackets pulling out of soffit that has rotted.
  • The real cost of a blocked gutter is the wall. Water running down a wall for a winter is a penetrating damp problem, and that is a four figure job, not an £80 one.
  • Do the gutters while the scaffolding is up. Access is most of the price of working at height, and paying for it twice is money burnt.

1. Cost per year, both ways

The arithmetic here is the same as for a roof, and the conclusion is the same shape: a small recurring cost usually beats a large one-off cost, right up until the moment the thing you are maintaining has nothing left to maintain.

Cleaning. £80 to £160 a visit. Once a year on most houses, twice a year under heavy trees. So £80 to £320 a year.

Replacing uPVC guttering on a semi. £700 to £1,400 fitted, with a service life of 20 to 30 years. So £23 to £70 a year.

But. New guttering does not stop leaves falling into it. You still clean it. So replacement is not an alternative to cleaning, it is an addition.

Replacing your gutters does not buy you out of cleaning them. If someone tells you it does, they are selling gutters.

The correct comparison, therefore, is not clean against replace. It is: what does your existing gutter still do? If it holds water and carries it to the downpipe, cleaning is the whole job and it is cheap. If it does not, no amount of cleaning will make it, and you are choosing between replacement and accepting water on the wall.

2. What a failed gutter actually looks like

Easy to check from the ground, ideally during heavy rain, which is the only time a gutter tells you the truth.

Signs it needs cleaning, not replacing. Water overflowing at one specific point. Plants growing out of it. Visible debris above the rim. A downpipe that gurgles or backs up. Staining on the wall directly under one overflow point.

Signs it has failed. The gutter line visibly dips between brackets. Water drips steadily from a joint when it rains rather than overflowing the edge. The plastic has gone chalky, pale and brittle, so the clips crack when disturbed. Brackets are pulling away, usually because the fascia or soffit behind them has rotted. Cast iron with rust blisters and flaking paint.

The one that gets missed. A gutter that is correctly laid but falls the wrong way, so water sits in it permanently. You see standing water in a dry spell. That is a refixing job rather than a replacement, and it is much cheaper, but a replacement quote will rarely mention the option.

Materials differ in how they fail. uPVC, which is what most British houses have, goes brittle and discolours with ultraviolet light and typically gives 20 to 30 years. Cast iron, common on older and listed properties, lasts far longer but needs painting and is heavy, expensive and slow to repair. Aluminium, usually seamless and made on site, sits between the two on price and life. Those figures come from the roofing and rainwater trade, which sells the products, so treat the top of each range as a best case.

4. The bill nobody puts in the quote

A blocked gutter does not cost you a gutter. It costs you the wall.

Water overflowing at a single point runs down the face of the masonry, hour after hour, through every wet spell of a British winter. On a cavity wall the mortar and brick get saturated and the wall stops drying out between downpours. On a solid wall, which is most pre 1930s housing, the water moves straight through to the inside. On any wall, a downpipe discharging against the brickwork rather than into a gully does the same thing in a more concentrated way.

What you get, three to twelve months later, is a damp patch inside, usually high on an external wall, sometimes with mould. And what happens next is that a damp company is called, and a very expensive treatment for the wrong problem is proposed. Our guide to what a damp survey costs and what it misses covers that trap in detail: a leaking gutter is one of the commonest real causes of what gets diagnosed as rising damp.

So the honest way to price gutter cleaning is not as maintenance. It is as insurance against a four figure misdiagnosis, at £80 to £160 a year.

There is a second cost too. Water running down a wall keeps the fascia and soffit wet, which rots timber fascias and lets brackets pull out, which is how a cleaning problem becomes a replacement problem. Every year you skip the clean brings the replacement forward.

5. Worked example: the Bannerjee house in Rochdale

A 1960s semi in Rochdale with a large sycamore in the neighbouring garden. uPVC guttering, original, so about 35 years old. They have never had it cleaned. There is now an overflow at the corner above the back door and a faint damp patch appearing inside the back bedroom.

Three quotes, one from a gutter cleaning firm and two from roofline companies.

Quote A, clean and clear all gutters and downpipes. £140.

Quote B, replace all guttering, fascias and soffits. £2,300.

Quote C, replace guttering only, keep the fascias. £980.

The cleaning firm, who had nothing to sell beyond the clean, reported what mattered: the gutters are full of sycamore debris, the plastic is chalky but not cracked, one bracket is loose, and the fall is correct. In other words the gutter has not failed. It is full.

What they actually did. Clean at £140, plus £60 for the joiner to refix the loose bracket and check the fascia. Total £200.

Annual cost going forward. Two cleans a year because of the sycamore, so £280 a year.

Quote C spread over 25 years. £980 ÷ 25 = £39 a year, plus the same £280 a year of cleaning, because the sycamore does not care that the gutter is new.

What the damp patch needed. Nothing, once the overflow stopped. It dried out over the following spring.

So replacing the guttering would have cost £980 and changed the annual cleaning bill by nothing. The plastic is 35 years old and will need replacing within the next few years, and when it does, £39 a year is a perfectly reasonable price. But it did not need doing this autumn, and doing it this autumn would not have fixed the thing they were actually worried about.

The one part of quote B that deserved a second look was the fascias. If the timber behind the loose bracket had been soft, the answer would have changed, because a rotten fascia means brackets have nothing to hold on to and the guttering will keep failing whatever you do. The joiner checked. It was sound.

Frequently asked

How often should gutters be cleaned? Once a year for most houses, after the leaves have come down, so late autumn. Twice a year if you have deciduous trees close by or a shallow pitched roof that sheds moss into the gutter. If you have no trees at all and no moss, every two years is defensible.

Are gutter guards and brushes worth it? They reduce how often you need to clear big debris and they can make the clearing harder when it is needed, because fine silt and moss still get through and now sit under a cover. They are a reasonable buy on a hard to access run. They are not a substitute for ever looking at the gutter again, and anything sold on that basis is overpromising.

Can I do it myself? On a single storey extension or a bungalow, with a stable ladder and somebody footing it, many people do. On a two storey house, working at height off a ladder is how people end up in hospital, and £140 to have someone else take that risk is not expensive. Ground based pole vacuum systems avoid the ladder entirely and are what most cleaning firms now use.

Should I do the gutters at the same time as the roof? Yes, and this is the single biggest saving available. Scaffolding or tower hire is a large share of any work at height, and having it up once for the roof, the gutters, the fascias and the chimney is far cheaper than three separate visits. Our guide to roof replacement against repair makes the same point from the other direction.

The honest verdict

Get the gutters cleaned once a year and stop thinking about it. At £80 to £160 it is the cheapest maintenance a British house takes, and it prevents the one failure mode that turns into a four figure damp bill.

Do not replace guttering because it is dirty or discoloured. Replace it when it sags, when joints drip rather than overflow, when the plastic cracks on handling, or when the fascia behind it has gone. Before you accept a replacement quote, ask specifically whether rehanging and refixing the existing gutter would do, because at £200 to £500 it often would and nobody volunteers it.

And whatever you decide, if you ever have scaffolding up for anything else, do the gutters then. You are already paying for the expensive part.
