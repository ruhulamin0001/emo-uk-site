Put the deposit on a credit card and the whole holiday is protected

Two different things get called card protection and they are not remotely the same. One is an Act of Parliament. The other is an agreement between card companies. Knowing which you have decides whether you get your money back.

The short version

  • Section 75 is law. Credit card purchases costing more than £100 and up to £30,000, under the Consumer Credit Act.
  • The card company is equally liable with the seller. That is the whole point of it.
  • Partial payment counts. Per MoneyHelper, protection still applies even if you only paid the deposit on the credit card.
  • Chargeback is not law. It is a scheme agreement of Visa, Mastercard and American Express, with a 120 day claim window.
  • The £100 test is per item, not per bill. Two £95 items bought together do not qualify.

1. What section 75 actually gives you

Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act makes your credit card provider jointly liable with the seller. Not a goodwill scheme. Equal responsibility, in statute.

Per MoneyHelper, it applies where you use a credit card to buy something costing more than £100 and up to £30,000. It covers the situations that actually go wrong:

  • the company failed to supply the goods or services;
  • what it supplied was not up to standard;
  • the company misrepresented what it was supplying, MoneyHelper's example being a software supplier saying a package will work with a particular computer when it does not.

It also covers the case people most need it for: the company going bust. If the seller no longer exists, the card provider is still liable.

Now the detail that is worth more than everything else on this page. The protection still applies even if you only partly paid using your credit card, and MoneyHelper gives the deposit on a holiday as the example. Put £150 of a £3,000 holiday on a credit card and the protection can reach the whole purchase. Pay the lot by debit card or bank transfer and you have none of it.

2. The £100 rule people read wrong

Two traps, both from one sentence.

It is more than £100, not £100 or more. An item at exactly £100 does not qualify. A penny more does.

And it applies per item, not per bill. MoneyHelper is explicit: the requirement applies to each item or set of items you buy, rather than the total. Its example is buying a dress and a jacket that are not part of a suit, each costing £100 or less. A £190 basket of two £95 things is not protected. One £190 thing is.

The words "set of items" are doing work. A three piece suit sold as a suit is one item. Two garments bought together are not.

Lorna, Aberdeen. She books a £2,400 kitchen fitting and pays a £400 deposit by bank transfer because the fitter offered a discount for it, then the balance on a credit card on completion. Six weeks later the work is defective and the firm stops answering. Her credit card payment was well over £100, so section 75 is available on that. Had she done the reverse, deposit on the card and balance by transfer, she would still have been covered, because partial payment on a credit card is enough. Had she paid the whole thing by bank transfer, as the discount encouraged, she would have had no card route at all. The discount for paying by transfer is almost always smaller than the protection it removes.

3. What chargeback is, and its clock

Debit card payments are not covered by section 75. Instead you have chargeback, and MoneyHelper is careful with the wording: chargeback is not legal protection. It is an agreement Visa, Mastercard and American Express have signed up to.

The practical differences that decide cases:

  • There is a deadline. You must claim within 120 days of making the purchase. Where the purchase is for something in the future, such as event tickets, the 120 days starts from the day the event would have happened.
  • You can only reclaim what you put on that card. Section 75, by contrast, makes the provider liable alongside the seller for the purchase.
  • Try the company first, per MoneyHelper, before making a chargeback claim.
  • The mechanism is a reversal. The card company tries to claim your money back from the business you paid.

Chargeback is still worth using. It is simply weaker, faster to expire, and built on scheme rules rather than statute.

4. How to use either one

  1. Ask the seller first, in writing, and keep the reply or the silence. Both routes expect it.
  2. Work out which route you have. Credit card and over £100 per item means section 75. Debit card, or under the threshold, means chargeback and a 120 day clock.
  3. Claim to the card provider, not the seller. Under section 75 you are claiming against the provider in its own right.
  4. Escalate to the Financial Ombudsman Service if the provider refuses. Free, independent, and it can order a refund, compensation and costs.
  5. Do not wait. Section 75 has no short deadline of its own, but chargeback dies at 120 days and evidence gets harder to gather either way.

The verdict

The behaviour change is small and worth real money: for anything over £100 that you will not receive immediately, a holiday, a kitchen, a sofa, a wedding supplier, put at least part of it on a credit card and pay the card off. You are buying joint liability for the cost of a card transaction.

If you have already paid by debit card and something has gone wrong, start counting. Chargeback is 120 days from purchase, or from the event date for something in the future, and nobody will remind you.

Frequently asked

Does section 75 apply if I paid by debit card? No. MoneyHelper states debit card payments are not covered by section 75. You can ask your bank about chargeback instead.

Does it work if I only paid the deposit on the credit card? Yes. MoneyHelper says protection still applies even if you only partly paid by credit card, and gives a holiday deposit as the example.

What if my claim is turned down? Complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service. It is free and independent, and can order your card provider to refund you, pay compensation and cover costs.
