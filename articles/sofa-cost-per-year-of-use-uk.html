Nobody Asks The Only Question That Decides A Sofa: How Many Years?

Sofas are sold on price, fabric and delivery date. None of those tell you what the thing will actually cost you. Divide by the years it survives and something odd emerges: almost every new sofa on the market lands in the same narrow band.

Ruhul Amin Published 10 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Cost per year, not price, is the number. Purchase price divided by the years it survives in your house.
  • Almost every new sofa lands between £80 and £100 a year. A £400 budget one over 5 years is £80. A £2,000 hardwood one over 20 years is £100. The market has priced longevity in.
  • Only two things beat that band. A good second-hand frame at about £30 a year, and reupholstering what you own at about £47.
  • Four specifications decide the lifespan. Kiln-dried hardwood frame, serpentine or sprung base, foam density of 30kg per cubic metre or more, and a Martindale rub count above 25,000.
  • £120 of arm caps and a washable cover on a £900 sofa buys roughly three extra years, a payback of about 4.2 years. Modest, but it is the only purchase here with a payback at all.

1. Why nearly every sofa costs the same per year

The arithmetic in this series usually rewards buying the expensive thing, because the cheap version dies early. Our page on flooring cost per year is the clearest example: budget carpet is genuinely the most expensive floor you can own.

Sofas do not work that way, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than repeating a rule that does not fit.

The reason is that the sofa market is competitive and mature, and manufacturers know roughly what a frame, a spring system and a grade of foam are worth. A hardwood-framed sofa really does last three or four times as long as a stapled particleboard one, and it costs three or four times as much. Divide one by the other and the ratio very nearly cancels.

So the money question is not cheap or expensive. It is new or not new, because that is the only choice in the category where the ratio does not cancel. A twelve year old hardwood sofa has done maybe half its life and sells for perhaps 15 per cent of its original price, which is the single largest arbitrage in household furniture.

One warning about sources before the numbers. Almost every published estimate of sofa lifespan comes from a company that sells sofas, and those figures tend to be generous at the top end and vague at the bottom. Where a claim comes from a seller, treat it as a marketing range rather than a measurement. The figures below sit at the conservative end of those published ranges, and they assume a sofa in a busy family living room rather than a formal front room used twice a year.

Look at rows one to four. £80, £90, £100, £100. Five times the price for a twenty five per cent worse cost per year. That is the finding, and it means the usual advice to buy the best you can afford is, on pure money terms, wrong for sofas. It is right on comfort and on how the room feels, which are legitimate reasons and are not what this page measures.

Then look at rows five and six. £30 and £46.67. Those are not marginal improvements, they are half or less of everything above them, and they are available to anyone willing to buy something that is not brand new.

Row seven is the one purchase in this article with a payback in the usual shape. A £900 sofa expected to last 8 years in a house with children and a dog costs £112.50 a year. Add £120 of arm caps and a washable loose cover and call it 11 years, which is £92.73 a year. The saving is £19.77 a year, so £120 divided by £19.77 is 6.1 years on that basis, or about 4.2 years if you take the more generous three-extra-years-on-a-ten-year-sofa version. Either way it is real, it is small, and it is the only number here that behaves like the rest of this series.

3. The four specifications that set the lifespan

All of the above depends on knowing which row a given sofa is in, and a showroom will not tell you unless you ask. These four things are checkable, they are usually in the written specification, and together they decide almost everything.

One: the frame. Ask what it is made of. Kiln-dried hardwood, jointed and screwed or dowelled, is the thing that lasts twenty years. Softwood is acceptable in the mid-range. Particleboard or chipboard, stapled, is the five year answer, and it is what is inside most sofas under about £500. A frame guarantee of 15 years or more is a reasonable proxy if the salesperson does not know.

Two: the seat platform. Serpentine springs, the zig-zag steel type, or a fully sprung base, will hold their shape for many years. Elastic webbing is cheaper, lighter and sags first, and sagging webbing is usually what makes a sofa feel finished long before anything actually breaks.

Three: foam density. This is measured in kilograms per cubic metre and it is the single best predictor of whether the seat is still comfortable in year six. 30kg per cubic metre or above for seat cushions is a durable specification. Below about 25 the cushion will flatten noticeably within a couple of years. Feather or fibre wrapped foam is comfortable but needs plumping, which is a maintenance job rather than a durability problem.

Four: the fabric rub count. Upholstery fabrics are tested on a Martindale machine and given a rub count. Above 25,000 rubs is the usual threshold for general domestic use and 40,000 and above is heavy duty. This number is nearly always published, and it is the easiest of the four to check online before you go anywhere near a shop.

A sofa that is hardwood, serpentine sprung, 30kg foam and 40,000 Martindale is a twenty year sofa and can be reupholstered at the end of it. A sofa failing all four is a five year sofa and cannot. Everything in between is in between.

4. Worked example: Priya in Chesterfield

Priya has a nine year old three-seater that looks tired. The cushions have gone flat, the fabric is worn on the arms, and she has been quoted £1,750 for a new one of similar size.

Before ordering she looks underneath. The frame is screwed hardwood with serpentine springs, and nothing is broken. Only the foam and the fabric have failed, which are the two parts of a sofa that are designed to be replaceable.

She gets three quotes for reupholstery and new foam interiors. The middle one is £680 including a 40,000 Martindale fabric, and the upholsterer is confident the frame has another fifteen years in it.

New sofa: £1,750 over an expected 18 years is £97.22 a year.

Reupholstery: £680 over 15 further years is £45.33 a year.

Difference: £51.89 a year, and £1,070 that stays in her account this month.

She also prices the third option, which is a second-hand hardwood two-and-a-half seater from a local house clearance at £260. On a ten year remaining life that is £26 a year, the cheapest number available to her, but it is the wrong size for the room and she would still be looking at the tired one in the other corner. She passes, which is a reasonable trade of about £19 a year for a room that works.

One detail worth copying. She takes the old seat cushions to the upholsterer and asks for 32kg per cubic metre foam rather than the standard 28. It adds £55 and it is the part of the job most likely to determine whether she is happy with it in year eight.

5. The honest verdict

If your frame is hardwood and unbroken, do not buy a new sofa. Reupholster. At roughly £47 a year against £100 for new, it is half the cost of ownership and it is the clearest saving in this article.

If you are buying, buy second-hand hardwood if you possibly can. About £30 a year against £80 to £100 for anything new, and a good frame at twelve years old has done less than half its life.

If it has to be new, stop agonising over the price band. On cost per year a £400 sofa and a £2,000 sofa are within twenty per cent of each other. Choose on comfort, on the four specifications above, and on how long you actually intend to live with it.

And whatever you own, put arm caps on it. The arms wear out first, every time, and £120 of covers is the one thing in this category that pays for itself.

If you are buying

The two things worth spending on for an existing sofa are arm protection and a proper washable cover, because they attack the two failure modes that finish a sofa before its frame does. Check the fabric rub count on anything you buy.

Frequently asked

How do I tell if my frame is worth reupholstering? Tip the sofa on its back and look underneath the lining. You want visible timber, joints that are screwed, dowelled or bolted rather than stapled, and no movement when you push the arms sideways. If the corners flex or you can see chipboard edges, it is not worth the labour cost.

Is reupholstery ever more expensive than replacing? Yes, on a small or low-value sofa. The labour is much the same regardless of what it is applied to, so a £700 job on a frame that would cost £600 to replace new makes no sense. It is the expensive frames where the arithmetic works, which is the opposite of most people's instinct.

What about sofas sold with a ten year guarantee? Read what it covers. Frame guarantees are usually long and genuine. Foam, fabric and springs, which are the parts that actually fail, are commonly covered for one or two years. A long headline guarantee on a sofa is nearly always a frame guarantee.

Do fabric protection treatments sold in store pay for themselves? Rarely at showroom prices, which are often £100 or more for a treatment you can buy in a can. The washable cover in the table above does the same job more reliably and comes off to go in the machine, which no treatment does.
