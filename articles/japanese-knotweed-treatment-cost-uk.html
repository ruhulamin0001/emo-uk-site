The Plant That Costs More in Mortgage Terms Than in Herbicide

Japanese knotweed is treatable and the treatment is not especially expensive. The expensive part is time, because the cheap route takes five years and a buyer's lender wants an answer in five weeks. Here are the two routes priced honestly, and the categories that decide whether anyone will lend on the house.

The short version

  • A herbicide programme typically runs three to five years and costs in the low thousands, with an insurance-backed guarantee that is the part lenders actually want to see.

1. What the law actually says, and what it does not

Start here, because the myths in this area are expensive.

Having knotweed on your land is not an offence. There is no duty to report it, no duty to remove it, and no criminal liability simply for owning a garden it has arrived in.

Planting it or causing it to grow in the wild is an offence under section 14 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. In practice that means you cannot tip it over a hedge, fly-tip the soil, or dump cuttings on a verge.

Soil and plant material containing knotweed is controlled waste under the Environmental Protection Act 1990, so it has to go to a licensed facility with the right permit, on a duty of care transfer note. That disposal requirement is the single largest reason excavation costs what it costs.

Allowing it to encroach on a neighbour can be a private nuisance. The courts have been clear that the actionable damage is interference with the neighbour's quiet enjoyment and use of their land, and a homeowner who knows it is there and does nothing is in a considerably worse position than one who has a treatment plan running.

And you have to declare it. Question 7.8 of the standard TA6 form asks directly. Answering no while knowing the answer is yes is misrepresentation, and buyers have successfully sued over it.

2. The two treatment routes, and why one takes five years

Herbicide treatment. A specialist applies glyphosate, usually as a foliar spray in late summer when the plant is drawing resources down into the rhizome, and repeats over successive seasons. The reason it takes years is that the rhizome is the plant. Knock the visible growth down and the rhizome sits dormant, and you cannot prove it is dead by looking at bare soil. A credible programme therefore includes monitoring visits after the last application, typically taking the whole thing to three to five years.

The output that matters is not the spraying. It is the insurance-backed guarantee, usually ten years, transferable to a buyer and to their lender. Without one, a treatment programme has very little value in a transaction.

Excavation and removal. Dig out the rhizome and the surrounding soil and remove it from site. Variants include on-site burial under a root barrier membrane at depth, and screening to reduce the volume going to landfill, both of which cut the disposal cost where the site allows.

Excavation is fast and expensive. The cost drivers are volume of soil, access for plant, landfill tax on contaminated waste, and how close the growth is to the building, since nobody is excavating a two-metre pit against a shallow Victorian foundation without engineering input.

3. The RICS management categories that decide the mortgage

The RICS professional standard reissued in 2022 replaced the old proximity-based risk categories with four management categories, assessed on the effect on the property rather than distance alone. This mattered because the old seven-metre rule produced absurd results, red-flagging a house because of a plant across a railway line while under-weighting one growing through a conservatory floor.

  • Category A. Significant impact on the property, its structure or its access. Remediation by a specialist under a management plan with an insurance-backed guarantee, and a mortgage retention is a realistic prospect.
  • Category B. Knotweed present and requiring specialist remediation with a guarantee, but the impact is less severe than category A.
  • Category C. Knotweed visible on the property but not causing damage. No retention recommendation, though lenders commonly want evidence of a plan.
  • Category D. Knotweed present off site, within three metres of the boundary.

The practical translation is simple. Categories A and B mean a lender will typically want a management plan and a transferable guarantee in place before completion. Category C usually proceeds on evidence of treatment. Category D is generally a matter for disclosure and monitoring.

Worked example: the Voss household, Basildon

Knotweed found at the bottom of a 30-metre garden, roughly 22 metres from the house, during a Level 3 survey. Assessed as category C: present, visible, no structural impact.

Quote one, herbicide over four years: £3,400, with a ten-year insurance-backed guarantee issued after the final monitoring visit. That is £850 a year.

Quote two, excavation and removal: £11,800, largely because the only plant access ran through a neighbour's driveway and the soil volume was high.

The decision. They were not selling, the stand was nowhere near the building, and the category C assessment meant no retention was recommended. They took the herbicide programme, and the guarantee document went straight into the house file for whenever they do sell.

The difference, £8,400, went nowhere near the garden. It stayed in the account. Had the stand been against the rear wall and category A, the arithmetic would have reversed entirely, because the mortgage timetable, not the plant, would have been setting the deadline.

5. How to choose a contractor without being taken

This is a market with a strong incentive to frighten, because fear shortens the decision and raises the price. Four questions separate the professionals from the rest.

Are you a member of a recognised trade body? The Property Care Association and the Invasive Non-Native Specialists Association are the two that lenders and surveyors recognise. Membership is checkable on their own registers.

Is the guarantee insurance-backed, and by whom? A guarantee from the contractor alone is worth exactly as much as the contractor's continued existence. An insurance-backed guarantee survives the company folding, which over a ten-year term is not a hypothetical concern.

Will you put the management plan in writing before I pay anything? A proper plan states the treatment method, the number of visits, the monitoring period, what triggers an extra visit, and what happens if regrowth appears in year seven. Anyone unwilling to write that down is selling visits rather than an outcome.

Have you identified the plant? Bindweed, bamboo, dogwood and Russian vine are all mistaken for knotweed regularly, and one of them is considerably cheaper to deal with than the others. Ask for photographs of the identifying features rather than accepting a verdict over the phone.

6. The honest verdict

If you are staying put and the growth is away from the building, take the herbicide programme from a properly accredited contractor and insist on the insurance-backed guarantee. It is a third of the price of digging and the extra years cost you nothing you actually need.

If you are selling inside two years, or the growth is at or under the building, excavation is usually the right answer despite the price, because a buyer's lender will not wait four years for a monitoring visit.

Do not treat it yourself if there is any chance of a sale. The chemicals work. The paperwork is what you are actually buying, and you cannot issue yourself a guarantee.

Frequently asked

Does knotweed really damage foundations? It exploits existing weaknesses rather than boring through sound concrete. It will find a crack in a drain, a gap in a wall or a joint in a patio and widen it. That is damaging enough, and it is why proximity to the structure moves the category.

Can I refuse to buy a house because of knotweed? Of course, and many buyers do. A more measured response is to price it: get a specialist assessment, obtain quotes for both routes, and negotiate on the higher one.

My neighbour has it and will not treat it. What can I do? Document it with dated photographs, write to them formally, and take legal advice on a private nuisance claim. Encroachment onto your land that interferes with your use and enjoyment is the recognised route.

Is it safe to compost or burn it? No to composting: a fragment of rhizome the size of a fingernail can regenerate. Burning is possible but the residue and any soil remain controlled waste, and it is a job for a licensed contractor rather than a garden bonfire.
