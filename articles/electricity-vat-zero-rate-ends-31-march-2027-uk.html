The Electricity VAT Cut Has an End Date, and It Is Closer Than the Coverage Suggests

Ofgem states the position in one line: the government has removed VAT from electricity bills from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. That is a six month measure with a date on it. Here is what comes back on 1 April 2027 if nothing changes, and the second thing landing on the same day that nobody is pairing it with.

The short version

  • The zero rate runs 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. Ofgem states both dates. It is temporary by design.
  • Gas was never zero rated. Gas stays at 5% throughout, so none of this touches the gas half of your bill.
  • At the current cap rate, 5% on electricity is 1.32p a kWh. The unit rate would go from 26.32p to about 27.64p.
  • The standing charge is caught too. 54.83p a day plus 5% is about £10 a year on its own.
  • Two changes land on 1 April 2027, not one. VAT returning, and a new cap level announced on 23 February 2027.
  • Nothing is decided yet. An extension is possible. What is published is an end date, and that is what to plan around.

1. What Ofgem actually says, and the two dates

The detail of the cut itself, who it helps most and why the headline cap number moved the way it did, is covered in our guide to the October electricity VAT cut. This page is about one thing only: the end date.

Ofgem's announcement of the 1 October to 31 December 2026 cap is explicit. Electricity prices for capped households do not include VAT from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. Gas rates include VAT at 5% and are unaffected.

So there are two dates in the measure, and the coverage in August concentrated almost entirely on the first one.

1 October 2026. VAT on domestic electricity goes to zero. This has happened.

31 March 2027. The stated end of the zero rate period.

Be clear about what that second date is and is not. It is the end of the announced measure, not a decision to reimpose anything. A government can extend a temporary rate, and often does. But the only thing published is an end date, and planning around what is written down beats planning around what might be announced.

2. What 5% costs at today's rates

This is calculable exactly, because Ofgem publishes the rates the cut applies to. The figures below use the cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, direct debit, England, Scotland and Wales average. Your region differs, and the cap changes every three months, so treat this as the size of the effect rather than a prediction of your April bill.

Electricity unit rate. 26.32p per kWh with no VAT. Add 5% and it is 27.64p. The difference is 1.32p per kWh.

Electricity standing charge. 54.83p a day with no VAT. Add 5% and it is 57.57p. That is 2.74p a day, or £10.01 a year, before you use a single unit of electricity.

Gas. 7.97p per kWh and 29.68p a day, both already including 5% VAT. Unchanged by any of this.

The standing charge is the part people miss. VAT applies to the whole electricity bill, not just consumption, so even a household using very little electricity picks up roughly £10 a year from it.

3. Worked example: the Osei household in Reading

The Osei household, Reading. A two bedroom flat, electricity and gas, on a standard variable tariff paying by direct debit. They use 2,700 kWh of electricity a year. That is their own figure from their annual statement, which is where yours should come from too rather than from an average.

On consumption. 2,700 kWh at 1.32p of returning VAT is £35.53 a year.

On the standing charge. £10.01 a year.

Total effect of VAT returning: about £45.54 a year, or £3.80 a month, at today's unit rates.

What it is not. It is not a £45 price rise announced by anyone. It is the arithmetic of a temporary rate ending, applied to rates that will themselves have changed twice by then.

4. The second change landing on the same day

This is the part worth knowing, because it decides what your April bill actually looks like.

Ofgem reviews the cap every three months. Its published schedule sets the next two announcements at 25 November 2026, for the January to March 2027 period, and 23 February 2027, for the April to June 2027 period.

So 1 April 2027 is the day two separate things change at once: the cap moves to whatever is announced in February, and the zero rate is scheduled to end. Those are independent. The cap could fall and still leave you paying more, or rise and be partly offset if the zero rate is extended.

So you cannot read your April bill against your March bill and conclude anything about either change on its own. The number to compare is the unit rate excluding VAT, because that is the only figure the VAT change does not distort. Ofgem made the same point in August, warning that costs cannot be compared directly to previous periods.

5. What is worth doing between now and then

Honestly, for most households: not much, and being told that is more useful than being sold an action.

  • Do not fix a tariff because of this. A six month VAT measure is no reason to lock into a long fix. Whether to fix depends on wholesale prices, covered in our piece on the January 2027 cap forecast.
  • Do check your direct debit in April, not March. With two changes landing at once, that is the month to look at the numbers rather than accept a new figure.
  • Do get your own consumption figure. Your annual statement has your kWh. Multiplying it by 1.32p tells you your exposure in ten seconds, and beats any average.
  • Watch 25 November. The January cap announcement will say more about your winter bill than the VAT date will.

The honest verdict

The zero rate is worth having and it has a published end date of 31 March 2027. At the rates in force today, losing it is worth roughly 1.32p a kWh plus about £10 a year on the standing charge, which for a modest flat is somewhere near £45 a year and for a high electricity user meaningfully more.

The reason to know this now is not to act on it. It is so that when the April bill arrives you can tell which part of the change was VAT and which part was the cap, because the two land on the same morning and the coverage will almost certainly blur them. Keep your own kWh figure, compare unit rates excluding VAT, and treat any confident prediction of your April bill, including this one, as arithmetic rather than forecast.

Frequently asked

Is the zero rate definitely ending? What is published is an end date of 31 March 2027. That is the stated scope of the measure. Governments can and do extend temporary rates, so an extension is possible, but no extension has been announced. Plan around the published date and update if that changes.

Does this affect my gas bill? No. Gas has carried 5% VAT throughout and Ofgem's own figures state that the gas unit rate and standing charge include VAT at 5%. The zero rate applied to electricity only, so gas is unaffected in both directions.

I am on a fixed tariff. Does VAT still apply to me? VAT is a tax on the supply, not a feature of the price cap, so it applies to fixed tariffs too. What differs is that your unit rate is set by your contract rather than by the cap, so the VAT change moves your bill without your unit rate changing.
