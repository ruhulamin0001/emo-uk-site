Notice periods: one week from you, one week per year from them, twelve at most

Statutory notice is deliberately one-sided, and most people do not realise it. Your obligation stays at a week however long you stay. Theirs grows every year you are there, and no contract can cut it below the floor.

Ruhul Amin Published 6 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • You must give at least a week's notice once you have been in the job more than a month (GOV.UK, page updated 20 August 2026). That figure does not grow with service unless your contract says so.
  • Your employer must give one week between one month and 2 years, one week for every year between 2 and 12 years, and 12 weeks at 12 years or more (GOV.UK, page updated 2 September 2026).
  • A contract can add to the statutory minimum but cannot cut it. A clause saying "one month either way" is lawful for your side and unenforceable against you on theirs once you pass four years.
  • Notice pay is based on your average weekly earnings over the 12 weeks before notice starts, not on your basic rate on the day.
  • Your notice period usually runs from the day after you hand it in, not from the day you hand it in.

1. What you owe them

GOV.UK's giving notice page is short and blunt: "You must give at least a week's notice if you've been in your job for more than a month."

That is the entire statutory obligation on an employee. It does not rise with service. Someone who has been somewhere eleven years owes exactly the same statutory week as someone who has been there five weeks, unless their contract says otherwise, which it usually does.

Some practical points from the same page.

Check whether it has to be in writing. Your contract will say. If it does not require writing, verbal notice is valid. Do it in writing anyway. GOV.UK's own advice: "Give written notice if you think you'll need to refer to it later, for example at an employment tribunal." An email with a date and a stated last working day costs nothing and settles arguments.

Notice runs from the day after. "Your notice period usually runs from the start of the day after you handed your notice in." So notice given on a Monday starts running on the Tuesday. On a one-week notice period that single day matters, and on a resignation timed around a pay date or a pension contribution it can matter a lot.

Getting it wrong is a breach of contract. GOV.UK says you may be in breach if you do not give enough notice, or give it verbally when it should have been in writing, and that your employer could take you to court. In practice that is rare for ordinary employees and much less rare for senior or specialist staff whose departure costs the business real money.

Heat of the moment. If you resign during an argument and change your mind, GOV.UK says to tell your employer immediately. They can choose to accept the resignation or not. The word "immediately" is the operative one: waiting a week and then trying to withdraw is a much weaker position than saying so the same afternoon.

2. What they owe you

The employer side is a sliding scale, and it is set out on GOV.UK's redundancy notice periods page.

  • at least one week's notice if employed between one month and 2 years
  • one week's notice for each year if employed between 2 and 12 years
  • 12 weeks' notice if employed for 12 years or more

In table form, which is easier to check yourself against:

Complete years of serviceStatutory notice from your employer
Under 1 monthNone
1 month to under 2 years1 week
2 years2 weeks
5 years5 weeks
9 years9 weeks
12 years or more12 weeks (the cap)

Note that it counts complete years. Nine years and eleven months is nine weeks, not ten. And it caps at twelve: twenty years of service still produces twelve weeks.

Although the page sits inside GOV.UK's redundancy guide, the statutory minimum notice applies to dismissals generally, not only to redundancy. The exception is dismissal for gross misconduct, where an employer can dismiss without notice.

Notice pay. GOV.UK says your notice pay is based on the average you earned per week over the 12 weeks before your notice period starts. That matters if your pay is variable: regular overtime, commission or shift premiums in those 12 weeks lift your notice pay above your basic rate. It also means the timing of a quiet period can pull it down, which is worth checking rather than accepting.

3. What your contract can and cannot do

One line on GOV.UK settles most of the arguments people have about this: "Your employer may give you more than the statutory minimum, but they cannot give you less."

So the statutory scale is a floor, not a default. A contract can improve on it and frequently does, particularly for senior roles where three or six months is normal. A contract cannot go below it, and a clause that tries to is simply unenforceable to that extent.

This is where the very common "one month's notice on either side" clause becomes interesting. For your obligation, one month is more than the statutory week, so it binds you. For their obligation, one month is roughly 4.3 weeks, which beats the statute up to four years of service and falls below it from five years onwards. After five years, that clause does not reduce what they owe you. It just sits there looking authoritative.

Read your contract for the higher figure, then read the statutory scale, and take whichever is greater on each side. That is genuinely the whole rule.

4. Marcus in Redditch: nine years, and the month his contract claimed

Marcus has worked for a components supplier in Redditch for nine years and four months. He earns £34,000 with no overtime or commission. His contract says one month's notice on either side. His role is being made redundant.

What his employer first offered: one month's notice, per the contract. On his salary that is £2,833.33.

What the statute requires: nine complete years of service, so nine weeks. His average weekly earnings are £34,000 divided by 52, which is £653.85. Nine weeks is £5,884.62.

The difference: £3,051.29, for one email pointing at the GOV.UK page.

The contract is not wrong exactly. It is just below the floor, and the floor wins. Marcus's own obligation, had he resigned instead, would have been the contractual month, because that is more than his statutory week.

Two things Marcus should also be checking, which are separate from notice:

  • Statutory redundancy pay is a different payment entirely, calculated on age, service and weekly pay. Notice pay does not replace it and one does not reduce the other.
  • Accrued holiday. He keeps building up leave throughout his notice period, and any statutory leave he has not taken by his last day has to be paid.

If he had been there twelve years and four months rather than nine, his notice would have been twelve weeks, which is the cap, and would have stayed twelve weeks however much longer he had been there.

5. Pay in lieu, garden leave and the holiday you have built up

Payment in lieu of notice. GOV.UK: your employment can end without notice if payment in lieu of notice is in your contract, and your employer pays you instead of giving you a notice period. You get all of the basic pay you would have received during the notice period, and you may get extras such as pension contributions or private health cover if they are in your contract.

An employer can also offer pay in lieu even where the contract does not mention it. If you accept, GOV.UK says you should receive full pay and any contractual extras. The word "accept" matters: this is an offer you can decline if you would rather stay employed for the notice period, for example because you are close to a service milestone, a bonus date or a pension vesting point.

Garden leave is different again. You remain employed and paid but are told not to come in and often not to contact clients. Your service keeps running, your benefits continue and your restrictive covenants usually start from your actual termination date. GOV.UK has separate pages on garden leave and restrictive covenants, and both are worth reading before you sign with a competitor.

Holiday. Statutory holiday continues to accrue during your notice period, including while you are on garden leave. Anything statutory and untaken at the end must be paid. An employer can also require you to take accrued holiday during your notice, which is lawful if the correct notice of that requirement is given.

The tax point. Notice pay is earnings and is taxed as pay through PAYE, whether it is paid as you work the notice or as a payment in lieu. It is not part of the tax-free redundancy amount, and people are regularly surprised by that when the final payslip arrives.

If any of this is disputed, Acas gives free advice on its helpline and Citizens Advice will go through your contract with you. Both are better first steps than a solicitor, and Acas early conciliation is a required step before most tribunal claims anyway.

The verdict

Work out two numbers before you accept anything: your contractual notice and your statutory notice. On your side of the deal, whichever is longer binds you. On theirs, whichever is longer binds them, and the statutory scale of one week per complete year up to twelve is a floor their contract cannot cut. If you have been somewhere five years or more and your contract says one month, they owe you more than that and the correction is one polite email. Then check your redundancy pay and your accrued holiday separately, because neither of them is included in the notice figure.

Frequently asked

Can I be sacked without any notice at all? Only for gross misconduct, where an employer can dismiss summarily. In every other case the statutory minimum applies from one month of service. If you have been dismissed with no notice and no allegation of gross misconduct, that is a wrongful dismissal claim for the notice pay, quite separate from any unfair dismissal question.

Does my notice period count towards my length of service? Yes, if you work it. Statutory notice counts towards continuous service, which can matter if you are close to a two-year or five-year threshold. That is one reason to think before accepting a payment in lieu that ends your employment early.

What if I want to leave sooner than my notice period? Ask. Many employers will release you early, particularly in a redundancy situation where they save the wages. If they refuse and you walk out anyway, you are in breach of contract, and while being sued is uncommon it is not unheard of, and the reference conversation gets awkward.

Is notice pay taxed? Yes. Notice pay is earnings, whether worked or paid in lieu, and goes through PAYE with tax and National Insurance. It does not fall inside the tax-free element of a redundancy package.
