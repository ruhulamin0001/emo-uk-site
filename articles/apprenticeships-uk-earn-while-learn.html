£8 an Hour and No Student Debt: The Apprenticeship Trade-Off, With the Numbers

Apprenticeships are usually sold on one line: earn while you learn, no debt. Both halves are true. What the brochures leave out is how little you earn in year one, when the rate legally has to rise, and that almost four in ten people do not finish.

The short version

  • The apprentice minimum wage is £8.00 an hour from April 2026. It applies if you are under 19, or 19 and over and in the first year.
  • After year one, if you are 21 or over, you jump to the full National Living Wage of £12.71. That is a 59% pay rise on an anniversary, and plenty of apprentices do not know to check it.
  • The national achievement rate is 65.4% for 2024/25. Which means roughly one in three does not complete. The dropout figure improved from 44.1% to 38.1% in the most recent comparison, but it is still high.
  • Levels run from 2 to 7. Level 4 is roughly the first year of a degree, and Levels 6 and 7 are degree and master's apprenticeships, paid, with no tuition fees.
  • The debt comparison is real. No tuition fee, no maintenance loan, and you are paid throughout. That is the strongest argument and it does not need exaggerating.

1. What you actually get paid, and when it rises

From April 2026 the rates are:

Who you areMinimum per hourRoughly, at 37.5 hours a week
Apprentice under 19, any year£8.00about £15,600 a year
Apprentice 19 or over, first year£8.00about £15,600 a year
Apprentice 18 to 20, after first year£10.85about £21,150 a year
Apprentice 21 or over, after first year£12.71about £24,780 a year

The single most valuable thing in this article: the apprentice rate only applies for the first year if you are 19 or over. On the first anniversary of your apprenticeship, if you are 21 or over, your legal minimum becomes the full National Living Wage. Going from £8.00 to £12.71 is a 59% increase, and it does not happen automatically in every payroll. Check your payslip the month after your anniversary. If it did not change, raise it, and if that goes nowhere, HMRC enforces minimum wage.

These are minimums. Many employers, particularly in engineering, construction and finance, pay well above them, so do not treat £8.00 as the expected rate. Ask what the actual rate is and what it becomes in year two.

£8.00 to £12.71 on your first anniversary is a 59% pay rise that some employers simply do not apply. It is your job to check the payslip.

2. The levels, in plain English

LevelNameRoughly equivalent to
2IntermediateGCSEs at grade 4 to 9
3AdvancedA levels
4 and 5HigherFirst year or two of a degree, foundation degree
6Degree apprenticeshipBachelor's degree
7Degree apprenticeshipMaster's degree

Level 2 has the fewest entry requirements and is the usual starting point if you left school without much. Level 3 is the natural next step and is where most trades sit. Levels 6 and 7 are the ones worth understanding properly: you get the degree, the employer pays the tuition, and you are on a salary the whole time.

Degree apprenticeships are competitive, often more competitive than the equivalent university course, because the number of places is set by how many employers want them rather than by how many students apply. Apply early and apply widely.

3. Why almost four in ten do not finish

The national apprenticeship achievement rate reached 65.4% for the 2024/25 academic year, just short of the government's long standing 67% target. In 2023/24, 38.1% of apprentices dropped out before completing, an improvement on 44.1% the year before, but still more than a third.

That is a high failure rate for something people are told is the safe practical option, and the reasons are consistent and mostly predictable before you start.

  • The money is too tight for too long. At £8.00 an hour, an apprentice living independently is under real pressure. People leave for a job paying £12.71 immediately, which is a rational decision, not a lack of commitment.
  • The off the job training does not happen. You are entitled to a set proportion of your time on training. Where the employer treats you as cheap labour and the training slides, the qualification does not arrive and people give up.
  • The employer is the wrong fit. An apprenticeship is a job first. A bad workplace ends it the same way it would end any job.
  • The end point assessment is a real exam. People underestimate it because the day job is going well, then fail at the last hurdle.

The useful reading of that 38%: most of it is decided by which employer you pick, not by the qualification. Interrogate the employer harder than the course.

4. Apprenticeship against university, five years out

Two 18 year olds in Manchester, same starting point, September 2026.

Jordan takes a Level 3 engineering apprenticeship. Year one at £8.00 an hour is about £15,600. Year two, now 19 and past the first year, £10.85 is about £21,150. Year three, now 20, similar. Year four, qualified, on a technician salary of roughly £30,000. Year five, £32,000.

Five year earnings: roughly £120,000. Debt: £0.

Sam goes to university. Three years of study, no salary, plus part time work of maybe £5,000 a year. Graduates with tuition and maintenance debt. Years four and five in a graduate role at around £28,000 then £31,000.

Five year earnings: roughly £74,000. Debt: substantial, repaid as a percentage of income above the threshold for up to forty years.

The honest caveats, because this comparison is often presented dishonestly. Jordan is ahead at year five and it is not close. But graduate salary curves are steeper in several fields, and by year fifteen the ranking can reverse depending on sector. A student loan is not a normal debt: it is repaid as a percentage of income above a threshold and written off eventually, so comparing it to a bank loan overstates it. And Sam has a degree that moves between industries more easily than a sector specific qualification.

What the comparison actually shows: if you know what work you want to do, the apprenticeship route is financially stronger for a long time. If you do not know, university buys optionality, and that has a price.

5. How to judge an apprenticeship before accepting

You are choosing an employer, not a course. Ask these before you say yes.

  • What is the actual hourly rate, now and in year two? Not the legal minimum, theirs.
  • What proportion of my week is off the job training, and where does it happen? If they are vague, that is the answer.
  • What is your completion rate? A good employer knows it. A bad one will not have thought about it.
  • How many of your last five apprentices are still employed here? The single most revealing question you can ask.
  • Who is my assessor and how often do I see them? The training provider matters as much as the employer.
  • Is there a job at the end, and at what salary? An apprenticeship that ends with "we will see" is a fixed term job with homework.

6. Your rights, which are the same as any employee

  • You are an employee with a contract of employment. Not a student on placement.
  • At least 20 days paid holiday plus bank holidays for full time work, the same as anyone else.
  • Paid time for off the job training. It is part of the apprenticeship, not something to do in your evenings.
  • Sick pay, pension auto enrolment and the same protections as any other employee once you meet the usual criteria.
  • The right to be paid at least minimum wage, enforced by HMRC. If your rate did not rise after your first year, that is a minimum wage issue and you can report it.

The honest verdict

An apprenticeship is a genuinely strong route if you know roughly what you want to do, and a weak one if you are using it to avoid deciding. The financial case is real: five years in, you are likely to be tens of thousands ahead with no tuition debt, and a Level 6 or 7 apprenticeship gets you the degree anyway, paid.

The risk is not the qualification, it is the employer. With more than a third not finishing, the questions in section five are the whole game. Ask them, and treat a vague answer about off the job training as a refusal.

And put a reminder in your phone for your first anniversary. £8.00 to £12.71 is a 59% pay rise you may have to ask for.

Frequently asked

Can I do an apprenticeship at 30 or 40? Yes. There is no upper age limit in England. If you are 21 or over you get the apprentice rate for the first year only, then the full National Living Wage.

Do I pay for the training? No. The employer and government fund it. You should never be asked to contribute towards apprenticeship training costs.

Can I go to university afterwards? Yes. A Level 3 apprenticeship is broadly equivalent to A levels for entry purposes, though individual universities set their own requirements.

What if my employer goes under mid apprenticeship? Your training provider should help you find a new employer to continue with. Contact them immediately rather than waiting, because the clock matters.
