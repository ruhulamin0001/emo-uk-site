How to Set Your Freelance Rate in the UK Without Undercharging

21 July 2026 · 6 min read · Checked July 2026

The short version
  • Your day rate is not your salary divided by 260. You do not get 260 billable days. Plan for about 200 working days and 130 to 150 billable ones.
  • UK benchmarks for 2026: copywriters average around £480 a day, designers sit roughly £300 to £700, developers £400 to £900 plus. The average across all IT freelance roles is about £576 a day.
  • The national median developer day rate rose to about £500, up 26% from £457 in early 2024. If you have not moved your rate since 2024 you have taken a real terms pay cut.
  • London adds 20 to 30%. Manchester, Birmingham and Bristol add 5 to 10%. Remote work is quietly closing that gap in the client's favour and the freelancer's.
  • Set the rate against profit you need, not against what the last client paid. Then check it against the benchmark, not the other way round.

1. The hourly rate trap

Almost every new freelancer starts the same way. They take the salary they used to earn, divide it by 1,950 hours, get something like £18 an hour, and charge that. Then they wonder why they are working constantly and still short at the end of the month.

Three things break that sum.

You do not bill every hour you work. Quoting, invoicing, chasing invoices, marketing, bookkeeping, admin, learning, sales calls that go nowhere. On a healthy freelance business, roughly 60 to 70% of your working time is billable. The rest is running the business, and nobody pays you for it directly.

You have no paid holiday, no sick pay, no employer pension contribution. An employer on a £35,000 salary is spending closer to £41,000 once National Insurance and pension are included, plus 28 days of paid leave. All of that now comes out of your rate.

Hourly billing punishes you for being good. If you get faster, you earn less for the same result. That is a genuinely perverse incentive and it is the main reason experienced freelancers move to day rates and project fees.

2. The maths your rate has to survive

Work backwards from the money you actually need, not forwards from an hourly figure.

  1. Target take home. What do you need in the bank, after tax, per year?
  2. Gross it up for tax. Add roughly 25 to 30% for income tax and National Insurance at typical freelance profit levels. More if you are into higher rate.
  3. Add business costs. Software, insurance, accountant, equipment, phone, travel, professional bodies, training. For most solo freelancers this is £1,500 to £4,000 a year.
  4. Divide by billable days, not working days. 52 weeks minus 5 weeks holiday, minus 8 bank holidays, minus a fortnight for illness and dead time gives about 200 working days. Multiply by a 65 to 70% billable ratio and you get roughly 130 to 140 billable days.

That last step is where nearly everyone goes wrong. Dividing by 260 instead of 135 makes your rate almost half what it needs to be.

3. Worked example: Priya in Leeds

Priya is a freelance graphic designer. She wants £32,000 in her pocket after tax.

£340 is the floor, not the target. It is the number below which Priya is working for less than she thinks. The 2026 designer benchmark runs £300 to £700, so £340 sits at the bottom of the market. With four years of experience and a portfolio, £425 to £475 is a defensible ask, and at £450 she hits her target in 103 days rather than 136. That is the whole argument for pricing properly: the same income with a third less work, or the same work and a great deal more money.

Copywriting is also often priced per word. Beginners realistically get 5p to 10p a word. Established specialists in technical or financial niches command up to 50p a word. That is a tenfold spread for the same job title, and the difference is almost entirely positioning and proof, not writing ability.

5. London, the regions, and what remote changed

London still carries a premium of roughly 20 to 30% over national rates. A developer on £500 nationally is a £600 to £650 contractor in the capital. Manchester, Birmingham and Bristol add a more modest 5 to 10%.

The interesting shift in 2026 is that remote work is eroding that gap from both directions. London clients are hiring remote contractors at close to national rates, which pulls the London premium down. But it also means a freelancer in Hull or Swansea can bill a London client without London costs, which is a genuine, permanent improvement in regional earning power.

Practical takeaway: if you are outside London and pricing against local competitors, you are pricing against the wrong market. Price against the client's market, not your postcode.

6. Day rate, project rate, or value pricing

Hourly. Fine for open ended support work and small ad hoc jobs. Bad for anything with a defined outcome, because it caps your income at your available hours and rewards slowness.

Day rate. The default for design, development and consultancy. Easy for clients to budget, easy for you to forecast. Watch out for the half day, which somehow always becomes six hours.

Project fee. One price for a defined deliverable. Better margin if you are efficient, and clients generally prefer the certainty. It only works with a written scope. Without one, you have signed up for unlimited work at a fixed price.

Value pricing. The fee reflects what the outcome is worth to the client, not the time it takes. A landing page that lifts conversion by two points on a £2m revenue line is worth far more than two days of work. This is where the real money is, and it requires you to understand the client's numbers well enough to have that conversation credibly.

Whichever you use, put a revision limit in writing. Unlimited revisions is the single most common way freelancers turn a good rate into a bad one.

7. Raising your rate on an existing client

The fear is that they will leave. Some will. In practice the ones who leave over a 10% increase are usually the ones costing you the most in chasing and scope creep.

  1. Give notice. Six to eight weeks. New rate applies from a specific date, and to work commissioned after that date.
  2. State it, do not ask it. "From 1 November my day rate moves to £475." Not "would it be possible to maybe increase".
  3. Give one reason, briefly. Costs, demand, or a change in what you deliver. Do not write three paragraphs justifying yourself.
  4. Have a fallback ready. If they push back, offer to hold the old rate for existing committed work, or offer a slightly reduced scope at the old price. Never just cave to the old number with nothing changed.
  5. Do it annually. A rate that never moves is a rate falling in real terms every year.

8. Tax, the bit that eats the difference

Your rate is revenue. Your income is what survives HMRC.

  • The personal allowance is £12,570, and the government has confirmed it stays there from 6 April 2026 through to 5 April 2028. Frozen thresholds mean rising real tax as your rate rises.
  • Class 2 National Insurance is £3.65 a week for 2026 to 2027, and is voluntary for most self-employed people with profits above the small profits threshold, though paying it protects your State Pension record.
  • Class 4 National Insurance is charged on profits above £12,570, at the main rate up to the upper profits limit and a lower rate above it. Check the current percentage on GOV.UK before you rely on it, because it has moved twice in three years.
  • The VAT registration threshold is £90,000 of taxable turnover in any rolling 12 months. Cross it and you must register. If your clients are VAT registered businesses, registering is often neutral or positive. If they are consumers, it is effectively a 20% price rise you cannot pass on.
  • Payments on account catch people out badly in year two. If your bill is over £1,000, HMRC asks for next year's tax in two instalments on top of this year's. Your first January as a freelancer can be a bill you did not budget for.

Put 30% of every invoice into a separate account the day it lands. Not at the end of the month. The day it lands.

Myths that cost people money

"I will start low and raise it once I have reviews." Rates are far harder to raise than to set. The client who hired you at £150 a day anchored on £150 and will resist £400 forever. Start where you intend to be and discount deliberately if you must, so the discount is visible and temporary.

"Charging more means I will get no work." The volume of enquiries falls. The conversion rate on serious enquiries often rises, because a very low price signals inexperience to the buyers with real budgets. What actually happens is fewer, better clients.

"My rate should match what other freelancers charge." Other freelancers have different costs, different tax positions and different billable ratios. Their number tells you about the market ceiling. It tells you nothing about your floor.

The honest bottom line

If you take one thing from this, take the divisor. Two hundred working days, not 260. Around 135 billable days, not 200. Everything else is refinement.

And a plain warning: freelancing is not automatically better paid than employment. At £340 a day for 136 days, Priya grosses £46,280 and takes home about £32,000, which is roughly a £38,000 salaried job once you account for holiday, sick pay and pension. Freelancing pays properly at £450 and up. Below about £300 a day, for most people in most cities, a salary is genuinely the better deal. That is not a popular thing to say and it is still true.

Frequently asked

Should I put my rates on my website? A starting from figure filters out the wrong enquiries and saves you hours. Exact prices for complex work usually cost you room to negotiate. A range or a from price is the middle ground.

What if a client asks for my rate before I know the scope? Give a range and a condition. "Projects like this usually land between £2,500 and £4,500 depending on how many pages and how much research." That answers them honestly without pricing blind.

Do I need insurance? Professional indemnity is often required in client contracts and typically costs £100 to £250 a year for a solo freelancer. Budget for it before you set your rate, not after.

Is it worth going limited instead of sole trader? It can be, usually somewhere above roughly £40,000 to £50,000 of profit, but it depends on your circumstances, your dividend position and whether IR35 applies to your contracts.
