Working From Home Tax Relief Ended on 6 April 2026. You Can Still Claim Up to Four Years of It, and Here Is What Each Year Pays

For fifteen years employees who had to work from home could claim tax relief on a flat £6 a week without a receipt. Budget 2025 abolished it, and from the 2026 to 2027 tax year the claim no longer exists. What most of the coverage missed is that the previous four years are still open, and for someone who genuinely qualified and never claimed, that is real money with a deadline on it. Here is the rule as it now stands.

The short version

  • From 6 April 2026 you cannot claim tax relief for working from home. Budget 2025 inserted section 360B into ITEPA 2003 to disallow the deduction for unreimbursed household costs. Source: GOV.UK, Claim tax relief for your job expenses, and HMRC's tax information and impact note, read 4 September 2026.
  • You can still claim for the four previous tax years, 2022/23 to 2025/26, but only if you had to work from home: your employer had no office, or your job required you to live far from it. Choosing to work from home, including under a hybrid contract, never qualified.
  • Each year is worth £62.40 at basic rate, £124.80 at higher rate and £140.40 at additional rate on the flat £6 a week. Claims for 2022/23 onward need evidence that you had to work from home.
  • The 2022/23 year must be claimed by 5 April 2027. Each later year has a year longer. Claims go through HMRC's online service, or through Self Assessment if you file one.
  • HMRC said over half of the claims it checked were ineligible, which is the stated reason for abolition. About 300,000 people lose the relief, at £62 or £124 a year each.
  • What survives: an employer can still pay you £6 a week, or £26 a month, tax and National Insurance free towards home working costs. That is now the only route, and it needs the employer to agree to pay it.

1. What ended, and why

Employees who were required to work from home could, under section 336 of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003, deduct the additional household costs of doing so. Because working out the actual cost of the extra heating, electricity and business calls was impractical, HMRC allowed a flat rate instead: £4 a week from 2011 to 2012, rising to £6 a week from 2020 to 2021, with no receipts required. During the pandemic years 2020 to 2021 and 2021 to 2022 the test was widened so that anyone told to work from home because of Covid could claim, and millions did.

Budget 2025, on 26 November 2025, announced the end of the relief. HMRC's tax information and impact note says the measure inserts a new section 360B into the Act to disallow the deduction for household expenses that an employer does not reimburse, with effect from 6 April 2026. The stated reason is compliance: HMRC says that after checking claims, over half were found to be ineligible. The note estimates 300,000 people are affected, losing £62 a year at basic rate or £124 at higher rate.

GOV.UK's guidance page now says it in one line: from the tax year 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, you will not be able to claim tax relief for working from home. It adds the sentence this article is about: you can still claim for the four previous tax years.

2. What is still claimable, and who qualified

The four open years are 2022 to 2023, 2023 to 2024, 2024 to 2025 and 2025 to 2026. For each of them the old test applies in full, and it was always narrower than most people believed.

GOV.UK's wording: you can claim if you have to work from home, for example because your job requires you to live far away from your office, or your employer does not have an office. You cannot claim if you choose to work from home, and that includes where your employment contract lets you work from home some or all of the time, or where your employer has an office but you do not go in. A hybrid arrangement, however permanent, is a choice in HMRC's eyes. The pandemic easement ended with the 2021 to 2022 year and does not help with any of the four open years.

So the people with a live claim are a specific group: fully remote employees of companies with no UK office, field-based staff whose contract requires them to work from a home base, and employees whose role was made home-based by the employer, in writing, rather than by preference. If that is you and you never claimed, the next section is your money. If it is not, the honest answer is that no claim was ever available, and the abolition changes nothing for you.

3. What a year is worth

The flat rate is £6 a week, £312 a year, and the relief is at your marginal rate of tax. GOV.UK's own example: a basic rate taxpayer claiming £6 a week gets £1.20 a week in relief, which is £62.40 a year on our arithmetic. A higher rate taxpayer gets £2.40 a week, £124.80 a year. An additional rate taxpayer gets £2.70 a week, £140.40 a year. Scottish rates differ slightly because Scottish bands do.

Four years at basic rate is £249.60. Four years at higher rate is £499.20. Those are the maximum sums at stake for a full-year, full-week claim, and they are what a qualifying employee who never claimed can still recover.

You could instead claim the exact additional costs, with evidence, if they were higher than £6 a week. In practice almost nobody does, because the extra cost of heating one room for a working day is hard to evidence and rarely much above the flat rate. Broadband and rent do not count, because they are used privately as well.

4. A worked example in Cardiff

Owen works for a software company that closed its Cardiff office in the summer of 2022 and has had no UK premises since. His contract was varied that August to make his home his place of work. He earns £34,000, so he is a basic rate taxpayer, and he has never claimed anything because he assumed the relief was a pandemic scheme that had ended.

He qualifies for all four open years, because the employer had no office and the arrangement was the employer's, in writing. On our arithmetic each year is worth £62.40, so £249.60 in total. He claims through HMRC's online service in September 2026 with the contract variation as evidence. The three earlier years are paid as a refund; the 2025 to 2026 year has already ended, so that is a refund too. From 6 April 2026 there is nothing more to claim, and his tax code for 2026 to 2027 should not carry any homeworking allowance. If it does, because an old claim was rolled forward, it will be wrong and he should have it removed rather than underpay tax for a year.

His colleague Bethan in the same company was promoted to a higher rate salary in 2024. Her claim is £62.40 for each of the first two years and £124.80 for each of the last two: £374.40. Same employer, same evidence, different rate.

Their friend Rhys works for a bank with a Cardiff office and a hybrid policy of three days at home. He has read the same articles and wants to claim. He cannot, for any of the four years. The contract lets him work from home; it does not require it. That was always the rule and the abolition has not changed it.

5. How to claim, and the evidence HMRC wants

Use HMRC's online service on GOV.UK, which first checks whether you can claim and then takes the claim. If you complete a Self Assessment tax return, you claim on the return instead. For any claim for 2022 to 2023 or later you must send evidence that you had to work from home; a contract, a letter from the employer, or a written statement that there is no office are the usual forms. For a claim on exact costs rather than the flat rate you also need the bills.

The deadline is four years from the end of the tax year. That puts 2022 to 2023 at 5 April 2027, 2023 to 2024 at 5 April 2028, and so on. HMRC pays refunds for closed years directly; there is no longer a tax code route because there is no current year to adjust.

Two cautions. First, do not claim for a year in which you chose to work from home on the basis that everyone was doing it; that is the population HMRC says made up more than half of the claims it checked, and it is now checking. Second, if you are a company director working from your own company's registered office at home, the rules are different and better handled through the company, which is outside this article.

6. The employer payment that survives

The abolition removes the employee's deduction. It does not remove the employer's ability to pay for home working. HMRC's expenses and benefits guidance still allows an employer to pay an employee £6 a week, or £26 a month for monthly paid staff, towards additional household costs without any tax or National Insurance, and without evidence. Above that, the employer needs to show the payment is no more than the actual extra cost.

The impact note says so explicitly: the measure does not affect employers who reimburse home working costs. So for a fully remote employee the £312 a year has not vanished; it has moved from HMRC's side of the ledger to the employer's. Whether the employer pays it is a question for the employer, and for anyone negotiating a remote role or a pay review from April 2026 it is a specific, small, tax-free item worth asking for. It costs the employer £312 a year per person and is worth £312 in the employee's pocket, which is a better rate than the same money paid as salary.

7. The honest verdict

The £6 a week relief is gone from 6 April 2026, and it was never available to anyone who chose to work from home. Hybrid workers lose nothing because they had nothing.

If your employer had no office or required you to work from home, and you never claimed, four years are still open: £62.40 a year at basic rate, £124.80 at higher, with evidence, and the 2022/23 year closes on 5 April 2027.

From now on the only route is the employer paying £6 a week tax free. Ask for it in writing. It is the same money and the same rule, just a different payer.

Frequently asked

Can I still claim working from home tax relief? Not for 2026/27 or any later year. You can still claim for 2022/23 to 2025/26 if your employer required you to work from home in those years. Choosing to, including under a hybrid contract, does not qualify.

How much is a year's claim worth? On the flat £6 a week: £62.40 at basic rate, £124.80 at higher rate, £140.40 at additional rate. Four years at basic rate is £249.60.

What is the deadline? Four years from the end of the tax year. 2022/23 must be claimed by 5 April 2027.

Can my employer still pay me for working from home? Yes. Up to £6 a week or £26 a month can be paid free of tax and National Insurance without evidence. Budget 2025 did not change that.
