£17.18 an Hour on Paper. Here Is What Food Delivery Pays Once the Car Is Paid For

Deliveroo publishes a pay floor of £17.18 an hour for car drivers, and it is a real, honest number. It is also a number that describes about half of the time you are actually working. This is the same job costed properly, including the insurance nobody mentions and a tax rule that quietly punishes the cheapest vehicle on the road.

The short version

  • The pay floor is real but narrow. From 1 April 2026, Deliveroo and the GMB agreed minimum fees per hour of estimated order time: £17.18 by car, £14.40 by scooter, £12.99 by eBike and £12.88 by bike. It runs from accepting an order to marking it delivered. Time spent waiting for the next offer is not in it.

1. What the pay floor actually guarantees

Deliveroo is unusually transparent about this, so it is worth quoting precisely. Under its agreement with the GMB union, for 2026/27 the agreed minimum fees per hour, inclusive of vehicle costs, are:

  • Car: £17.18
  • Scooter: £14.40
  • eBike: £12.99
  • Bike: £12.88

These took effect on 1 April 2026. Deliveroo's own worked example is that a car order estimated at 20 minutes pays at least £5.73.

Now the qualifier that changes everything. The floor applies to the time Deliveroo estimates it will take you to complete an order, from the moment you accept it to the moment you mark it delivered. It is a floor on engaged time, not on session time. If you log in at 5pm and your first offer arrives at 5:22pm, those 22 minutes are outside the guarantee entirely.

This is not a scandal and Deliveroo does not hide it. But almost every "how much do delivery drivers earn" article treats £17.18 as an hourly wage, and it is not one. The number that matters to you is fees earned divided by hours you were unavailable to do anything else.

Uber Eats and Just Eat work differently again. Neither publishes a comparable UK engaged-time floor for couriers, and both price each job on estimated distance and time. Just Eat does directly employ some couriers on hourly contracts in certain areas, but the majority of its UK couriers are self-employed on per-order Transit Pay. Deliveroo is used throughout this guide precisely because its published numbers can be checked.

2. The three costs that are never in the advert

Fuel. In the week commencing 17 August 2026, average UK unleaded was 161.12p a litre on the government's weekly road fuel price series. Urban delivery driving is short-hop, cold-engine, stop-start work, so 32mpg is a fair real-world assumption for a small petrol car. That works out at roughly 22.9p of fuel per mile.

Hire and reward insurance. This is the big one, and it is the one that catches people out. The moment you carry goods for payment, an ordinary social, domestic and pleasure motor policy does not cover the use you are making of the vehicle. Driving uninsured for the use in question is an offence under the Road Traffic Act 1988, and a claim during a delivery on the wrong policy can be declined. Market guides in 2026 put car hire and reward cover at roughly £800 to £1,800 a year depending on age, postcode and history, with under-25s paying substantially more. Pay-as-you-go top-up cover exists and is charged by the hour, which suits genuinely occasional work.

Spread across a 10 to 12 hour week, a £1,200 policy is about £25 a week before you have delivered anything. That is a fixed cost, which means the fewer hours you work, the worse your hourly rate gets.

Everything else. Tyres, brakes, servicing, an extra phone charger, mobile data, parking. Small individually, real in aggregate. We use £8 a week below, which is conservative.

3. Worked example: Tomasz in Luton, 12 hours a week

Tomasz has a full-time job and wants an evening top-up. He has a 2016 petrol hatchback, pays £1,200 a year for hire and reward cover and works 12 hours a week. We run him twice, because the only variable that really moves the answer is how much of that time he spends on an order.

Scenario A, disciplined peak only. Friday and Saturday 6pm to 10pm plus Sunday evening. Demand is high, so 80% of his logged-in time is engaged, and he covers about 130 miles.

  • Engaged time 9.6 hours × £17.18 = £164.93
  • Fuel: 130 miles × 22.9p = −£29.76
  • Insurance: £1,200 ÷ 48 weeks = −£25.00
  • Running costs = −£8.00
  • Net £102.17 for 12 hours, which is £8.51 an hour

Scenario B, weekday afternoons. Same 12 hours, spread across quieter periods. Utilisation drops to 50% and he covers 95 miles.

  • Engaged time 6.0 hours × £17.18 = £103.08
  • Fuel −£21.74, insurance −£25.00, running costs −£8.00
  • Net £48.34 for 12 hours, which is £4.03 an hour

Same person, same car, same platform, same number of hours. Choosing the wrong three hours of the week costs him more than half his money. Tips are on top of both figures and riders keep 100% of them, but tips are not a plan.

For comparison, we ran his neighbour on a pedal bike at 70% utilisation: 8.4 engaged hours × £12.88 = £108.19, minus about £6 a week for tyres, lights and maintenance, giving £8.52 an hour. The bike matched the car almost to the penny, with no fuel bill, no insurance premium and no uninsured-driving exposure. That result surprised us, and it holds because the car's higher fee is almost exactly consumed by the cost of being a car.

Both figures sit below the £12.71 National Living Wage that applies from 1 April 2026. The scenarios are ours, built on Deliveroo's published floor and public cost data. Your utilisation is the number to test, and you can measure it in a week.

4. The mileage rule that punishes the cheap vehicle

This is the part almost nobody covers, and it changed five months ago.

HMRC's simplified expenses flat rates for the 2026/27 tax year are:

  • Cars and goods vehicles, first 10,000 business miles: 55p, up from 45p before 6 April 2026
  • Cars and goods vehicles thereafter: 25p, unchanged
  • Motorcycles: 24p, unchanged
  • Bicycles: not on the list, so no flat rate exists

Take Tomasz's peak-only year: 130 miles a week over 48 weeks is 6,240 business miles. As a car he can deduct £3,432. Riding the identical routes on a moped he could deduct £1,497.60. That is £1,934 less relief for the same journeys, worth roughly £503 a year in extra tax to a basic rate payer once 20% income tax and 6% Class 4 National Insurance are counted. On a pedal bike he must track actual costs instead, which for most riders comes to very little.

Two warnings, because this cuts both ways. First, mileage relief reduces taxable profit, it is not cash back. If your total income sits under the £12,570 personal allowance, it is worth nothing to you. Second, if you use the flat rate you cannot also claim your insurance, fuel, servicing or repairs, because the flat rate is designed to cover them. Once you use flat rates for a vehicle you must keep using them for that vehicle.

So which is better? At 22.9p a mile of fuel, a £1,200 insurance premium and an allowance of 10p a mile for servicing and tyres, claiming actual costs beats the 55p flat rate below about 5,400 business miles a year. Above that, the flat rate wins. That crossover is our calculation and it moves with your own premium, so run it on your figures before you file.

On the reporting side, nothing new is being taxed but a lot more is being seen. Delivery platforms are inside HMRC's digital platform reporting rules, with reports covering the 2025 calendar year onwards. The £1,000 trading allowance still applies and is shared across all your self-employed and online selling income together, not per platform. Go over it in 2026/27 and you must register for Self Assessment by 5 October 2027. We cover this properly in our side hustle tax rules guide.

5. Who this genuinely works for

Being straight about it: as a way to reliably beat minimum wage, food delivery by car does not do it in our figures. But there are three situations where it still makes sense.

You already own a bike and live in a dense city centre. No fuel, no premium, short hops, high utilisation. This is the version that works, and it is the version the adverts show least.

You need money this week and cannot wait for a payroll cycle. Delivery pays out fast and starts fast. That liquidity has genuine value that an hourly comparison does not capture.

You want strictly bounded hours around something else. Three evenings and nothing else, no notice, no rota. Few things flex like that.

If none of those describe you, tutoring, proofreading and skilled freelance work all clear the bar more comfortably. See our ranking of under-£100 side hustles against minimum wage.

The honest verdict

The published pay floor is accurate and Deliveroo deserves credit for putting it in writing. It is just measuring something narrower than the thing you care about. Engaged time is not session time, and the gap between them is where your hourly rate goes.

By car, at peak, in our example, this is roughly £8.50 an hour before tax. Off peak it is closer to £4. Neither reaches the £12.71 minimum wage, and no amount of app strategy closes a gap that large. If you have a bike and a city centre, the picture changes completely and the maths works. If you are buying a car, or a hire and reward policy, specifically in order to do this, the numbers say do not.

Before you commit, do one thing: work a single 4 hour peak session, write down your fees and your logged-in minutes, and divide. Everything above is our arithmetic. That number is yours.

Frequently asked

Do I really need special insurance for occasional delivery? Yes. The obligation attaches to the use, not the frequency. One paid delivery on a social, domestic and pleasure policy is one delivery driven without cover for that use. Pay-as-you-go hire and reward exists precisely for irregular work, charged by the hour, and is far cheaper than an annual policy if you work rarely.

Is the £17.18 a guaranteed hourly wage? No. It is a floor on the fee for the time Deliveroo estimates an order will take, from acceptance to delivery. Waiting between orders sits outside it, and fees are only paid for completed orders.

Am I an employee, or a worker? Neither, in the Deliveroo case. In November 2023 the Supreme Court held that Deliveroo riders were not in an employment relationship for collective bargaining purposes, largely because of an unrestricted right to send a substitute. That is a different outcome from the 2021 Uber ruling on private hire drivers, who were found to be workers. Employment status here is fact-specific and still developing, so do not assume one platform's outcome applies to another.

Do I have to tell HMRC? If your gross self-employed income across everything exceeds the £1,000 trading allowance in a tax year, yes. Platforms now report earnings data to HMRC under the digital platform reporting rules, so assume it is visible.
