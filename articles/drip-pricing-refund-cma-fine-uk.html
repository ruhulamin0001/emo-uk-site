The AA hid a three pound fee and it cost them nearly five million

The regulator now has the power to fine for hidden fees and to order your money back, and it has started using both. Three more household names went under investigation last month.

Ruhul Amin Published 12 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • £4.2 million fine and over £760,000 refunded. The CMA's first consumer law penalty, against the AA, announced 15 April 2026.
  • The hidden charge was £3. A mandatory booking fee kept out of the price shown to more than 80,000 learner drivers between April and December 2025.
  • The average payout was about £9 and customers had to do nothing.
  • Three new investigations opened on 19 August 2026: Trainline, Virgin Atlantic and RED Driving School. No conclusions reached.
  • The rule is simple: if a fee is mandatory it must be in the price from the start, not added at checkout.

1. What the AA case actually decided

On 15 April 2026 the Competition and Markets Authority ordered Automobile Association Developments Limited, owner of AA Driving School and BSM Driving School, to refund more than 80,000 customers and pay a £4.2 million fine. Refunds came to over £760,000, a total of almost £5 million.

The conduct was narrow and very ordinary. People booking lessons online between April and December 2025 were shown prices that did not include a mandatory £3 booking fee. For new customers the full price appeared only at checkout, after lessons had been selected, times chosen and personal details entered. For returning customers the fee sat separately from the initial price and was only folded into the total on the following page.

Three numbers matter. The fee was £3. The average refund was around £9, because most people bought more than one package. The fine was £4.2 million, after a 40 per cent reduction for admitting the breach and settling early. The penalty is not proportionate to what one customer lost. It is proportionate to doing it 80,000 times.

Sarah Cardell, the CMA's Chief Executive, put the rule in one line: if a fee is mandatory, it must be in the price from the very start, not added at checkout. This was the first penalty the CMA has imposed for a consumer law breach using its direct enforcement powers, and the first time it has used them to get money back.

2. The three firms now under investigation

On 19 August 2026 the CMA opened three further consumer protection investigations. All three had already been sent advisory letters during the CMA's first enforcement drive, which began in November 2025, and the CMA says concerns remained after ongoing monitoring.

  • Trainline. Whether all mandatory fees are in the upfront prices for advance train and coach tickets. The CMA says it observed fees from £0.59 to £2.79 on train travel, and a £1.50 coach booking fee.
  • Virgin Atlantic. Whether mandatory resort fees and local taxes are in the upfront prices for package holidays. The CMA notes these vary but can run to hundreds of pounds.
  • RED Driving School. How a mandatory booking fee and a "digital" fee, described as £7 or more per booking, have been displayed.

Say this plainly, because a lot of coverage did not: the CMA is at the beginning of these investigations and has reached no conclusions. Nobody is owed a refund from any of them today. What is worth knowing is the size of the stick. If the CMA finds an infringement it can order compensation and fine a company up to 10 per cent of global annual turnover.

3. What counts, and what to do about it

The test is whether the charge is mandatory. If you cannot complete the purchase without paying it, it belongs in the headline price. A booking fee everyone pays is caught, as is a compulsory resort fee or an unavoidable local tax. A seat you chose to reserve, insurance you opted into or delivery you upgraded is not. The grey area is anything presented as optional but effectively unavoidable, which is what the CMA's monitoring is looking at.

Yusuf, Bradford. He books ten lessons at an advertised £38, so £380 in his head. At checkout a £3 fee appears. He books a second block, then a third for his sister, paying the fee each time. Nine pounds on £1,140 of lessons. Under one per cent, never worth an afternoon to chase, and that is precisely why the practice works. It is only a £760,000 problem once you add up everyone's nine pounds. The regulator can do that arithmetic. A customer never can.

So, practically:

  1. Wait to be contacted. Where the CMA orders redress the firm writes to affected customers. In the AA case customers did nothing, and refunds went to the card used or by cheque.
  2. Screenshot both prices if you spot a mandatory fee appearing late. Without the listing price and the checkout total, there is nothing to show anyone.
  3. Report it to the Citizens Advice consumer service, which passes reports to Trading Standards.
  4. Do not expect much per head. The honest number from the only completed case is about £9.

The verdict

For most households this changes behaviour more than bank balances. Nine pounds is not a windfall. But a first fine of £4.2 million, and three more names under investigation four months later, says the practice is now expensive enough that big brands will quietly stop. That is worth more than the refund.

The habit worth forming: never compare on the price in a search result. Take two tabs to checkout and compare the totals. Until the enforcement work is finished, the headline price is an opinion and the checkout total is the fact.

Frequently asked

I bought AA or BSM driving lessons in 2025. Do I need to claim? No. The CMA said affected customers do not need to take any action. The driving school writes to each customer and refunds the card used, or sends a cheque if that is not possible.

Is drip pricing actually illegal, or just frowned on? It is unlawful where a mandatory fee is kept out of the price shown upfront. That is the basis on which the CMA fined the AA and ordered refunds in April 2026.

How much can the CMA fine a company? Up to 10 per cent of global annual turnover where it finds an infringement, alongside orders to pay compensation to affected customers.
