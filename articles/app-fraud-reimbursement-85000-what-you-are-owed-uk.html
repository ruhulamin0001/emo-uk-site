If a Scammer Talked You Into a Bank Transfer, the Refund Is a Right and Not a Favour

Mandatory reimbursement has applied to UK bank transfers since 7 October 2024, with a ceiling of GBP 85,000 and a normal deadline of five business days. The parts that decide most cases are the 13 month time limit, an excess that is entirely optional, and a definition of fraud that excludes something people often assume is covered.

The short version

  • Reimbursement is mandatory, not discretionary, for authorised push payment fraud on payments made on or after 7 October 2024.
  • The ceiling is GBP 85,000 per claim, which the regulator says covers over 99% of claims. Firms may choose to pay more, and above that you can go to the Financial Ombudsman Service, whose limit is GBP 430,000.
  • You should normally be paid within 5 business days. A firm can stop the clock to gather information but must reach an outcome within 35 business days.
  • The GBP 100 excess is optional. Each firm decides whether to apply it, to set a lower figure, or not to use one at all. It cannot be applied to vulnerable customers.
  • You have 13 months from the payment to report it. Miss that and the mandatory scheme is no longer the route.
  • It covers individuals, microenterprises and charities, on Faster Payments and CHAPS transfers between UK accounts.
  • Paying a real business that then fails to deliver is not this. That is a civil dispute with its own rights under the Consumer Rights Act, and a reimbursement claim will be refused.

1. What counts as APP fraud, and what does not

Authorised push payment fraud is when you are tricked into sending money to a criminal by bank transfer. You made the payment yourself, which is why it took so long for anyone to accept that you should get it back. It covers impersonation scams, romance scams, fake invoices, bogus investment offers and the rest.

The rules apply to UK bank transfers, meaning money moved between UK accounts over Faster Payments or CHAPS. Card, cash and cheque payments have separate protections. The scheme covers individuals, microenterprises and charities, and takes in high street banks, building societies, smaller payment firms and e-money firms. Credit unions, municipal banks and national savings banks are not in it.

Now the exclusion that catches people out. If you paid a legitimate business for goods or services and they never arrived, or arrived broken, that is a civil dispute, not fraud. There was no intent to deceive you, so the reimbursement rules do not apply and a claim will be turned down. Your rights there come from the Consumer Rights Act instead, and from the payment method you used. It is a real route, just a completely different one, and being sent down the wrong one wastes weeks.

APP fraud is also different from unauthorised fraud, where someone uses your card or account without your knowledge. That has its own, older protections.

2. What you are owed, and how fast

The maximum claim under the mandatory scheme is GBP 85,000. The regulator says that covers over 99% of claims, which is worth knowing, because it means the ceiling is not the live issue for almost anyone. Individual firms may choose to reimburse more than GBP 85,000 if they want to.

Lose more than GBP 85,000 and not get it back, and you can take the case to the Financial Ombudsman Service, whose compensation limit is GBP 430,000. That service is free and looks at each case on its own merits.

On timing, you should expect to be reimbursed within 5 business days of making your claim. Firms are allowed to stop the clock where they genuinely need more information, for example to assess whether you were vulnerable to the scam, but they must reach an outcome within 35 business days. Those two numbers are the ones to quote if you are being strung along.

The limit on you is 13 months. Report the fraud to your bank as soon as you can, and in any case within 13 months of making the payment.

Two numbers do most of the work in a difficult phone call: five business days for a decision, and thirty-five at the outside. Neither is a guideline.

3. The optional excess, and the vulnerability rule

Most coverage reports a GBP 100 excess as though it were fixed and universal. It is neither.

The excess is optional. A firm may apply the full GBP 100, may choose a lower figure up to that maximum, or may choose not to apply one at all. So whether you lose that GBP 100 depends entirely on which bank you were with when it happened. That is a genuine difference between providers that nobody advertises, and it is a fair question to ask before you open an account.

More importantly: the excess cannot be applied to vulnerable consumers. If you were vulnerable to the scam and your bank has deducted GBP 100, that is worth challenging rather than accepting.

4. The only two grounds for refusing you

A firm can decline to reimburse on two bases. The first is that you were complicit in the fraud. The second is gross negligence.

Gross negligence is the one banks reach for, and the regulator is explicit that it is a high bar. It is not the same as having been careless, or having ignored a warning, or feeling foolish afterwards. Nearly every victim of a well-built scam did something they would not do again. That is not gross negligence.

And the exception does not apply to vulnerable consumers at all. If you are vulnerable, gross negligence cannot be used against you.

If your bank refuses on this ground, ask it to put the reason in writing, then take it to the Financial Ombudsman Service. Free, independent, and it decides on the individual facts rather than the bank policy.

Worked example: Eileen Marsh in Sunderland

Eileen, 71, gets a call from someone claiming to be her bank fraud team. Over forty minutes she is talked into moving GBP 9,400 to a so-called safe account. It is a criminal account. She reports it to her real bank the next morning.

The straightforward case. Her bank applies the full excess. She is reimbursed GBP 9,400 less GBP 100, so GBP 9,300, and it should reach her within 5 business days of the claim.

If her bank does not use an excess. Some do not. She gets the full GBP 9,400. Identical scam, identical loss, GBP 100 difference, decided purely by which bank she happens to use.

If she is treated as vulnerable. At 71, living alone, targeted by a sustained impersonation call, there is a serious argument she was vulnerable to this scam. If so the excess cannot be applied, so she gets GBP 9,400, and gross negligence cannot be held against her either.

If the bank says gross negligence. It must say so, and she can take it to the Ombudsman for nothing. The bar is high and being persuaded by a convincing impersonator over forty minutes is close to the definition of the harm the rules exist to cover.

What would not be covered. If instead she had paid a real, trading sofa shop GBP 9,400 and the sofa never came, this scheme would not apply at all. That is a civil dispute, and the route runs through the Consumer Rights Act and her payment method.

5. The honest verdict

This is one of the strongest consumer protections in UK financial services and a lot of people who are entitled to it do not know it exists. If you were tricked into a bank transfer on or after 7 October 2024, reimbursement is the default and refusal is the exception the bank has to justify.

Three things actually decide cases. Report inside 13 months. Know that the decision is owed in 5 business days and 35 at the outside. And do not accept a gross negligence refusal at face value, because the bar is high and it cannot be used against a vulnerable customer at all.

Where this does not help you is the case people most often bring to it: money paid to a real business that let you down. That is not fraud and no amount of arguing will make the scheme fit. Say so plainly and go down the Consumer Rights Act route instead, which is where your actual rights are.

One last thing worth doing while nothing has gone wrong: find out whether your own bank applies the excess. It is a GBP 100 question with a one sentence answer, and it is the kind of detail that only matters on the worst day of your year.

Frequently asked

Do I have to report it to the police? You should report the details to the police, or consent to your bank reporting on your behalf. Action Fraud is the national reporting centre.

What if my bank misses the five days? It can stop the clock where it needs more information, but it must reach an outcome within 35 business days. If it does neither, that is a complaint, and then an Ombudsman case.

Does it cover payments to my own account abroad? The rules cover transfers between UK accounts over Faster Payments and CHAPS. Other payment types and routes have different protections, so ask your bank which applies.

Is my credit union covered? No. Credit unions, municipal banks and national savings banks are outside these arrangements.
