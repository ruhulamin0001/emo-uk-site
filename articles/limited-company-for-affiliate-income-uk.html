The Dividend Rise Quietly Killed the Case for Putting Affiliate Income in a Company

Almost every guide that tells you to incorporate once you pass thirty or forty thousand pounds was written when dividends were taxed at 8.75%. They are not any more. On 6 April 2026 the basic rate went to 10.75% and the higher rate to 35.75%, and that is enough to turn the answer upside down at the income levels most affiliate sites actually reach.

The short version

  • Dividend tax changed on 6 April 2026. The rates for 2026 to 2027 are 10.75% basic, 35.75% higher and 39.35% additional, on dividends above the £500 dividend allowance. GOV.UK publishes these on its tax on dividends page.
  • At £40,000 of profit, sole trader wins by £1,236 a year. Sole trader keeps £32,868. A company paying a £12,570 salary and the rest as dividends keeps £31,633. That is before you pay anyone to do the accounts.
  • At £80,000 of profit, sole trader wins by £1,947 a year. Sole trader keeps £57,711 against £55,765 through a company. The gap widens, it does not close.
  • The corporation tax side has not moved. 19% up to £50,000 of profit, 25% above £250,000, and an effective 26.5% on every pound in between through marginal relief.
  • Incorporating still makes sense for reasons that are not tax. Limited liability, selling the site as a share sale later, and a handful of advertisers and networks that will only contract with a company.
  • Running costs are real. Companies House charges £50 a year for a digital confirmation statement and £100 to incorporate digitally, both since 1 February 2026, and a company needs statutory accounts and a CT600 that a sole trader does not.

1. What actually changed on 6 April 2026

The structure people were sold for years went like this. Take a small salary out of the company, take the rest as dividends, and because dividends carried no National Insurance and were taxed at only 8.75% in the basic rate band, you came out ahead of a sole trader paying income tax and Class 4 National Insurance on the same profit.

That gap was never huge. It was usually a few hundred pounds at modest profits, and the argument leaned heavily on the idea that it would grow as you did. Then two things happened at once.

Dividend rates went up. For the 2026 to 2027 tax year GOV.UK now publishes them as 10.75% in the basic rate band, 35.75% in the higher rate band and 39.35% in the additional rate band, on anything above the £500 dividend allowance. That is two percentage points on top of the old basic and higher rates.

And Class 4 National Insurance, the thing dividends were supposed to help you avoid, is no longer the number it was either. For 2026 to 2027 a sole trader pays 6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270, and 2% above that. Class 2 is treated as paid rather than charged once profits reach £7,105, so it costs nothing.

Six percent of National Insurance is a smaller thing to dodge than it used to be. Ten and three quarter percent of dividend tax is a bigger price to pay for dodging it.

Put those two movements together and the arithmetic that used to favour a company at around thirty thousand pounds of profit does not favour it at eighty thousand. That is not an opinion, so here is the working.

2. The arithmetic at £40,000 of profit

Take an affiliate site making £40,000 of profit after hosting, tools and any content costs. One person, no employees.

As a sole trader. Personal allowance £12,570, so £27,430 is taxable at 20%, giving income tax of £5,486. Class 4 National Insurance is 6% of that same £27,430, giving £1,645.80. Total £7,131.80.
You keep £32,868.20.

Through a limited company. Pay yourself a £12,570 salary. Employer National Insurance is 15% on the amount above the £5,000 secondary threshold, so 15% of £7,570 is £1,135.50. That leaves £26,294.50 of company profit. Corporation tax at 19% is £4,995.96, leaving £21,298.54 to pay out as dividends. The first £500 is free, and the remaining £20,798.54 is taxed at 10.75%, giving £2,235.84.
You keep £31,632.70.

The company is £1,235.50 a year worse off, before any accountancy fee.

Two things in that working are worth pausing on, because they are where people go wrong. The £1,135.50 of employer National Insurance is real and it is often left out of comparisons. A one director company with no other employees cannot claim the £10,500 Employment Allowance that would otherwise wipe it out. And the corporation tax is charged on profit after the salary, not on the headline £40,000.

3. The arithmetic at £80,000 of profit

The usual response at this point is that the company wins once you get bigger. It does not. Here is the same comparison at £80,000 of profit.

As a sole trader. Income tax is 20% on £37,700 and 40% on £29,730, giving £19,432. Class 4 is 6% on £37,700 and 2% on £29,730, giving £2,856.60. Total £22,288.60.
You keep £57,711.40.

Through a limited company. Salary £12,570 again, employer National Insurance £1,135.50, leaving £66,294.50 of profit. That sits in the marginal relief band, so corporation tax is 25% of £66,294.50 less marginal relief of 3/200 of the gap up to £250,000, which works out at £13,818.05, an effective rate of 20.8%. That leaves £52,476.45 in dividends. After the £500 allowance, £37,200 is taxed at 10.75% and £14,776.45 at 35.75%, giving dividend tax of £9,281.58.
You keep £55,764.87.

The company is £1,946.53 a year worse off. The gap got wider, not narrower.

The reason is the 26.5% marginal rate. Every pound of company profit between £50,000 and £250,000 is taxed at an effective 26.5% before you have taken a penny of it out, and then the dividend on top attracts 10.75% or 35.75%. A sole trader in the higher rate band pays 40% plus 2%. Those two stacks are now close enough that the extra layer of corporation tax is not buying you anything.

Adjusting the salary moves these numbers by a few hundred pounds either way and is worth modelling against your own figures. It does not flip the answer at either level.

4. Worked example: the Rahman household in Stevenage

Nadia Rahman runs a home and garden review site alongside a part time job. The site made £41,200 of profit in the last twelve months and she has been told by three different YouTube videos that she is "leaving money on the table" by not incorporating.

She is not. On those profits, as a sole trader, she pays roughly £5,726 of income tax and £1,717.80 of Class 4, keeping about £33,756. Through a company on the salary and dividend structure above she would keep roughly £32,486.

The tax difference is about £1,270 against her.

Then add the running costs. A £50 confirmation statement each year. Statutory accounts and a CT600 that she cannot file from the same forty minutes she currently spends on her Self Assessment. An accountant to prepare them, which for a company this size is typically several hundred pounds a year more than the sole trader equivalent.

Realistic total cost of incorporating: somewhere between £1,700 and £2,300 a year. For a site making £41,200.

None of that means she should never incorporate. It means the reason will not be tax, and anyone selling her on the tax angle either has not run the 2026 to 2027 numbers or is selling company formations.

5. Three reasons to incorporate anyway

Limited liability, if you carry real risk. A company is a separate legal person, so a claim against the business does not automatically reach your house. For most affiliate publishers the realistic risk is low, but it is not zero if you employ freelancers, hold customer data, sign advertiser contracts with indemnities, or sell anything of your own alongside the affiliate links.

You intend to sell the site. Buyers of established sites often prefer to buy the shares in a company rather than the assets of a sole trade, and the seller's tax position on a share sale can be materially better. This only matters if a sale is a real plan with a real timeline, not a someday. If it is, incorporating well ahead of a sale is better than incorporating during one.

A counterparty insists. A small number of advertisers, agencies and networks will only contract with a limited company, and some require you to be VAT registered too. If the deal is worth more than the two thousand pounds a year the structure costs you, take the deal. That is a commercial decision, not a tax one.

One thing incorporating does not buy you is privacy. Your name, your role and a service address go on the public register at Companies House, and while you can now apply to suppress a residential address from most public documents for £34 per document, the default is visibility.

The honest verdict

If your affiliate income is anywhere between about twenty thousand and a hundred thousand pounds of profit a year, and you have no specific non tax reason, stay a sole trader. The dividend rise on 6 April 2026 removed the arithmetic that the standard advice rests on, and nobody has rewritten the standard advice yet.

If one of the three reasons in section five genuinely applies to you, incorporate with your eyes open and budget roughly two thousand pounds a year for the privilege, split between higher tax and higher compliance.

And if you already have a company built purely on the old tax argument, this is worth taking to an accountant rather than acting on alone. Closing a company properly has its own costs and its own tax consequences, and "the arithmetic reversed" is a reason to review, not a reason to rush.

Frequently asked

Do these numbers change if I have a job as well? Yes, and usually against the company. Employment income uses up your personal allowance and your basic rate band first, so more of your dividends fall into the 35.75% band while your sole trader profits would have been taxed at 40% plus 2%. The gap narrows a little, but the sole trader still tends to come out ahead at the profit levels above.

What about paying myself only in salary through the company? That is worse again in most cases, because the company pays 15% employer National Insurance and you pay employee National Insurance and income tax on the same money. The salary and dividend split exists precisely because full salary is expensive.

Does a company help me claim more expenses? No. The rules on what is allowable are very similar either way. The genuine differences are narrow, such as certain pension contributions and some equipment purchases, and they are not worth the structure on their own.

When does the answer flip? At high profits that you do not need to take out. A company is a place to leave money, not a way to take it out cheaply. If you can genuinely leave £50,000 a year inside the business, the deferred tax argument is real. If you need every pound to live on, it is not.
