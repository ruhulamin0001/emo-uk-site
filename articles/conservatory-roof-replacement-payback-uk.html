A Solid Conservatory Roof Pays Back In 239 Years

This is not an argument against replacing your conservatory roof. It is an argument against the reason you are being given for it. The energy saving is real, it is just far smaller than the sales pitch, and knowing why changes what you should ask for in the quote.

Ruhul Amin Published 11 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The roof swap works exactly as advertised. A 25mm polycarbonate roof on a 14 square metre conservatory loses 19.6 watts per degree. An insulated replacement loses 2.1. That is a 89 per cent cut to the roof.
  • It is still only a 17.5 per cent cut to the room. Total heat loss falls from 99.8 to 82.3 watts per degree, because the glazed walls were always the bigger problem.
  • After the roof, the walls are 81 per cent of the remaining heat loss. Nobody selling you a roof mentions that.
  • The saving is about £29 a year on gas, or £87 on a plug-in electric heater. Against a £7,000 job that is a payback of 239 years on gas or 81 years on electric.
  • And your bill will probably go up, not down. Most people do not heat a cold conservatory. After the roof you will use the room, and heating it properly costs £138 a year on gas or £410 on electric that you were not spending before.

1. The two U-values the whole thing turns on

Both numbers are published by the manufacturers themselves, which is the only reason this calculation is possible at all.

What you probably have. Ariel Plastics makes Corotherm, one of the most widely fitted conservatory glazing sheets in the country. Its 25mm 7Xwall product page states a U-value of 1.4 W/m²K. Interestingly, the relative U-value table further down the same page gives 1.6 for the same sheet. We have used 1.4 throughout, because it is the better of the two figures for the existing roof and therefore produces the smaller, more cautious saving. If the real figure is 1.6, every saving below is understated by about a seventh.

What you are being sold. Ultraframe states that its Ultraroof, the system formerly called the UltraRoof380, has a U-value as low as 0.15 W/m²K, with an option to go to 0.13. It weighs 38 kg per square metre, which is why it can sit on existing conservatory frames, and it carries a 10 year parts guarantee. Ultraframe is of course the manufacturer, so this is a performance claim by an interested party. It is a conventional warm roof construction and 0.15 is entirely plausible, but it is their number, not an independent one.

Everything below uses those two figures, plus the Ofgem price cap rates for 1 October to 31 December 2026: gas at 7.97p per kWh and electricity at 26.32p per kWh.

2. Where the heat actually goes, before and after

Take a common conservatory: 3.5 metres square, 12.25 square metres of floor, with a pitched roof of about 14 square metres and three glazed walls 2.4 metres high. That gives 25.2 square metres of glazed wall, a concrete slab floor with no insulation under it, and the usual draughty ventilation.

There it is. The roof job removes 17.5 watts per degree, a 17.5 per cent cut. That is a genuine improvement, and it is not what you were led to expect from a claim of "89 per cent less heat loss through the roof", which is also true.

The double glazing figure of 2.65 is Ariel Plastics' own comparison figure, fair for a standard sealed unit fitted ten or twenty years ago. If yours has modern low emissivity argon filled glass the walls come down to about 1.6, which makes the roof a larger share and improves the payback. It still does not rescue it.

3. The payback table, at four job prices

Assumptions, stated plainly: you heat the room to 20 degrees against an average outside temperature of 5 degrees, for 6 hours a day over a 210 day heating season. That is a generous assumption in your favour, because it credits the roof with a saving every single day from October to April.

Before, that costs 1.497 kW × 6 × 210 = 1,886 kWh of heat a year. After, 1.235 kW × 6 × 210 = 1,556 kWh. The saving is 330 kWh of heat a year.

On a gas boiler running at about 90 per cent efficiency, that is 367 kWh of gas at 7.97p, or £29.23 a year. On a plug-in electric heater it is 330 kWh at 26.32p, or £86.86 a year.

The roof carries a 10 year parts guarantee. The payback is between 58 and 411 years. Those two facts sit in the same brochure.

For comparison, the things on this site that genuinely do pay back through the fabric of a house are loft insulation, draught proofing and cavity wall insulation, all of which come in under ten years because they start from a much worse baseline and cost a fraction as much. Double glazing sits awkwardly in the middle for the same reason this does: it is expensive, and what it replaces is usually not quite bad enough.

4. The trap: a room you did not used to heat

This is the part that turns a small saving into a real increase, and it is the single most useful thing in this article.

Ask yourself honestly how much you heat your conservatory now. For most households with a polycarbonate roof the answer is: barely at all in deep winter, because it is hopeless and you know it. The door to the house stays shut and the room is a cold store from November to March.

After the roof goes on, the room becomes usable, which is the entire point. So you use it. And heating it to 20 degrees for six hours a day across the season costs:

  • £137.80 a year if it is on the gas central heating
  • £409.54 a year if you heat it with a plug-in electric heater or a portable oil filled radiator

That is new spending. The £29 or £87 "saving" is measured against a counterfactual where you heated the old conservatory to the same temperature, and almost nobody did. In cash terms, the likely outcome of replacing a conservatory roof is that your energy bill goes up, because you have gained a room.

That is not a scandal, it is what buying a room looks like. But it means the right comparison is £7,000 plus £138 a year against any other way of getting an extra usable room. Against a single storey extension at £30,000, the roof is excellent value. Against doing nothing, it is a cost.

One cheap thing to try first: if the room is unusable mainly through summer glare rather than winter cold, thermal blinds or a solar control film cost a fraction of this. Our comparison of thermal curtains against blinds covers the heat retention side.

5. Building Regulations, and the bit that is genuinely unclear

Here is what the law actually says, rather than what conservatory websites say it says.

Schedule 2, Class 7 of the Building Regulations 2010 exempts "the extension of a building by the addition at ground level of a conservatory, porch, covered yard or covered way" where "the floor area of that extension does not exceed 30m²", provided that a wholly or partly glazed conservatory or porch has glazing satisfying Part N of Schedule 1. That is the whole of the statutory exemption. It sets a floor area limit. It does not, in that text, set a percentage of the roof that must be glazed.

The familiar conditions you will read everywhere, that the roof must be at least 75 per cent translucent, that the walls must be at least 50 per cent glazed, that the conservatory must stay thermally separated from the house by external quality doors, and that fixed heating must not be extended into it, come from the Approved Document guidance rather than from Schedule 2 itself. They matter, because that guidance is what a building control body will apply in practice.

What follows is the honest position: fitting a solid roof can take a conservatory outside the guidance that kept it exempt, and building control may then treat it as an extension. Some systems, including Ultraframe's, are marketed with third party approval schemes precisely to smooth this. But whether your specific job needs a building notice depends on the size, the roof specification, whether the doors between house and conservatory are staying, and your local authority's reading of it.

Do not take an installer's word for it and do not take ours. Ring your council's building control team before you sign anything. It is a free phone call and it is the difference between a room and an unmortgageable room, because a missing completion certificate turns up in every conveyancing search for the rest of the house's life.

6. Worked example: the Hendersons in Ormskirk

The Hendersons have a 3.5 metre square lean-to conservatory with a 25mm polycarbonate roof, built in 2009. They use it from May to September and shut the door on it the rest of the year. They are quoted £7,400 for an insulated tiled replacement roof with two glass panels.

The salesman's line is that the room will "pay for itself in reduced heating bills". They do the sum.

Roof heat loss falls from 19.60 to 2.10 watts per degree, a saving of 17.50. Across the season that is 330 kWh of heat. Their conservatory has no radiator, so the honest comparison is the 2 kW oil filled radiator they occasionally drag in there, at 26.32p a unit: £86.86 a year.

£7,400 divided by £86.86 is 85 years. Mrs Henderson is 58.

They buy it anyway, and they are right to. What they are buying is a dining room they can use in February, in a house with nowhere to put eight people at Christmas. The alternative quote for a small rear extension was £34,000.

What the arithmetic changed was the specification. Knowing the glazed walls would be 81 per cent of the remaining heat loss, they spent £900 of their contingency upgrading the two worst wall units to low emissivity argon filled glazing at the same time, which does more for comfort than the last 0.02 of roof U-value ever would. And they rang building control first, got a building notice, and have the completion certificate in the file.

7. The honest verdict

Replace the roof if you want a room. Do not replace it to save money, because it will not. The payback at a typical £7,000 job is 239 years on gas and 81 years on an electric heater, and in most households the bill goes up afterwards because you start heating a space you used to ignore.

If you do go ahead, spend part of the budget on the walls. They are two thirds of the heat loss before the job and four fifths of it after. An installer who quotes only for the roof is quoting for the smaller half of your problem.

And phone building control yourself. Not the installer. Not us. The council.

If your actual complaint about the conservatory is that it is boiling in July rather than freezing in January, a solid roof is an expensive answer to that question and blinds or film are a cheap one. Try the cheap one first.

Frequently asked

Will a solid roof stop the conservatory overheating in summer? Yes, and this is the strongest genuine argument for one. A glazed or polycarbonate roof is a solar collector pointed at the sky, and an insulated roof stops that. If your complaint is July rather than January, the roof does fix it. So does external shading or a solar control film, for a great deal less.

Can my existing frames take the weight? Usually. Ultraframe publishes its Ultraroof weight at 38 kg per square metre and markets it as suitable for most existing conservatory frames without extra support. That is a manufacturer's claim about its own product, and it should still be checked on site against your actual frames and base, particularly on an older self-build conservatory.

Does it add value to the house? Possibly, and more reliably it removes a negative. A tired polycarbonate conservatory reads to buyers as a job to do. A warm, signed-off room reads as a room. We would not put a percentage on that because nobody honestly can, but "turns a liability into a feature" is fair.
