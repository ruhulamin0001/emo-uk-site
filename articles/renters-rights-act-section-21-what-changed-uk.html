Section 21 Is Gone, Your Fixed Term Is Gone, and Your Landlord Needs a Reason. What the Renters' Rights Act Changed on 1 May 2026, and What It Did Not

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 is the biggest change to private renting in England since 1988, and most of it switched on in one day. Existing tenancies changed automatically, new ones follow new rules, and a set of protections that tenants have asked about for years is now law. It also has limits that the headlines skip. Here is what changed, what did not, and where to get free help.

The short version

  • From 1 May 2026 most existing assured shorthold tenancies in England became assured periodic tenancies automatically, and every new private tenancy is one. Tenancies roll weekly or monthly and can no longer have an end date. Source: GOV.UK, Renters' Rights Act overview for tenants, updated 1 May 2026; commencement by SI 2026/421.
  • Section 21 no fault evictions ended on 1 May 2026, even if your agreement says otherwise. A landlord must now use a ground for possession, the correct form, and the correct notice, usually four months. Proceedings started before 1 May can in some cases be completed.
  • Rent can rise once a year, not in the first 12 months, on Form 4A with two months' notice, and you can challenge a rise above market rent at the tribunal. Rent in advance is capped at one month once you have signed, and no rent can be taken before you sign.
  • Landlords must include a price in any advert and cannot accept bids above it, cannot refuse you for being on benefits or having children, and must consider a request to keep a pet. You can end your tenancy with two months' notice in writing.
  • What it does not do: it does not cap rents, does not stop a landlord selling or moving in (after 12 months), does not apply to lodgers, council or housing association tenants or student halls, and does not apply in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.
  • Existing tenants should have received a government Information Sheet from their landlord or agent by 31 May 2026. If you did not, the landlord can be fined. Shelter and Citizens Advice give free help; nothing here is legal advice.

1. What happened to your tenancy on 1 May

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 27 October 2025, and the Renters' Rights Act 2025 (Commencement No. 2 and Transitional and Saving Provisions) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/421, brought the core of Part 1 into force on 1 May 2026. GOV.UK's guidance for tenants, published by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government and last updated on that date, describes the effect in one sentence: most existing assured shorthold tenancies automatically became assured periodic tenancies on 1 May 2026.

An assured periodic tenancy runs on a rolling basis, weekly or monthly depending on how rent is paid. It is no longer possible to have an assured tenancy agreement with an end date; if your agreement has one, it no longer applies. You did not need to sign a new agreement. Instead your landlord or letting agent had to give you a government-produced Information Sheet explaining the changes before 31 May 2026, and can be fined for not doing so. If you never had a written agreement, the landlord must now give you the key terms in writing.

Anyone who signed a new tenancy with a private landlord on or after 1 May 2026 is on an assured periodic tenancy from the start, with the same written information duty. The words fixed term, six months, twelve months and break clause have no legal effect in a private tenancy in England any more.

2. Eviction now: the grounds, the forms and the notice

The change most people know about is the end of section 21. From 1 May 2026 a tenant on an assured periodic tenancy can no longer be evicted using the no fault section 21 process, even where the tenancy agreement says the landlord can. GOV.UK adds one saving: where the court process was started before 1 May 2026, in some cases it can be completed after that date. That is the transitional provision in SI 2026/421, and it is the one area where a tenant served with a section 21 notice earlier in 2026 needs specific advice.

A landlord who wants possession now needs a ground. GOV.UK lists the main ones: the landlord needs to sell or move into the property, which cannot be used within the first 12 months of the tenancy; a student HMO ground for tenants who do not leave at the end of the academic year; rent not paid on time; and antisocial behaviour by the tenant, people living with them or visitors. The full list, with the notice period for each, is in the government's grounds for possession guidance. The landlord must use the correct form and give the correct notice, which GOV.UK says is usually four months but shorter for some grounds, such as serious rent arrears or antisocial behaviour.

A notice is not an eviction. If you do not leave by the end of the notice period, the landlord must apply to court for a possession order, and only a court, followed by bailiffs, can remove you. Leaving early because a notice arrived is a choice, not a requirement, and it is worth taking advice before making it.

3. Rent rises, rent in advance and bidding

Rent can now only be increased once a year, and not at all in the first 12 months of a new tenancy. The landlord must use the statutory Form 4A and give at least two months' notice. If you think the proposed rent is above the market rent for the property, you can challenge it at the First-tier Tribunal, and GOV.UK says so in terms. Rent review clauses in old agreements that allowed increases on any other basis no longer operate.

On the way in, two changes. A landlord cannot ask for, encourage or accept any rent before you have signed the tenancy agreement, and once you have signed, the most that can be asked for in advance is one month's rent. Deposits are separate and still capped under the Tenant Fees Act 2019 at five weeks' rent where the annual rent is under £50,000.

Rental bidding has also ended. A landlord or agent must state a specific price in any written advert or offer, and cannot accept or encourage offers above it. The tenant who offers £50 a month more to jump the queue can no longer be taken up on it, at least not lawfully.

4. Pets, discrimination and the information sheet

You can now ask to keep a pet. The landlord must consider the request and should give a reason if refusing; a blanket no pets clause is no longer the end of the conversation. The Act also makes it unlawful for a landlord to refuse to let to someone because they receive benefits or have children, alongside the existing protections against discrimination on the grounds in the Equality Act.

Ending a tenancy from the tenant's side is simpler than it was. Under an assured periodic tenancy you give two months' notice in writing, by letter, email or text, on the day rent is due or the day before, keep paying rent through the notice period, and leave. You and the landlord can agree a shorter period in writing.

Enforcement sits with councils, which have new powers to investigate and fine, and with the First-tier Tribunal, where for some offences, such as letting an unlicensed property, a tenant can apply for a Rent Repayment Order of up to two years' rent. Shelter and Citizens Advice, both free, are the places GOV.UK itself points tenants to.

5. A worked example in Nottingham

Aleksandra rents a two bedroom flat in Nottingham on a 12 month agreement signed in September 2025 at £895 a month, with a clause allowing a rent review after six months. On 1 May 2026 that became a monthly assured periodic tenancy with no end date. Her landlord's agent emailed the Information Sheet on 19 May, inside the 31 May deadline.

In June the agent proposed £995 a month from July. That is a rise inside the first 12 months of the tenancy, which is not allowed, and it was not on Form 4A with two months' notice. She replied citing the GOV.UK guidance and the agent withdrew it. In October, after the 12 months, the agent served Form 4A proposing £975 from December, two months' notice. Similar flats in her block were letting at around £920, so she applied to the tribunal, which can set the rent at the market level; the landlord settled at £930 before the hearing. On our arithmetic that is £540 a year less than the first proposal, and the rent cannot move again for 12 months.

Her neighbour Tomasz, on a tenancy that began in April 2026, was told in August that the landlord wanted to sell and would serve notice. The selling ground cannot be used within the first 12 months of the tenancy, so the earliest notice is April 2027, followed by a four month notice period, taking the earliest possession application to August 2027. Nothing in the Act stops the sale; it fixes the timetable.

6. What the Act does not do

It does not control rents. A landlord can still propose a market rent once a year; the tribunal tests it against the market, not against affordability, and in a rising market that means rises. It does not stop evictions; it requires a ground, and the selling and moving in grounds are broad ones, delayed by 12 months rather than removed. It does not write off arrears or change the rent arrears ground beyond its notice rules.

It applies to private tenancies in England only. GOV.UK says different rules apply if you rent from a housing association or a council, live in student halls, or are a lodger, which is why our lodger guide is unaffected. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own tenancy law; the Act does not reach them.

It does not undo a possession claim that was properly issued before 1 May 2026, in the cases the transitional regulations preserve. And it does not enforce itself: a landlord who ignores it can still serve an invalid notice, and the tenant's protection is knowing it is invalid and getting help. GOV.UK names Shelter and Citizens Advice for that, and this article is general information, not legal advice.

7. The honest verdict

From 1 May 2026 your tenancy rolls, your landlord needs a ground to evict, rent can rise once a year on Form 4A with a tribunal challenge, and no more than one month's rent can be taken in advance. Those are the four things to know.

It is not rent control and it is not eviction control. Selling and moving in remain grounds after 12 months, and the tribunal tests a rise against the market, not your income.

If you did not get the Information Sheet by 31 May, or a notice looks wrong, use Shelter or Citizens Advice before you act. Both are free, and a wrong notice is the tenant's best protection only if the tenant knows it is wrong.

Frequently asked

Has section 21 really been abolished? Yes, for assured periodic tenancies in England from 1 May 2026, under the Renters' Rights Act 2025 brought into force by SI 2026/421. A landlord now needs a ground for possession, the right form and the right notice. Court proceedings started before 1 May can in some cases be completed.

Can my landlord still put the rent up? Once a year, not in the first 12 months of a new tenancy, on Form 4A with at least two months' notice. You can challenge a rise above market rent at the First-tier Tribunal.

I never got the Information Sheet. What should I do? Ask the landlord or agent for it and keep the request in writing. They were required to provide it by 31 May 2026 and can be fined. Your tenancy still became periodic on 1 May whether or not you received it.

Does the Act apply to me as a lodger or a council tenant? No. GOV.UK says different rules apply to lodgers, council and housing association tenants and student halls. It also applies in England only.
