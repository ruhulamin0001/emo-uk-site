The Second Payslip Looks Wrong Because It Has No Personal Allowance In It

Taking a second job and finding that tax comes off every single pound of it feels like a mistake. Usually it is not. It is a tax code doing exactly what it is designed to do, and understanding it takes about five minutes and can be worth several hundred pounds if it turns out to be wrong.

The short version

  • BR means all your income from that job or pension is taxed at the basic rate. GOV.UK adds that it is usually used if you have more than one job or pension.
  • The reason is the Personal Allowance. It is normally given against one job, through a code like 1257L, which is the code GOV.UK says is currently used for most people with one job or pension.
  • D0 and D1 are the higher versions. D0 taxes all income from that job at the higher rate, D1 at the additional rate.
  • 0T is different and usually the one to query. It means your Personal Allowance has been used up, or you started a job and your employer did not have the details to give you a code.
  • Scotland and Wales have their own codes. SBR, SD0, SD1, SD2 and SD3 in Scotland, CBR, CD0 and CD1 in Wales.
  • BR is wrong when your total income does not use the whole allowance, for example two part time jobs that together come to less than the Personal Allowance. Then you are overpaying.

1. Why the second job gets taxed on every pound

The Personal Allowance is the slice of income you can have before paying Income Tax. It is given to you once, not once per employer, and your tax code is the mechanism that hands it to a particular payroll.

GOV.UK explains the numbers: they "tell your employer or pension provider how much tax-free income you get from them in that tax year", worked out from your Personal Allowance with deductions taken off and the final digit replaced by a letter. It gives 1257L as "the tax code currently used for most people who have one job or pension", built on the standard tax-free Personal Allowance of £12,570.

Once that allowance sits on job one, there is none left for job two. So job two gets a code that gives no allowance at all, and the commonest of those is BR.

Which is why the second payslip feels punitive and generally is not. Your employer has no way of knowing what the first job pays, so HMRC allocates the allowance to one payroll and taxes the other from the first pound. The alternative, giving the allowance twice, would produce a bill later rather than a deduction now.

2. What each code on the table means

These are quoted from the GOV.UK tax code table, which is the authoritative list.

CodeWhat GOV.UK says it means
BRAll your income from this job or pension is taxed at the basic rate, usually used if you have more than one job or pension
D0All your income from this job or pension is taxed at the higher rate
D1All your income from this job or pension is taxed at the additional rate
0TYour Personal Allowance has been used up, or you started a new job and your employer does not have the details they need to give you a tax code
1257LThe code currently used for most people with one job or pension, giving the standard Personal Allowance
NTYou are not paying any tax on this income
KYou have income you are not paying tax on which is more than your Personal Allowance

The important distinction for anyone with two jobs is between BR and 0T. BR is a deliberate allocation: HMRC knows you have another job and has put the allowance there. 0T is often a gap in information, and GOV.UK's own wording includes the case where "you've started a new job and your employer does not have the details they need to give you a tax code".

A 0T code on a new second job is the one to chase, because it can mean nobody has told HMRC what is happening rather than that a decision has been made.

D0 and D1 matter for anyone whose first job already uses up the basic rate band. If your main job takes you into higher rate territory, taxing the second at basic rate would under-collect, so D0 exists to tax the second job at the higher rate from the first pound.

3. The two situations where BR is genuinely wrong

BR is usually right. There are two clear cases where it is not, and both cost you money every month until they are fixed.

Your total income is below the Personal Allowance. Two part time jobs paying, say, £6,000 and £5,000 come to £11,000, which is under £12,570. You should be paying no Income Tax at all. With 1257L on one and BR on the other, you are paying basic rate tax on the whole of the second job for no reason. The allowance can be split between the two codes so that each job uses part of it.

Your main job has changed. If the job carrying your allowance is now the smaller of the two, or has ended, the allowance is sitting in the wrong place. The second job keeps taxing every pound while the first cannot use the allowance it has been given.

There is a third, less clear-cut case. If your income varies a lot between the two jobs across the year, the codes may be right in principle and still leave you over or under paid at the year end. That is what the end of year reconciliation exists for, and our guide to the HMRC P800 tax calculation letter covers what arrives when it happens.

What is not a reason to query it: the second payslip simply having more tax on it than you expected. That on its own is the code working correctly.

4. Scotland and Wales are different

This is routinely left out of explanations and it matters, because the rates behind the letters are not the same.

Scotland uses an S prefix. GOV.UK lists SBR as all income from that job taxed at the basic rate in Scotland, SD0 at the intermediate rate, SD1 at the higher rate, SD2 at the advanced rate and SD3 at the top rate. Note that SD0 is intermediate rather than higher, so the Scottish letters do not map onto the England, Wales and Northern Ireland ones by position.

Wales uses a C prefix. CBR is basic rate in Wales, CD0 higher rate, CD1 additional rate.

If you live in Scotland or Wales and your second job's code has no prefix, that is worth checking, because the code may be applying the wrong nation's rates to your income.

Worked example: Martyna in Norwich

Martyna has two part time jobs. The first pays £7,200 a year and carries code 1257L. The second pays £4,600 and carries BR.

What is happening. Her total income is £11,800, which is below the £12,570 Personal Allowance. She should be paying no Income Tax. Instead the second job is taxed at the basic rate on all £4,600.

What that costs. At 20% on £4,600, roughly £920 a year, taken in monthly instalments she may not have questioned, because tax coming off a payslip looks normal.

Why the codes are not obviously broken. Each code is doing what it says. 1257L gives the full allowance to a job that cannot use all of it, and BR correctly assumes the allowance is spent elsewhere. Nothing is malfunctioning. The allocation is simply wrong for her.

The fix. She asks HMRC to split the allowance across both jobs, so each payroll gets a share and neither over-taxes. The rates and thresholds here are illustrative, and the right split depends on her actual pay.

And the year already gone. Tax overpaid in an earlier year is not lost simply because the code was wrong at the time. That is a separate conversation with HMRC and worth having.

5. What to do about it

Four steps, in order, and none of them cost anything.

  1. Read the code on every payslip you receive. Not just the new one. The problem is usually the combination, not a single code.
  2. Use HMRC's own tool. GOV.UK has a check what your tax code means service that tells you what the numbers and letters mean, how much tax you will pay and what you may need to do next.
  3. Add the jobs up. If the total is under £12,570, you should not be paying Income Tax on any of it, and the allowance needs splitting.
  4. Tell HMRC if it looks wrong. GOV.UK has a page on what to do if you think your tax code is wrong. Employers cannot change a code on their own, so contacting the employer is not the route.

One thing worth knowing before you ring: a code is forward-looking. Changing it fixes the deductions from here on, and any overpayment already made is dealt with separately, usually through the end of year reconciliation or a refund.

Finally, remember that the code has nothing to do with whether the second income is taxable. It is about where your allowance sits. If the second stream is self-employed rather than a payroll job, none of the above applies and a different set of rules does, starting with how the frozen thresholds interact with a rising income.

The honest verdict

BR on a second job means what it says: all income from that job is taxed at the basic rate, because your Personal Allowance has been given to another payroll. GOV.UK describes it as the code usually used where you have more than one job or pension, and for most people with two jobs it is correct.

It is wrong in two specific cases. If your combined income is below the £12,570 Personal Allowance, you are paying tax you do not owe and the allowance should be split. If the job carrying your allowance is no longer the main one, the allowance is in the wrong place.

The code to treat with more suspicion is 0T, because GOV.UK's definition includes the case where the employer simply did not have the details needed to give you a code. That is an information gap, not a decision.

Where this does not help you: it will not tell you what your code should be, because that depends on your total income across every source. Add your jobs up, run the code through HMRC's checker, and contact HMRC rather than your employer if the numbers do not work. And if you are in Scotland or Wales, check for the S or C prefix, because without it the wrong nation's rates may be applied.

Frequently asked

What does BR actually stand for? Basic rate. GOV.UK defines it as all your income from this job or pension being taxed at the basic rate, and notes it is usually used if you have more than one job or pension, because your Personal Allowance is being given against another one.

Is BR on a second job always wrong? No, it is usually right. It becomes wrong when your combined income from all jobs is below the Personal Allowance, in which case the allowance should be split between the codes, or when the allowance is sitting on a job that can no longer use it.

What is the difference between BR and 0T? BR taxes everything from that job at the basic rate and assumes your allowance is elsewhere. 0T means your Personal Allowance has been used up, or that you started a job and your employer did not have the details needed to give you a code. The second of those is often worth querying.

Can my employer change my tax code for me? No. Tax codes come from HMRC. GOV.UK has a page on what to do if you think your tax code is wrong, and that is the route. Your employer applies whatever code it is given.
