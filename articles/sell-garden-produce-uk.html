The Moment You Cook It, You Stop Selling a Carrot and Start Running a Food Business

Every summer the same thing happens. Six courgette plants seemed sensible in April, and by August you are leaving bags of them on neighbours' doorsteps. Selling the surplus is allowed, and the rules are simpler than the internet suggests, but they change sharply at one specific point.

1. The one distinction that decides everything

UK food rules split what you grow into two groups, and almost every question people ask is really a question about which side of this line they are on.

Selling a courgette is one thing. Turning it into chutney and putting a lid on it is a different legal activity, and the change happens the moment the pan goes on.

Know which group you are in before you do anything else. Almost every problem people run into is because they thought they were still in the first column.

2. Selling veg and fruit

This is the easy end. A table at the gate with a few boxes of runner beans and an honesty box is normal practice across the country.

Keep it sensible: sell it clean, sell it fresh, and do not make health claims. "Organic" is a legally protected term requiring certification, so do not use it unless you are certified.

Check your allotment tenancy first. Many councils restrict or forbid selling produce grown on council land, and some allow it only for surplus rather than as a commercial activity. That single check saves a lot of trouble, and it is the most common thing people skip.

Realistic money: a decent vegetable plot in a good year might produce £200 to £400 of surplus. It is pocket money, not a business, and the real return is that you stop buying vegetables yourself. Which crops actually pay is covered in our grow your own guide, and the answer is not potatoes.

3. Selling eggs, and the dates that matter

Eggs have their own specific rules and people get caught out here more than anywhere else.

  • Small flock, under 50 birds. You can sell ungraded eggs directly to consumers at the farm gate or door, not through shops.
  • Registering. Selling loose ungraded eggs from your own premises, including via an honesty box, normally means registering as a food business with your council. It is free and straightforward, and it is not a licence or an inspection fee.
  • Two dates matter. The best before date must be no more than 28 days from laying, and eggs must reach the customer within 21 days of laying.
  • Do not wash them, and do not sell cracked ones.
  • Keeping poultry has its own registration requirements in Great Britain, separate from food business registration. Check the current threshold, because it has changed.

Realistic money: half a dozen sells for around £2 to £3.50 depending on area. Six hens laying well might bring in £15 to £25 a month in summer, against feed costs. This is a hobby that partly pays for itself, not an income, and it drops sharply in winter when laying slows.

4. Jam, chutney and baked goods

Here the bar rises, and this is where the real obligations start.

  1. Register as a food business with your local council. Free, and it must be done at least 28 days before you start trading. Trading unregistered is an offence.
  2. Label properly. That means the name of the food, the full ingredients list in descending order by weight, allergens emphasised, a date mark, the weight, and your business name and address. Allergen labelling is the part people skip and it is the part that matters most, because getting it wrong can genuinely harm someone.
  3. Your kitchen can usually be your home kitchen. Environmental Health may visit, and a clean, organised domestic kitchen with basic records normally passes. The council would rather help you get it right than shut you down, and they are worth ringing before you start rather than after.
  4. Keep records. Batch dates, ingredients used, and where they came from. If something goes wrong, this is what limits the problem to one batch.

Realistic money: jars sell for £3.50 to £6 at markets and fetes. Ingredients and jars cost roughly £1 to £2 a jar. It becomes real money only at volume, which means market stalls, and stall fees of £15 to £40 a day change the maths quickly. Sell twelve jars at £5 with a £25 stall fee and you have made £35 for a day of your life plus the cooking.

6. A worked year, with the real numbers

A standard allotment plot and six hens

Spring. Sells surplus seedlings, tomato and courgette plants raised from seed, at £1 each on a gate table. £85 from seed costing about £8. The best margin of the entire year, and it happens in April before a single vegetable exists.

Summer. The glut. Beans, courgettes, tomatoes on the gate table with an honesty box. £210 across the season, with maybe £15 unpaid because that is what an honesty box costs you.

Eggs, year round. Six hens, registered as a food business, roughly four dozen a month sold at £3. About £144 a year against roughly £110 of feed. Net £34, and the hens were going to be there anyway.

Autumn preserves. Registered 28 days ahead, labelled properly. 40 jars at £5, ingredients and jars about £60, one market stall at £25. £115 net for roughly two full days of cooking, labelling and standing behind a table.

Year total: about £444 net. Against perhaps 200 hours across the year of growing, picking, cooking and selling.

The lesson in the numbers: the seedlings took the least time and made the best return per hour. The preserves took the most time, needed the most compliance, and made £115. Value added beats volume, and early beats late.

7. The tax bit, briefly

Money from selling produce counts as trading income. The first £1,000 of total sales in a tax year is covered by the trading allowance, so most gate sellers never need to tell HMRC anything.

Past £1,000 in gross sales, not profit, you need to register for Self Assessment. On the worked year above, gross sales were about £554, comfortably inside. Add a second market stall and it would not be. The full explanation, including the gross-versus-profit trap, is in our side hustle tax rules guide.

Is it worth doing?

As an income, no. As a way to stop wasting a glut, cover your seed and feed costs, and meet your neighbours, it is one of the better things you can do with a garden.

If you want the growing to actually pay, the money is in the next step up: seedlings and plants in spring sell far better than vegetables in summer, and preserves sell better than the fruit they came from. Value added beats volume, which is the same lesson as our print on demand guide reaches from a completely different direction.

And before anything else, read your allotment tenancy and ring your council. Both are free, both take ten minutes, and both prevent the only problems in this article that actually cost money.

Frequently asked

Do I need insurance for a gate stall? Not legally for casual surplus sales, but public liability is worth considering once you are at markets, and most market organisers require it before they will give you a pitch.

Can I sell at a car boot sale? For primary produce usually yes, subject to the organiser. For processed food you still need to be registered as a food business, and the venue does not change that.

What if I only sell to friends? Selling is selling. The food business rules apply to placing food on the market, not to whether you know the buyer. Giving it away is different.

Do I need a food hygiene certificate? Not strictly a legal requirement for the smallest operations, but you must be trained appropriately for what you do, and councils treat a Level 2 Food Safety course as the working standard. It is a cheap online course and it makes conversations with Environmental Health much shorter.

Is it different in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland? The broad framework is similar but the enforcing bodies and some details differ, with Food Standards Scotland covering Scotland. Check your own nation's guidance.
