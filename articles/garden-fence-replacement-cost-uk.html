There Is No Rule That the Left Hand Fence Is Yours

A fence panel replaced properly costs 95 to 190 pounds fitted, and the number that decides the twenty year bill is not the panel at all. It is what the post is made of. Before any of that, though, work out whether the fence is even yours.

1. Whose fence is it, and how to find out for seven pounds

Almost everybody in Britain believes there is a rule about which fence belongs to which house. Left as you look out from the back door. Right as you face the house from the street. The one with the posts on your side. Every version of this is folklore and none of it has any legal standing.

What actually decides it is the title deeds. Go to GOV.UK, search property information from HM Land Registry, and download the title plan for 7 pounds. On that plan you may see small T shapes drawn against boundary lines. The stem of the T sits on the side of the boundary belonging to the party responsible for maintaining it. An H shape, which is two Ts back to back, means the boundary is shared.

Three things worth knowing before you spend the 7 pounds. Many title plans carry no T marks at all, in which case there is no recorded responsibility and the question is genuinely open. The plan shows general boundaries, not an exact surveyed line, unless someone has applied for a determined boundary. And responsibility to maintain is not the same as ownership of the fence itself, though in practice it decides who pays.

If there are no T marks, the practical answer is a conversation rather than a solicitor. A shared fence replaced on a shared bill is the cheapest outcome available to both households, and the legal route costs more than the fence in about twenty minutes.

2. The posts, which are the entire argument

Walk down any street and look at the fences that have failed. Almost none of them failed in the middle of a panel. They failed where the post enters the ground, because that is where timber sits permanently damp, permanently in contact with soil bacteria, and gets no air.

A pressure treated timber post set in concrete will typically give you eight to twelve years before it snaps at ground level in a gale. When it goes, the panel usually goes with it, and replacing it means digging out a concrete plug that somebody buried around the old post.

A concrete post with a gravel board at the bottom does two things. It removes the timber from ground contact entirely, so the panel never sits in wet soil. And it holds the panel in a slot, so when a panel eventually fails you lift the old one out from above and drop a new one in, with no digging, no concrete and no specialist. That second point is worth more over twenty five years than the first.

The trade off is honest. Concrete posts cost more, they are heavy work to install, and some people dislike how they look. If you are fencing a front garden where appearance carries real weight, that is a legitimate reason to choose timber and accept the shorter life.

If a salesperson tells you composite is the cheapest over its life, ask them to show you the sum.

4. Height, permission and the neighbour conversation

The Planning Portal sets it out plainly. You do not need planning permission to put up or alter a fence provided it does not exceed one metre next to a highway used by vehicles, or a footpath of such a highway, or two metres anywhere else. If an existing fence already exceeds those limits, you must not increase its height.

Three exceptions cancel all of that: a listed building or anything within its curtilage, a boundary with a neighbouring listed building, and any property where the right has been removed by an article four direction or a planning condition. New build estates commonly carry conditions restricting front boundary treatments, so check your own deeds before assuming.

Trellis on top counts towards the height. So does a fence built on top of a raised bed or a retaining wall, because height is measured from ground level.

On the neighbour question, the practical advice is unromantic. Tell them before the contractor arrives, not after. If the fence is on the boundary and the posts will sit partly on their side, you need their agreement to go onto their land at all, and a refusal can leave you building the fence entirely within your own boundary and losing a few centimetres of garden. That conversation costs nothing and prevents the single most common cause of a job stopping half finished.

5. Worked example: the Marsh garden in Wakefield

Tony Marsh has 14 panels of waney lap along the back and one side of a Wakefield semi. Three panels blew out in February and two posts are soft at the base.

First he spends 7 pounds on the title plan. The back boundary carries T marks pointing into his garden, so that run is his. The side boundary carries nothing, so it is undetermined, and his neighbour agrees to split that run fifty fifty.

Quotes for all 14 panels: 1,540 pounds with timber posts, or 2,170 pounds with concrete posts and gravel boards. Removal and disposal is 700 pounds either way.

Over twenty five years the timber build needs doing roughly two and a half times, so call it 3,850 pounds of fence plus 1,750 of removal, which is 224 pounds a year. The concrete build needs the panels replaced once at about 1,100 pounds with no post work and no digging, so 2,170 plus 700 plus 1,100 is 159 pounds a year.

He pays 630 pounds more today and saves about 65 pounds a year for twenty five years. The neighbour pays half of the side run, which takes 420 pounds off his share.

The thing he nearly got wrong: the first quote included taking the old fence to the tip at 700 pounds, and the second did not mention disposal at all. Comparing those two numbers as though they covered the same work would have pointed him at the wrong fence.

Frequently asked

Can I make my neighbour repair their fence? Generally no. Being responsible for a boundary under the deeds usually means you may maintain it, not that you must, unless a specific covenant says otherwise. If their collapsed fence is letting your dog out, the realistic options are to offer to pay for it yourself or to build your own fence just inside your boundary.

Which side do the posts go? There is no rule and no legal requirement. Convention puts the smooth side facing outwards, which is a courtesy rather than an obligation. If you are paying, you choose.

Can I paint my side of their fence? Not without asking. It is their property, and treating or painting it without permission is technically interference. In practice most neighbours say yes instantly, which is why asking is worth the thirty seconds.

What about tree roots and concrete? If a post hole hits a substantial root of a neighbour's tree, or a tree with a preservation order, stop. Cutting roots can destabilise or kill a tree and you can be liable for it. Move the post 200 millimetres and carry on.

The honest verdict

Spend the 7 pounds on the title plan before you spend anything else, then fit concrete posts with gravel boards. At about 7 pounds per panel per year against 12 for a timber post build, the dearer fence is the cheaper one, and the day a panel blows out you replace it yourself in ten minutes.

Choose timber posts only where appearance genuinely matters, such as a front garden, and accept that you are buying about ten years rather than thirty.
