Healthy Start: two ways in, and the £408 figure that is not what people think

Healthy Start helps you buy milk, fruit and vegetables and gets you free vitamins if you are more than 10 weeks pregnant or have a child under 4. The £408 that appears in write-ups is real, but it is a monthly income limit on one specific route, and quoting it as an annual payment sends the wrong people away.

The short version

  • You qualify if you are more than 10 weeks pregnant or have a child under 4, and you are claiming certain benefits (GOV.UK, Healthy Start, read 6 September 2026).
  • If you are under 18 and more than 10 weeks pregnant, you can claim even if you receive no benefits at all. That route needs no qualifying benefit.
  • £408 is a monthly income limit, not an annual payment. It applies to the separate route for families who cannot claim public funds and have a British child under 4.
  • It is not just money. The scheme also covers free vitamins, which are a separate and often forgotten part of it.
  • Scotland is not covered. Best Start Foods is the Scottish scheme and it has its own rules.

1. What the scheme actually gives you

Healthy Start is an NHS scheme, not a DWP benefit, and GOV.UK describes what it does in two parts. If you are more than 10 weeks pregnant or have a child under 4, it can help you:

  • buy healthy foods such as milk or fruit
  • get free vitamins

The money arrives on a prepaid card that is topped up automatically and is spent in shops on a defined list of items. The current card amounts are published and maintained by the NHS on the Healthy Start site rather than on GOV.UK, so that is the place to check what your own family would get, because the amounts differ between pregnancy, a baby under one, and a child aged one to four.

The free vitamins half is the part that gets forgotten. Vitamins for pregnancy and for young children are a recurring cost that most families simply absorb, and this removes it. If you take nothing else from this article, that is the bit worth acting on.

Where it does not apply. GOV.UK: "If you live in Scotland you cannot get Healthy Start. You can apply to the Best Start Foods scheme instead." Best Start Foods is run by Social Security Scotland with its own eligibility rules and its own amounts, so a Scottish family reading a Healthy Start guide is reading the wrong page.

2. Route one: pregnant or a child under 4, on benefits

The main route has two tests and both have to be met.

The circumstance test: you are more than 10 weeks pregnant, or you have a child under 4.

The benefit test: GOV.UK says "You need to be claiming certain benefits to qualify" and points to the NHS Healthy Start site for the detail. The qualifying list is maintained there rather than on GOV.UK, which is a slightly awkward split, and it is the reason to check on healthystart.nhs.uk before concluding you are out.

Two points of timing worth planning around.

  • Ten weeks, not twelve. The threshold is more than 10 weeks pregnant, which is often before the first scan and well before most people start thinking about entitlements. Applying at ten weeks rather than at twenty is several months of card top-ups and vitamins.
  • Under 4, not under 5. The child route ends at the fourth birthday, not at school age. A family that assumes it runs until reception loses a year.

It is also worth claiming alongside the other things that open at the same time. A pregnancy on a qualifying benefit typically also opens the Sure Start Maternity Grant, which is a separate one-off £500 payment with its own form and its own deadline, and free NHS prescriptions and dental treatment during pregnancy and for a year after the birth. None of them are automatic.

3. The under 18 route that needs no benefits at all

This is the single most under-known line on the GOV.UK page, and it is one sentence:

"If you're under 18 and more than 10 weeks pregnant, you can claim even if you do not receive any benefits."

No qualifying benefit. No means test described on the page. The two conditions are your age and how far along the pregnancy is.

The reason it matters is that a pregnant 17 year old is exactly the person least likely to be claiming a benefit in her own right, most likely to be living in someone else's household, and least likely to have been told this exists. Every general guide leads with "you need to be claiming certain benefits", which is the sentence that makes her stop reading.

If you know someone in that position, this paragraph is the one to send them.

4. Route two: a British child, and the £408 a month limit

There is a second route, and it is where the £408 figure actually lives.

GOV.UK, under the heading "If you're not a British citizen but your child is", says you might be eligible if all of the following are true:

  • you have at least one British child under 4 years old
  • your family earns £408 or less per month after tax
  • you cannot claim public funds, either because of your immigration status or because you do not have an immigration status

So £408 is a monthly household income ceiling, measured after tax, on one specific route. It is not an annual payment, it is not what the scheme gives you, and it is not a figure that applies to the ordinary benefits route at all.

That distinction is not pedantry. Quoting £408 as an amount you receive tells the wrong families they are eligible and, worse, tells a family on £390 a month that the number has nothing to do with them.

How to check whether you cannot claim public funds. GOV.UK says you can look at your eVisa, which is the digital record of your identity and immigration status, and you can get a share code to prove your immigration status. You can also check an expired biometric residence permit, or a Home Office letter that states whether you can claim public funds.

How to apply on this route. It is different from the ordinary one. You cannot use the standard online form. You have to ask for an application form by email, at healthystartnrpf@nhsbsa.nhs.uk, and GOV.UK is clear that you should only use that address if you think you cannot claim public funds because of your immigration status. There is separate published application guidance on GOV.UK called the Healthy Start extension.

What this route does not do. It does not change your immigration status, it does not count as claiming public funds, and it does not create any entitlement to anything else. It is a food and vitamins scheme, and that is the whole of it. If you are unsure how any application interacts with your status, take free advice before you send anything, from Citizens Advice or from an OISC registered adviser. 5. Amara in Coventry: £390 a month and a British toddler

Amara lives in Coventry with her husband and their two year old daughter, who was born in the UK and is a British citizen. Amara's leave states that she cannot claim public funds. The household's income after tax is £390 a month.

She reads a guide that describes Healthy Start as being for people on Universal Credit, concludes it is not for her, and closes the tab.

What the rule actually says. Test by test:

ConditionAmara's positionMet
At least one British child under 4Daughter is 2 and BritishYes
Family earns £408 or less per month after tax£390 a monthYes
Cannot claim public fundsLeave states no recourse to public fundsYes

All three are met, so she is potentially eligible, and the route is the email address rather than the ordinary form.

Change one number. At £420 a month after tax she would be over the limit and out, on a difference of £12 a month. That is a hard cliff and it is worth measuring your own figure properly rather than estimating it, because the test is on income after tax and for the family rather than for one person.

Change the child. If her daughter were not a British citizen, this route would not be available at all, whatever the household income. GOV.UK links to its own check on whether a child is a British citizen, and that is the right place to settle it rather than assuming from a birth certificate.

And the timing. Her daughter turns 4 in two years, and the entitlement ends there. Two years of card top-ups and free vitamins is worth an email today.

The verdict

If you are more than 10 weeks pregnant or have a child under 4 and you are on a qualifying benefit, apply on the NHS Healthy Start site and take the free vitamins as well as the card. If you are under 18 and more than 10 weeks pregnant, apply even though you claim nothing, because that route does not need a benefit at all. And if you are not a British citizen, your child under 4 is, your family income after tax is £408 a month or less, and you cannot claim public funds, there is a separate route that exists specifically for you and it starts with an email rather than the ordinary form. What £408 is not is a payment, and anyone telling you otherwise has misread the page.

Frequently asked

Is £408 what I get? No. On GOV.UK the £408 is a monthly income limit, after tax, that applies to the route for families who cannot claim public funds and have a British child under 4. What the scheme pays is a prepaid card topped up automatically, and the current amounts are published by the NHS on the Healthy Start site.

Which benefits qualify? GOV.UK says you need to be claiming certain benefits and directs you to the NHS Healthy Start website for the current list. Because that list is maintained there rather than on GOV.UK, check it directly before ruling yourself out.

Can I claim if I am pregnant and not on any benefits? Only on one route: if you are under 18 and more than 10 weeks pregnant, GOV.UK says you can claim even if you do not receive any benefits. Otherwise the benefit test applies.

Does Healthy Start count as claiming public funds? The whole point of the route for families with a British child is that it is available to people who cannot claim public funds.
