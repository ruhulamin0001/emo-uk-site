Employed Assistants Earn £13.54 an Hour. Freelance VAs Charge £25 to £50 for the Same Work

The gap between what an employed assistant earns and what a freelance one charges is the largest of any common side hustle, and it is not because freelancers are exploiting anyone. Understanding exactly why the gap exists is what stops you pricing yourself at the wrong end of it.

Ruhul Amin Updated 21 August 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked August 2026
Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • An employed assistant in the UK averages about £13.54 an hour. A freelance VA typically charges £25 to £35, and specialists £50 or more.
  • The gap is not profit. It covers your tax, National Insurance, holiday, sick pay, pension, software, insurance and every unbilled hour you spend finding clients.
  • Mid level VAs charge £15 to £25. Agencies charge clients £30 to £40 and pay their VAs less than that.
  • Retainers are where this becomes a real income. Part time retainers run £1,200 to £2,500 a month, full time £2,500 to £4,500.
  • Charging £12 an hour is the classic beginner error. After costs it is below minimum wage, and it signals inexperience to exactly the clients who pay well.

1. The rate bands, and where you fit

The band you land in is decided far more by what you can do than by how long you have been doing it. A VA who can run a client's bookkeeping or their CRM is in a different market from one offering inbox management, and the price difference is roughly double.

2. Why the freelance rate is double

The most common reason new VAs underprice is comparing their rate to a salary, which is not the right comparison. Work through where £30 an hour actually goes.

LineEffect on £30 an hour
Income tax and National InsuranceComes out of your rate, not on top of it
No paid holidayAn employee gets 28 days paid. You bill nothing those days
No sick pay, no pension contributionBoth are yours to fund
Software, insurance, accountantPerhaps £40 to £80 a month
Unbilled timeMarketing, proposals, invoicing, chasing payment. Realistically 20 to 30% of your working hours

Do the arithmetic. If you bill 25 hours a week at £30 but spend 8 more unbilled, you are working 33 hours for £750, which is £22.70 an hour before tax and costs. That is not far above the employed rate, and it is why £30 an hour is a normal price rather than a greedy one.

A freelance rate is not a wage with a bigger number on it. It is a wage plus everything an employer would otherwise pay for on your behalf.

3. Hourly against retainer

Hourly billing is how almost everyone starts and it has two real problems. Your income stops when you stop, and you are financially punished for getting faster.

Retainers fix both. The client buys a block of hours each month at a slightly reduced rate, and you get predictable income. UK figures: £1,200 to £2,500 a month for part time support, £2,500 to £4,500 for full time.

  • Sell blocks, not open ended time. Ten, twenty or forty hours a month, agreed in advance.
  • A modest discount is fair. Five to ten per cent for a committed retainer. Not thirty.
  • State whether hours roll over. Usually they should not, or you accumulate an unpayable liability.
  • Invoice at the start of the month, not the end. This single change fixes most cash flow problems for new freelancers.

Two or three part time retainers is a full time income, and it is a far more stable business than twenty hourly clients.

4. What clients actually pay a premium for

General admin sits at the bottom of the range because the supply is large. The jump to £35 and above comes from a specific capability the client cannot easily replace.

  • Bookkeeping. Reconciling accounts, chasing invoices, preparing records for an accountant. With Making Tax Digital for Income Tax now live for sole traders and landlords over £50,000, demand for this went up, not down.
  • CRM and systems. Setting up and running HubSpot, Notion, Airtable, or automations. Business owners will pay well to never touch this themselves.
  • Executive support. Diary management for a genuinely busy person, inbox triage with the authority to make decisions. This is trust based work and it prices accordingly.
  • Industry knowledge. A VA who understands estate agency compliance, or how a physiotherapy clinic books, needs no training and can charge for that.
  • Content and social. Not just scheduling. Writing the posts, editing the video, handling the replies.

The pattern is straightforward: you are paid for what the client cannot do or refuses to do, not for hours of general helpfulness.

5. Starting with no clients and no budget

  • Pick two services, not twelve. "Inbox and diary management for consultants" gets clients. "Anything you need" does not.
  • Register as a sole trader. Free, and required once you pass £1,000 of gross trading income.
  • Get professional indemnity insurance before your first client, roughly £60 to £150 a year. Many clients ask, and you want to be able to say yes.
  • Write one simple contract. Scope, rate, hours, payment terms, notice period, confidentiality. A template is fine, but have one.
  • Find the first three clients through people, not platforms. Local business groups, LinkedIn, and telling everyone you know. Platforms pay the least and take the longest.
  • Set your rate at £22 to £25 and hold it. Low enough to win early work, high enough that you are not trapped there.
  • Handle client data properly. You will hold personal data, so UK GDPR applies to you. Check whether you need to register with the ICO, keep client data separate, and use a password manager.

6. A worked first year

Priya, 41, former office manager in Reading. Starts part time around school hours.

Setup costs: £186. Insurance £96, domain and email £30, password manager £30, accounting software £30 for the year. No course, no website builder, no logo designer.

Months one to three. One client at £22 an hour, six hours a week. £1,584 gross. She spends roughly the same again unbilled on outreach, so her true rate is nearer £11. This is normal and it is temporary.

Months four to six. Second client, and she moves the first onto a 20 hour monthly retainer at £24. £3,400 gross, unbilled time falling as referrals start.

Months seven to nine. She learns Xero properly, two evenings a week for a month, and adds bookkeeping. New clients at £32. Existing clients moved to £28 at renewal. £5,100 gross.

Months ten to twelve. Two retainers plus ad hoc work, about 22 billed hours a week at an average £30. £7,900 gross.

Year one: about £18,000 gross on part time hours. Take off roughly £600 of costs and set aside for tax. The rate went from £22 to £30 not because a year passed, but because she added one skill clients could not do themselves.

7. The traps that keep VAs cheap

  • Competing with overseas rates. VAs abroad charge a fraction of UK rates. You will never win that comparison, and the clients who make it are not the clients you want. Sell the things distance makes hard: your timezone, your understanding of UK business, your accountability.
  • Scope creep. "Could you also just..." is how a 10 hour retainer becomes 18 hours for the same money. Track your time from day one and raise it at 12 hours, not at 18.
  • Never raising rates on existing clients. Review annually, give a month of notice, and expect to lose one client occasionally. That is the cost of not being underpaid by all of them.
  • Discounting to win the first client. A client acquired at £15 rarely accepts £30 later. Your first rate is close to your ceiling with that client.
  • Working without a contract. Most disputes are about scope, not money, and a one page contract prevents nearly all of them.
  • Ignoring the £1,000 trading allowance. It is on gross income, so at £22 an hour you cross it in about 46 billed hours.

The honest verdict

Virtual assistance is one of the few side hustles that comfortably clears the £12.71 benchmark from the first client, provided you do not price yourself as an employee. £25 to £35 an hour is the ordinary UK market rate and asking for it is not ambitious.

The route to a real income is retainers rather than hourly work, and one specialist skill rather than general helpfulness. Bookkeeping is the highest return addition for most people, and MTD has made it more in demand.

Start at £22 to £25, get three clients through people you know rather than platforms, add a specialism in the first year, and review your rate every twelve months. That is the whole plan, and the part most people skip is the review.

Frequently asked

Do I need a qualification to be a VA? No. Clients want evidence and references. Software certifications, particularly in accounting packages, do help you charge more, but nothing is required to start.

Should I go through an agency? It removes the marketing problem and pays less. Agencies charge clients £30 to £40 and keep a share. Useful for your first months, not a long term plan.

Do I need insurance? Professional indemnity is strongly advised and often asked for. Around £60 to £150 a year.

What about GDPR? You will handle personal data on behalf of clients, so UK GDPR applies. Keep client data separated, use strong unique passwords, and check the ICO guidance on whether you need to register.
