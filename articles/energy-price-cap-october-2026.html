The Price Cap Announcement Is Days Away: What to Actually Do This Week

Ofgem confirms the October energy price cap by 26 August, and forecasters expect another rise to around £1,700. Here is what the cap genuinely controls, the fix-or-stay decision explained with real numbers, and the four things worth doing before the announcement lands.

1. What the price cap actually caps

This is the single most misunderstood number in UK household finance. The headline figure, currently £1,663, is not a maximum bill. It is what a household using a typical amount of gas and electricity would pay over a year at the capped rates.

What Ofgem actually limits is two things: the unit rate you pay per kilowatt hour, and the standing charge you pay each day regardless of use. If you use more than the typical household, your bill will be higher than the headline number. If you use less, it will be lower.

So when the news says the cap is rising 2%, that means the rates rise about 2%. Your own bill moves by that percentage against your own usage, not against the headline.

The price cap is a speed limit on the rate, not a ceiling on the total. Nothing in it stops a cold house with an old boiler costing far more than the number on the news.

That gap of roughly £132 is worth pausing on. If you pay on receipt of bill and could comfortably move to monthly direct debit, that is a saving available today with no switching, no comparison site and no risk.

It is also worth knowing that July's cap was around 13% higher than April's, largely driven by wholesale gas prices responding to conflict in the Middle East. That is the context for why bills have felt worse this summer despite it being summer.

3. What October is expected to bring

Cornwall Insight, the forecaster most quoted on this, has predicted the October cap at approximately £1,700 a year for a typical household, a further rise of around 2%.

Two caveats matter. First, this is a forecast. The confirmed figure comes from Ofgem by 26 August 2026. Second, the calculation uses a wholesale market observation window running from 19 May to 18 August, so late movements in that window can shift the outcome.

The government removing VAT on electricity eases part of the pressure, but the expectation is still that overall bills rise rather than fall. There has also been discussion from the Chancellor about future support being linked to household income, effectively a social tariff, though nothing is confirmed.

4. Fix or stay: the decision, honestly

This is the question everybody asks and almost nobody answers properly, because the honest answer is that it depends on the specific deal in front of you.

Here is the framework that actually works.

  1. Find your current unit rates and standing charge, from your latest bill or your supplier's app. Not the headline cap figure, your actual rates.
  2. Compare a fixed deal against those rates, not against the cap. A fix that is 3% below the current cap is only a good deal if the cap does not fall below it during the fix.
  3. Check the exit fees. A fix with high exit fees removes your ability to react if prices drop.
  4. Match the length to your situation. A twelve month fix through winter buys certainty over the expensive months. A two year fix is a bigger bet on where prices go.

The rough rule most analysts use: a fix at or slightly below the current cap level is reasonable insurance if certainty matters to you, and a poor gamble if you are purely chasing the lowest possible cost. Nobody, including the forecasters, knows where prices go next winter.

Worked example: the Doyle household

Three bedroom semi, two adults, one child. They use slightly more than typical, so at current capped rates their annual cost is around £1,850, not the headline £1,663.

If October rises 2% as forecast, their cost goes to roughly £1,887, about £37 more a year, or roughly £3 a month. Meaningful, but not the catastrophe the headline percentage implies for them.

They pay quarterly on receipt of bill. Moving to monthly direct debit is worth around £130 a year at typical use, and proportionally more for them. That single change is worth over three times the forecast increase.

They turn the thermostat down 1°C when the heating comes back on in October. On their usage that is roughly £150 a year, again more than the price rise.

The lesson is not that the cap does not matter. It is that two free decisions in their own control outweigh the announcement they were worrying about.

5. Four things to do this week

Submit a meter reading before 30 September. This is the important one. If your supplier estimates your usage across the change of rates, they can apply the new higher rate to gas and electricity you actually used in September. A reading dated at the end of the month removes that risk entirely. Take a photo of the meter.

Check your payment method. If you are on quarterly billing and can manage monthly direct debit, that is a difference of over £130 a year at typical use.

Look at your direct debit amount, not just the rate. Suppliers adjust these automatically and often build a large credit balance in summer. If you are hundreds of pounds in credit and the amount keeps rising, you can ask for a review or a refund. It is your money.

Do the free efficiency wins now, not in November. Bleed radiators, put foil behind those on external walls, sort draughts around doors and letterboxes. Doing this before the heating goes on is worth far more than doing it in December, and our energy bills guide covers what actually pays.

6. Help you may be entitled to

A surprising amount of support goes unclaimed every year, usually because people assume they will not qualify.

  • Warm Home Discount. A rebate off your electricity bill for eligible households. Check the criteria rather than assuming.
  • Winter Fuel Payment. For those of eligible age, subject to the current rules.
  • Priority Services Register. Free, and available if you are of pensionable age, disabled, chronically ill, or have young children. It gives extra support during outages and with meter access.
  • Supplier hardship funds. Several large suppliers run grant schemes that can clear energy debt. They are not advertised loudly. Ask directly.
  • Insulation and boiler grants. Eligibility depends on your home and income, and the schemes change, so check the current position on GOV.UK.

If you are already behind on energy bills, contact your supplier before it escalates. They are obliged to discuss a payment plan, and doing it early gives you far more options.

7. Three myths that cost people money

"The cap means my bill cannot go above £1,700." It cannot. The cap limits rates, not totals. A large or poorly insulated home will pay well above the headline.

"Leaving the heating on low all day is cheaper." For most UK homes it is not. Heating when you need it, with a sensible schedule, costs less than heating an empty house continuously.

"Switching is pointless now." The switching market is quieter than it was, but fixed deals do exist and the payment method difference alone is worth over £130 a year. Being on the default tariff with quarterly billing is the most expensive place to sit.

8. The honest verdict

The October announcement, due by 26 August, is likely to bring another modest rise of around 2%. For most households that is a few pounds a month, not a crisis, though it lands on top of a July increase that was much larger.

The useful response is not to wait for the number. Submit a meter reading before 30 September, check whether monthly direct debit saves you the £130-plus gap, review your direct debit if you are sitting in large credit, and do the cheap draught proofing before the heating goes on.

Those four steps are worth more than the announcement, and unlike the announcement, they are entirely within your control.

Frequently asked

When exactly is the October cap announced? Ofgem confirms it by 26 August 2026, and the new level applies from 1 October to 31 December 2026.

Should I fix before the announcement? Only if a specific deal in front of you beats your current rates and the exit fees are acceptable. Fixing blind, purely because a rise is expected, is guessing rather than planning.

Does the cap apply in Northern Ireland? No. Ofgem's cap covers England, Wales and Scotland. Northern Ireland has a separate regulator and market.

What if I have a prepayment meter? There is a separate cap level for prepayment. Check your supplier's current rates, and make sure you are on the Priority Services Register if you qualify.
