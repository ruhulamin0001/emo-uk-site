One Audio Hour Takes Four Hours to Type. That Ratio Decides Whether Transcription Pays

Transcription is quoted per audio minute, which sounds generous until you notice that an audio minute is not a minute of your time. Once you convert the rate into an hourly figure, most of the work available to beginners falls below minimum wage, and AI has taken the bottom out of the rest.

The short version

  • The average UK freelance transcription rate is about £1.04 per audio minute, ranging from 65p to £3.50. Agencies charge clients roughly 90p to £1.50 a minute for standard work.
  • A beginner needs about four hours to transcribe one audio hour. At £1.04 a minute that is £62.40 for four hours of work, so £15.60 an hour. At the 65p end it is £9.75.
  • UK transcriptionists average around £14 an hour, or roughly £29,980 a year full time. That is the realistic ceiling, not the headline per minute rate.
  • AI transcription costs about $0.25 an audio minute and hits 95% accuracy or better. The plain typing job at the bottom of the market is largely gone.
  • What survives is quality control and specialisation: legal, medical, technical and regulated content where being 95% right is not acceptable.

1. The ratio that decides everything

Transcription is priced per audio minute, meaning one minute of recording, not one minute of your time. The gap between those two things is the entire economics of the job.

Your levelHours of work per audio hourWhat that means
Complete beginner5 to 6Constant rewinding, unfamiliar with the tools
A few weeks in4The realistic working assumption
Experienced, clean single speaker audio2.5 to 3Good touch typing, foot pedal, familiar subject
Poor audio, several speakers, heavy accents6 to 8This is where people lose money

Everything follows from that ratio. A job advertised at £1.20 a minute is £72 an audio hour. At a 4:1 ratio that is £18 an hour. At 6:1, because the recording turned out to be a noisy four person meeting, it is £12 an hour, below the National Living Wage of £12.71.

Which is why the single most important habit in this work is listening to a sample before accepting a job. The rate does not tell you what you will earn. The audio quality does.

The rate is per audio minute. Your wage is per real minute. Nobody who has quoted you a price has any incentive to help you convert between the two.

2. What the work actually pays

Two different numbers get quoted, and it matters which one you are looking at.

What agencies charge clients: roughly 90p to £1.50 per audio minute for a standard two speaker interview, so £54 to £90 per audio hour. Complex multi speaker work rises to £2 to £5 a minute, or £120 to £300 an hour.

What a freelance transcriptionist earns: per minute rates run from 65p to £3.50, with an average around £1.04. If you work through an agency rather than directly, you see a share of the client rate rather than all of it.

Converted to your time at the realistic 4:1 ratio:

3. What AI took, and what it left

This is the part older guides do not mention, and it changes the advice completely.

Automated transcription now costs around $0.25 per audio minute, and services quote as little as $10 per audio hour against a minimum of roughly $100 for a human professional. Accuracy on clean audio is 95% or better for the stronger engines, with human transcription still ahead at 99% plus. Consumer meeting tools sit around 85 to 90%.

What that killed: straightforward typing of clean, single speaker, non technical audio for general clients. If a machine does it for a tenth of the price at 95% accuracy and the client does not need better, that work is not coming back.

What it did not kill, and in some cases made more valuable:

  • Regulated and sensitive content. Legal, medical, police, safeguarding. Where 95% is not a pass mark and confidentiality is contractual.
  • Technical vocabulary. Engineering, pharmaceutical, financial. AI mangles domain terms it has not seen, and a wrong drug name is not a typo.
  • Difficult audio. Several speakers talking over each other, strong regional accents, background noise, poor recordings. Machines degrade sharply here and humans do not.
  • Editing and quality control of AI output. This is now a real job. Faster than typing from scratch, and it is where a lot of the remaining work sits.
  • Verbatim work. Where every stutter, pause and false start must be captured, usually for research or legal purposes.

The strategic point: do not compete with a machine on speed and price. Compete on the things it gets wrong, and charge accordingly. That is the difference between the 65p end and the £3.50 end.

4. Does it pass the £12.71 test?

Measured against the National Living Wage:

  • Beginner on a general platform at 65p to 80p a minute: fails. £9.75 to £12 an hour at a 4:1 ratio, and worse if the audio is poor.
  • Beginner on average rates: passes narrowly. £15.60 an hour before tax and unpaid admin.
  • Experienced at 3:1 on average rates: passes well. Around £20 an hour.
  • Specialist in legal, medical or technical: passes comfortably. This is where the job becomes genuinely worth doing.

The honest reading: general transcription is a marginal side hustle in 2026. Specialist transcription is a decent one. The difference is a few months of deliberately building toward a niche rather than taking whatever appears.

5. Getting faster, which is the only real lever

You cannot easily raise your rate in your first months. You can halve the hours a job takes, which has exactly the same effect on your earnings.

  • A foot pedal. Around £30 to £60, and it is the single biggest speed gain available. Stopping and rewinding with your feet instead of your hands removes thousands of interruptions.
  • Proper transcription software. Adjustable playback speed, automatic rewind on pause, timestamp shortcuts. Free options exist and are far better than a media player and a document side by side.
  • Text expansion. Shortcuts for speaker names, recurring phrases and formatting. Ten minutes of setup pays back on the first job.
  • Touch typing. If you are below 60 words a minute, that is the bottleneck. Free practice sites fix it in a few weeks and it raises everything else.
  • Start from an AI draft where the client allows it. Editing a 95% accurate draft is usually faster than typing from nothing. Check the contract, because some clients forbid uploading their audio to third party services, and for confidential material you must not.

6. A worked first three months

Jo, 44, Newcastle. Twelve hours a week. £48 spent on a foot pedal.

Month one. General platform work at 80p a minute. Ratio 5:1 because she is learning. Transcribes 9 audio hours across roughly 45 working hours. £432 gross, £9.60 an hour. Below minimum wage, and the honest truth of month one.

Month two. Foot pedal arrives, she sets up text expansion, ratio improves to 4:1. Same 48 working hours produces 12 audio hours. £576, £12 an hour. Still marginally under.

Month three. She stops taking everything. She listens to a sample before accepting and turns down bad audio, and she approaches two local companies directly at £1.30 a minute. Ratio now 3.5:1 on the work she has chosen. 13 audio hours in 45 working hours. £1,014, £22.50 an hour.

What changed between month one and month three. Not her typing. She bought a pedal, she stopped accepting bad audio, and she went direct instead of through a platform. Her rate per minute went up 62% and her hours per job went down 30%, which together more than doubled her hourly earnings.

Tax note: she passed £1,000 of gross trading income during month two and needs to register for Self Assessment.

The honest verdict

General transcription is no longer a good side hustle. Automated services at $0.25 an audio minute with 95% accuracy have removed the floor, and platform rates for straightforward typing sit below minimum wage once you apply the four to one ratio.

Specialist transcription is a different job and still pays properly. Legal, medical, technical and difficult audio all need a human, and rates up to £3.50 a minute exist for people who can do it reliably.

If you want to try it: buy a foot pedal, always sample the audio before accepting, aim to get your ratio under 4:1, and pick a niche within three months. Without the niche, you are competing with software on price, and that is not a competition you can win.

Frequently asked

Do I need a qualification? No, but medical and legal transcription have their own terminology and formatting conventions, and courses in those niches genuinely help you charge more. General transcription courses are usually not worth the money.

What equipment do I actually need? A computer, decent over ear headphones, and a foot pedal. Nothing else is essential. Total outlay under £100.

How fast should I be able to type? Aim for 60 to 80 words a minute with good accuracy. Below 60, fixing your typing speed is the highest return thing you can do.

Is transcription safe work for confidential material? It can be, and that responsibility is yours. Do not upload confidential client audio to consumer AI services, keep files encrypted, and expect to sign confidentiality agreements for legal and medical work.
