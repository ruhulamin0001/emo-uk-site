A Dehumidifier Does Not Save You Money. Except In One Room, Where It Pays Back In Two Years.

Dehumidifiers get sold as an energy saving in winter, which they are not: a machine that draws 216 watts cannot lower your bill on its own. What it can do is dry a load of washing for about 32p, replace a condenser tumble dryer, and stop the wall behind the wardrobe growing mould. We took the wattage and extraction figures from Meaco's own data table, priced them at the October 2026 cap, and worked out what the machine really costs per hour, per litre and per wash load.

The short version

  • A 20-litre compressor dehumidifier draws 216 W at 20 degrees and 60% humidity, according to Meaco's published table for the Arete One 20L. At 26.32p per kWh that is 5.7p an hour.
  • In a cold room it is cheaper per hour but far dearer per litre. At 10 degrees the same machine draws 180 W but extracts only 3.4 litres a day instead of 8.5, so water costs about 33p a litre instead of 16p.
  • Eight hours a day for a six-month winter is about £82 a year. Running it around the clock is about £246.
  • Drying one wash load, about two litres of water, costs roughly 32p in a warm room. That is a third of the 92p a condenser tumble dryer uses.
  • Replacing a condenser dryer for 160 loads a year saves about £96, so a £180 dehumidifier pays back in under two years. Used only to "feel less damp", it never pays back, because it is replacing nothing.
  • The 20-litre number on the box is a laboratory maximum at 30 degrees and 80% humidity. In a British bedroom in January expect a fifth of that.

1. The manufacturer figures, and what the litres on the box mean

Meaco publishes an extraction table for its Arete One 20L that gives the power draw and the water removed at six combinations of temperature and humidity. That is rare, and it is the reason we used this machine rather than a cheaper one with no data. Here is the table, exactly as published, with our cost columns added at the Ofgem rate of 26.32p per kWh for 1 October to 31 December 2026.

10C, 60% humidity3.4 litres180 W4.7p33.4p
10C, 80% humidity5.68 litres184 W4.8p20.5p

20C, 60% humidity8.5 litres216 W5.7p16.0p
20C, 80% humidity14.02 litres225 W5.9p10.1p

Cost per litre is power multiplied by 24 hours divided by the litres removed, then priced. It is the number that tells you where the machine is worth running, and the table says something the box does not: a compressor dehumidifier is at its worst in exactly the cold, mildly damp bedroom people buy it for. At 10 degrees and 60% humidity every litre costs twice what it costs at 20 degrees, because the compressor draws nearly the same power but the cold air holds far less water for it to find.

The "20 litres a day" on the box is the extraction at 30 degrees and 80% humidity, a test condition that does not occur in a British house. A 20-litre machine in a January bedroom at 12 degrees is a 3 to 4 litre machine. That is not a criticism of Meaco, who publish the real figures, it is a criticism of the way every dehumidifier is named.

Meaco gives a five-year warranty on this range. Cheaper compressor models from other brands are commonly one or two years, and at 200 watts of continuous winter duty the compressor is the part that fails. That does not change the running cost, but it changes the cost over ten winters.

2. Cost per hour, per day and per winter

Taking the 20 degrees, 60% row as a lived-in room in winter, all our own arithmetic:

Eight hours a day, 180 days311£82 a winter

The eight-hour figure is the honest one for most homes, because a machine with a humidistat stops when the air reaches the target humidity and restarts when it climbs. The 24-hour figure is what you pay if you set the target too low or put the machine in a room that can never dry, such as one with a leak, a broken extractor fan or washing hung up every day. £246 a winter is more than many people's tumble dryer bill, and it is entirely possible to spend it while being told the machine saves energy.

The claim that a dehumidifier lowers heating bills because dry air feels warmer is not something we can price, because nobody publishes a figure for it and we do not compute figures we cannot source. Treat it as comfort, not a saving.

3. Cost per litre: why a cold room is the expensive place to run it

The cost per litre column is the useful one for deciding where to put the machine, because your goal is water out of the house, not hours on the meter.

At 20 degrees and 60% humidity it is 16p a litre. At 10 degrees it is 33p. At 10 degrees and 50% it is 56p, which is the condition of an unheated spare room in February that is only slightly damp, and it means the machine is spending most of its electricity moving air it cannot extract anything from.

Two practical consequences. First, put the machine in the warm room where the moisture is made, the kitchen, the bathroom after a shower, the room where washing dries, and let it work at 16p a litre, rather than in the cold room where the mould appears, at 33p. The water travels; the machine does not need to. Second, if the problem room is cold and damp, the cheaper fix is usually heat plus ventilation, because warming the room lets the air hold the water and opening the window lets it out for free.

The exception is a desiccant dehumidifier, which uses a heated absorbent wheel rather than a compressor. Those work well in the cold but draw 300 to 600 W and throw out heat, so they are dearer per hour and only sensible in garages and unheated rooms. We have not priced one here because we could not confirm a like-for-like extraction table this run.

4. The one use that pays: drying washing

A wash load after a fast spin holds somewhere between one and a half and three litres of water. Call it two. At 20 degrees and 60% humidity the Arete removes 8.5 litres in 24 hours, so two litres takes about 5.6 hours and uses about 1.22 kWh: 32p a load. That is our calculation from Meaco's table.

The dryer figures are from Hotpoint's published annual consumption figures divided by 160 cycles, and the heated airer from Lakeland's 300 W rating; we set them out in full in our heated airer versus tumble dryer article.

So the dehumidifier is not the cheapest way to dry washing. The heated airer is, among the things that cost anything. But the dehumidifier does two jobs at once: it dries the clothes and it takes the two litres out of the house rather than putting them into the bedroom air, which is the exact problem the airer and the clothes horse create. In a flat with no outdoor line and no tumble dryer that is worth the extra 12p a load.

Against a condenser tumble dryer, the saving is 60p a load, or about £96 a year at 160 loads. A £180 machine pays back in about 1.9 years, and it goes on doing the damp job in between.

5. Worked example: Marcus in Swansea

One-bed flat, no outside space, condensation on the bedroom window every morning

Marcus rents a first-floor flat with a combi boiler, a clothes horse in the living room and a condenser tumble dryer the landlord left behind. He does three loads a week and the bedroom window runs with water every morning from October to March.

His current drying cost. 156 loads a year at 92p in the condenser: £144 a year. The condensation is separate: the water comes from breathing, showering and cooking, not the dryer, which vents its own moisture into a tank.

Buying a dehumidifier for the damp only. Eight hours a night in the bedroom at 10 to 12 degrees, roughly the 4.7p row: 180 nights at 38p is £68 a winter, and it removes perhaps a litre a night at 33p a litre. The window stops streaming. The dryer bill is unchanged. Net cost £68 a year, and the machine never pays back because it is replacing nothing.

Buying it and retiring the dryer. Washing goes on the clothes horse in the living room with the dehumidifier beside it, six hours a load at 32p: 156 loads is £50. Bedroom door left open, so the same machine pulls the overnight moisture too. Total electricity for drying and damp: about £50 to £60 a year against £144 before. Saving £84 to £94. A £180 machine pays back in about two years.

What he should do first, for nothing. Run the bathroom extractor for twenty minutes after every shower, keep lids on pans, and open the bedroom window for ten minutes each morning. That will not dry the washing, but it might halve the condensation before he spends £180 on it.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy a dehumidifier to save money on heating. It cannot. It draws around 200 watts and every one of them goes on your bill; the "dry air feels warmer" argument has no published figure behind it and we will not invent one.

Do buy one if you dry washing indoors and currently own a condenser tumble dryer. Used that way a compressor model at roughly £180 pays back in about two years, dries a load for about 32p, and removes the water from the house instead of putting it on the walls. Put it in the warm room where the moisture is made, not the cold room where the mould appears, and buy a model whose maker publishes its power draw at 10 and 20 degrees, because that table is the only honest description of what you are getting.

Frequently asked

How much does it cost to run a dehumidifier for 24 hours? At 216 W and 26.32p per kWh, about £1.36 a day, or £246 over a 180-day winter. With a humidistat cycling it for eight hours a day, about 45p a day.

Is it cheaper to run a dehumidifier or a tumble dryer? Per wash load, the dehumidifier at about 32p is cheaper than a condenser dryer at 92p and a heat pump dryer at 44p, but dearer than a heated airer at about 20p. It is the only one that also removes the moisture from the room.

Does a dehumidifier reduce heating bills? There is no published figure for it, so we do not claim one. The machine adds around 200 W to your bill whenever it runs; any warmth it gives off is electricity you paid for at the full rate.

Compressor or desiccant? Compressor for heated rooms, where it is cheapest per litre. Desiccant for garages and unheated rooms below about 10 degrees, accepting a higher hourly cost. We could not confirm a matching desiccant data table this run, so those are not priced here.
