Skilled Worker Visa and Modern Slavery: What the 3 September Rule Change Really Does

3 September 2026 · Updated 22 September 2026, 08:00 · 12 min read · Rules checked September 2026

The short version
  • The change was laid on 3 September 2026 and takes effect on 8 October 2026. From that date a Skilled Worker visa holder recognised as a victim of modern slavery can work for any employer for the rest of their existing permission, without a new sponsor. Until 8 October the old rules still apply.
  • The change is in Statement of Changes HC 584, amending Appendix Skilled Worker.
  • It is not automatic and it is not immediate. You need a positive Conclusive Grounds decision from the Home Office through the National Referral Mechanism first.
  • At 31 August 2026 there were 5,948 cases still queuing for that decision, and the ones still queuing had been waiting a median of 69 days. Of the decisions actually issued in the first quarter of 2026, 68% were positive.
  • The rule does not extend your visa. It only removes the tie to one employer for whatever time you have left.

What actually changed on 3 September

A Skilled Worker visa is normally tied to one sponsor. Your permission to be in the UK is attached to a specific employer holding a specific Certificate of Sponsorship. Change jobs and you normally need a new sponsor and a fresh application.

That tie is what makes exploitation possible. If leaving means losing your visa, an employer who is underpaying you, withholding wages or threatening you knows you probably will not leave.

On 3 September 2026 the Home Office laid Statement of Changes in Immigration Rules HC 584, amending Appendix Skilled Worker. The amendment lifts the sponsor restriction for Skilled Workers who have been identified by the Home Office Competent Authorities as victims of modern slavery. Those recognised victims can work for any employer for the remaining duration of their existing permission.

Correction and clarification, added 3 September 2026 at 20:30. An earlier version of this page said the change applied from 3 September. That is the date it was laid before Parliament, not the date it starts. The Implementation section of HC 584 lists the Skilled Worker paragraphs, APP SW1 and APP SW2, among those that take effect on 8 October 2026. The Explanatory Memorandum, paragraph 6.3, says the same. Guidance will be published on GOV.UK when the change takes effect, not before.

Where the wrong date came from, checked 20 September 2026. It was not the newspapers. At 11:35 on 3 September 2026 the Home Office posted from its own account on X: "No victim of modern slavery should be trapped with an exploitative employer because of their immigration status. From today, recognised victims on Skilled Worker visas can leave abusive employers and work elsewhere without risking their immigration status." That post had been viewed about 1.5 million times when we checked it again on 20 September 2026, seventeen days after it went up, and it had not been corrected or deleted. The Statement of Changes it was announcing says the Skilled Worker paragraphs take effect on 8 October 2026. So if you have read anywhere that the rule already applies, that is where it started. A positive Conclusive Grounds decision made this week still counts, because SW 18.3 only asks that the decision falls within your current permission, but your work conditions cannot be changed under it before 8 October.

The new rule is a single paragraph, SW 18.3, inserted into Appendix Skilled Worker. In the words of the Statement of Changes, it covers a Skilled Worker who has been referred into the National Referral Mechanism and is in receipt of a positive Conclusive Grounds decision, where that decision was made during their most recent permission. Such a person may have their work conditions amended so that any work, including self-employment and voluntary work, is permitted, except work as a professional sportsperson or sports coach. Three things in that wording matter. The decision must fall within your current grant of permission. The conditions may be amended, which is Home Office language for a step that happens, not a switch that flips by itself. And self-employment is included, which the announcement did not spell out. If your conditions are varied and you do take gig or platform work, or go self-employed, be ready for the checks at the other end: the right to work checks that platforms and agencies have to run are themselves changing from October 2026, and our guide to right to work checks for gig and platform workers sets out what you will be asked to show.

The Home Office framed the purpose in two parts: to stop the sponsorship system being weaponised against vulnerable workers, and to encourage victims to come forward earlier rather than stay silent for fear of losing their status.

The workers most affected are the ones the sponsorship system has drawn in largest numbers: care workers, chefs and hospitality staff.

The part the headlines are leaving out

A lot of coverage of this change says something close to "if you are being exploited, you can now leave immediately". That is not what the rule says, and repeating it does real harm.

The rule does not apply because you are being exploited. It applies once the Home Office has formally recognised you as a victim, through a positive Conclusive Grounds decision in the National Referral Mechanism.

So the honest sequence is:

  1. Something is being done to you that may amount to modern slavery or trafficking.
  2. A First Responder refers you into the National Referral Mechanism, with your consent.
  3. The Home Office makes a Reasonable Grounds decision. This is the early, lower threshold.
  4. Later, the Home Office makes a Conclusive Grounds decision. This is the one that unlocks the new rule.
  5. If that decision is positive, the sponsor tie is lifted for the time remaining on your visa.

Between step 1 and step 5 there is a real wait, and during it you still hold a sponsored visa. Anyone telling you that you can walk out today and be fine is describing a rule that does not exist yet for you.

That does not make the change worthless. It is a genuine and significant protection at the end of the process. It means the reward for coming forward is now much larger. But you should go in knowing the shape of it.

The National Referral Mechanism, explained plainly

The National Referral Mechanism, usually shortened to NRM, is the UK's official system for identifying victims of modern slavery and trafficking and getting them support.

You cannot refer yourself. A referral has to be made by a designated organisation called a First Responder. First Responders include the police, local authorities, the Home Office itself, the National Crime Agency, and several charities including the Salvation Army, Migrant Help, Barnardo's and the Medaille Trust.

You have to consent to being referred if you are an adult. Nobody can put you in the NRM against your will.

There are two decision stages:

StageTestWhat it gives you
Reasonable GroundsThe lower threshold: the decision maker suspects but cannot prove you are a victimAccess to support, including accommodation and assistance, and a recovery period
Conclusive GroundsThe higher threshold: on the balance of probabilities you are a victimFormal recognition. This is the decision the new visa rule depends on.

How long the recovery period lasts, and what it actually protects. The table above says a positive Reasonable Grounds decision gives you a recovery period. Most material on this system, including plenty of it written by people trying to help, still puts that period at 45 days. That number is out of date. The recovery period is set by section 61 of the Nationality and Borders Act 2022, in force since 30 January 2023. Section 61(3) defines it as beginning on the day the positive Reasonable Grounds decision is made and ending on whichever of these comes later: the day your Conclusive Grounds decision is made, or the end of 30 days beginning with the Reasonable Grounds day.

The second limb is the part that gets lost, and it is the part that matters to you. Thirty days is a floor, not a ceiling. The recovery period does not expire after 30 days and leave you unprotected while you are still waiting. It runs until the Conclusive Grounds decision actually arrives, however long that takes. Set against the waiting figures in the next section, that is nothing like 30 days.

What it protects, precisely. Section 61(2) says an identified potential victim may not be removed from, or required to leave, the United Kingdom during the recovery period. That is the whole of it. It is a protection against removal and nothing else. It is not a grant of leave, not permission to work, and not a visa. If you are on a Skilled Worker visa it does not extend your permission or move its end date by a day.

Where it stops. Section 63 takes the protection away in two situations: where the competent authority is satisfied you are a threat to public order, or that you claimed to be a victim in bad faith. Section 63(2) is blunt about the consequence, and it is worth knowing before you start. When it applies, the section 61 protection and the section 65 duty to grant leave fall away together. Public order is defined broadly at section 63(3) and includes convictions for offences listed in Schedule 4 to the Modern Slavery Act 2015. Public order disqualification decisions are currently paused, which is covered in the questions section below. Separately, section 62 deals with a second Reasonable Grounds decision resting on things that happened before the first one, and there the protection is discretionary rather than automatic: the authority may apply it, and is not obliged to.

Being in the NRM is not an immigration application and it is not a visa route in itself. It is an identification and support system. The new rule bolts a specific immigration consequence onto the end of it.

Three warnings the Home Office gives about its own numbers, and you will not find them in most write-ups. First, it has revised how it counts cases awaiting a decision, and says the new figures are not comparable with the ones published before, so the move from 4,726 to 5,948 is partly a counting change rather than purely a surge. Second, the 69 days is the median age of the people still waiting, which is not the same thing as the average time a decision takes. The government still says it intends to clear the backlog by December 2026. Third, and this one affects the dates in the table rather than the figures in it. So treat the September date as firm, the November date as a plan, and that notice as applying to both. There is a third date, and it is the one that actually moves the two figures at the top of the table, because those come from the monthly bulletin rather than from the quarterly release. The September 2026 edition of that monthly bulletin is confirmed for 8 October 2026 at 09:30. It was listed for 15 October until the Home Office brought it forward on 21 September 2026, giving as its published reason that the data is now available earlier than previously, creating an opportunity to publish sooner and support more timely public scrutiny. Read those numbers carefully, because they cut both ways.

The good news is real and large. A queue whose median case has been waiting 69 days is a completely different system from the one that made people wait 678 days two years ago. If that holds, a referral made now could produce a decision in a small number of months, and the new rule would then apply.

The caution is equally real. A median is not a promise. Half of cases take longer, some considerably longer, and complex cases involving criminal investigations routinely do. And a 68% positive rate means roughly one in three Conclusive Grounds decisions is negative. A negative decision leaves you where you started, still tied to a sponsor.

A worked example, from first phone call to the new rule applying

Take a care worker we will call Amara. She came to Hertfordshire in March 2025 on a Health and Care Worker visa, which sits inside Appendix Skilled Worker, so the new SW 18.3 covers her in exactly the same way as any other Skilled Worker. Her permission runs to March 2028. Her sponsor holds her passport, takes an unexplained "accommodation and training" deduction from every payslip, and has told her that if she complains she will be reported to the Home Office and sent home. Here is how the timetable actually runs for her, using the Home Office's own figures.

DateWhat happensHer visa position
10 September 2026She calls the Modern Slavery and Exploitation Helpline on 08000 121 700. They connect her with a First Responder, in her case the Salvation Army. She agrees to a referral into the NRM.Still tied to her sponsor. Nothing has changed yet.

About 22 September 2026Reasonable Grounds decision. The statutory guidance aims for five working days. If positive, she enters a recovery period of at least 30 days with support.Still tied to her sponsor. A Reasonable Grounds decision does not trigger SW 18.3.

Three things to notice. First, the earliest point at which the rule can touch her is roughly twelve weeks after her first phone call, and that assumes a median case. If her case is one of the slower half, it is longer. Second, the Rules say her conditions "may be amended". How she asks for that, and whether it happens automatically once the decision is issued, is what the caseworker guidance due on 8 October should tell us, and we will update this page when it does. Third, she should not walk out on 10 September without advice. Her sponsor's duty to report unauthorised absence to the Home Office exists right up until her conditions are actually varied.

One question the wording leaves open: if her Conclusive Grounds decision had arrived on, say, 1 October, a week before commencement, would it still count? SW 18.3 refers to a worker who is "in receipt of" a positive decision made during their most recent permission. It does not say the decision must be dated after 8 October. That reads as covering her, but treat it as unconfirmed until the guidance says so.

Who this helps, and who it does not

It helps you if: you hold a Skilled Worker visa, you have been referred into the NRM, and you receive a positive Conclusive Grounds decision. From that point you can work for any employer for whatever time is left on your permission.

It does not help you if:

  • Your job is simply bad. Rude management, unpleasant shifts, a boss you cannot stand, none of that is modern slavery, and the NRM is the wrong route. That does not mean you have no rights. Unpaid wages, unlawful deductions and unsafe conditions are employment law matters, and ACAS is free.
  • You have not been referred. No referral, no decision, no rule change.
  • You want more time in the UK. The rule does not extend your visa by a single day. It only removes the employer tie for the time you already have.
  • Your sponsor has lost its licence. That is a separate and quite common situation with its own, much shorter, timetable. It is not covered by this change. Our sister site sponsorjobuk.com has a plain guide to the free government-backed help available when a sponsor licence is revoked, which is the part most people do not realise exists.

Two more things worth saying plainly. This is not an amnesty and it is not a shortcut to settlement. And anyone offering to "get you a Conclusive Grounds decision" for a fee is not someone you should be paying. The NRM is free, the referral is free, and paying an unregulated adviser to game it can damage your case badly.

What to do if this is happening to you

  1. If you are in immediate danger, call 999.
  2. Call the Modern Slavery and Exploitation Helpline on 08000 121 700. It is free, confidential and open 24 hours. They can explain the NRM and put you in touch with a First Responder.
  3. Keep evidence, starting now. Payslips, bank statements, rotas, messages, the contract you were promised versus the one you got, photographs of your accommodation, and a dated diary of what happened. Cases turn on documents. Keep copies somewhere your employer cannot reach.
  4. Get regulated advice, and check the register before you pay. Immigration advice in the UK must come from a solicitor or from an adviser registered with the Immigration Advice Authority. The IAA is the same body that was called the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner until 16 January 2025, so an older leaflet naming the OISC means the same regulator. There are two registers, not one. IAA advisers appear on the Adviser Register and the Adviser Finder, both reachable from the IAA pages on GOV.UK, and solicitors appear on the separate register kept by the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Look the person up on whichever register applies to them before you hand over money or documents, and use the report route on the same IAA pages if someone is charging for immigration advice and appears on neither. Charities including Migrant Help, the Salvation Army and Kalayaan advise for free. Checked 20 September 2026.
  5. For pay and conditions rather than slavery, use ACAS. Free, impartial, and the correct route for unpaid wages, unlawful deductions and holiday pay.
  6. Do not simply stop attending work without advice while you still hold a sponsored visa. Your sponsor is obliged to report unauthorised absence to the Home Office, and that can start a curtailment process against you. Get advice first.

Questions people are asking

Can I leave my employer today? No. The rule does not take effect until 8 October 2026, and even after that date it only applies once you hold a positive Conclusive Grounds decision. Until then the ordinary sponsorship rules still apply to you. Get advice before you walk out.

When does the rule actually start? 8 October 2026. That is the commencement date in the Implementation section of HC 584 for paragraphs APP SW1 and APP SW2. The 3 September date in most coverage is the day the Statement of Changes was laid, which is not the same thing.

Is there Home Office guidance on this yet? No, and that is worth knowing before you trust an explainer that claims to know how your conditions get changed. The Skilled Worker caseworker guidance on GOV.UK is dated 3 August 2026, which is how GOV.UK displays an underlying timestamp of 2 August 2026 at 23:01 UTC, and it does not mention SW 18.3 anywhere, checked again on 22 September 2026. The Explanatory Memorandum says guidance will be published when the change takes effect, so expect it on or around 8 October 2026. Until it appears, nobody outside the Home Office can tell you the exact steps for getting your work conditions amended.

I am on a Seasonal Worker, Overseas Domestic Worker or another sponsored visa. Am I covered? No. The change sits inside Appendix Skilled Worker and nowhere else. The Explanatory Memorandum lists everything HC 584 does at paragraph 4.2, and the modern slavery change appears there against Appendix Skilled Worker only. The other work routes the same Statement of Changes touches are amended for unrelated reasons, nothing to do with exploitation. So a Seasonal Worker, an Overseas Domestic Worker in a private household, and a Creative or Charity Worker get nothing at all from this rule, even though those are routes where exploitation is reported often. Health and Care Worker visas do sit inside Appendix Skilled Worker, so they are covered. If you are outside Skilled Worker the National Referral Mechanism, the helpline and ACAS are all still open to you. The visa condition change is not.

Does a Reasonable Grounds decision count? On the wording of the change, no. The rule attaches to recognition by the Competent Authorities through a Conclusive Grounds decision. Reasonable Grounds gives you support and a recovery period, which matter a great deal, but it is not the trigger for this.

Does this give me longer in the UK? No, and it is worth knowing who set the date it runs out. Under SW 18.1 of Appendix Skilled Worker a Skilled Worker is granted permission until 14 days after the end date of their certificate of sponsorship, and that certificate can run for up to five years from its start date. So the day your permission ends was chosen by the employer who issued your Certificate of Sponsorship, which in this situation may be the employer you are getting away from. SW 18.3 changes the work conditions at SW 18.2(b) to (e). It does not touch SW 18.1, so the end date does not move by a single day. The practical step is a small one. Find the end date on your Certificate of Sponsorship, add 14 days, and treat that as your real deadline. Staying as a Skilled Worker beyond it means a fresh application with a new Certificate of Sponsorship, because sponsorship is 20 of the 50 mandatory points under SW 4.1, the sponsor must be authorised by the Home Office to sponsor that job under SW 5.2, and under SW 5.3 the sponsor must be A-rated on the register of licensed sponsors unless you are continuing with the same sponsor as before. So the sponsor tie comes back at the point you need to extend, even though the conditions were lifted in the meantime. If the time you have left is short, the other door is temporary permission to stay under section 65 of the Nationality and Borders Act 2022, which the question further down about leaving the Skilled Worker route altogether covers, and it is worth raising with a regulated adviser early rather than in the last few weeks. Checked against Appendix Skilled Worker on GOV.UK on 21 September 2026.

What if my visa runs out before the Conclusive Grounds decision arrives? Then the rule does nothing for you, and this is the sharpest limit on it. SW 18.3 covers a Skilled Worker in receipt of a positive Conclusive Grounds decision, "where that decision was made during their most recent permission", and what it then does is allow the conditions in SW 18.2(b) to (e) to be amended. Both halves of that depend on permission you actually hold. If your leave has ended there are no conditions left to amend, so there is nothing for the rule to operate on, and a decision that arrives after your leave has ended is outside the wording in the first place. Put that beside the waiting times above and the problem is plain. The median case still waiting at 31 August 2026 had been waiting 69 days, half of cases wait longer, and anyone whose permission has only a few months left is in a race against it. It gets tighter, not looser, if your sponsor loses its licence or withdraws your sponsorship, because your permission can then be curtailed and the window for the decision to land in shrinks with it. Nobody outside the Home Office can yet tell you how "most recent permission" will be read in practice, because no caseworker guidance on SW 18.3 exists, and this is one of the questions the guidance expected around 8 October should answer. Two practical points meanwhile. Tell your adviser and the First Responder how long you have left at the point of referral, because it is the single fact most likely to decide whether SW 18.3 ever reaches you. And if your leave does run out while you are waiting, SW 18.3 is not the door to try: the temporary permission to stay route under section 65 of the Nationality and Borders Act 2022, covered above, is the one written for a person who does not have leave to remain. Checked against the wording of APP SW2 in HC 584 on 21 September 2026.

If I leave the employer, can I claim benefits while I wait? No, and this is the part of the change most likely to be misread. SW 18.3 amends the conditions at SW 18.2(b) to (e) of Appendix Skilled Worker, which are the work conditions. It does not touch SW 18.2(a), which reads "no access to public funds". That condition stays exactly where it is. Being recognised as a victim of modern slavery frees you to work for a different employer. It does not put you on benefits, and nothing in HC 584 says it does. The phrase is also narrower in the Rules than it sounds in ordinary speech: "public funds" is a closed, named list in paragraph 6 of the introduction to the Immigration Rules, covering housing under the named Housing Acts and named benefits including Universal Credit, income support, housing benefit, child benefit, jobseeker's allowance, personal independence payment, council tax reduction and the Scottish and Northern Ireland equivalents. If something is not on that list it is not public funds for immigration purposes, and support provided under the Modern Slavery Victim Care Contract is not on it. So asking the NRM support route what help you can get is a different thing from claiming benefits. Ask the Modern Slavery and Exploitation Helpline on 08000 121 700, or Migrant Help, what support is actually available in your case before you walk out of a job, because the answer decides whether you can afford to wait for a decision. Checked against Appendix Skilled Worker and paragraph 6 of the Immigration Rules introduction on 20 September 2026.

What happens to my partner and children? Nothing in this change touches them, and that is worth knowing, because fear of dragging the family down is part of what keeps people in a bad job. A dependent partner or dependent child of a Skilled Worker is not tied to your sponsor and never has been. SW 36.3 of Appendix Skilled Worker sets their conditions: no access to public funds, study permitted subject to the ATAS condition if they are over 18, and work, including self-employment and voluntary work, permitted except as a professional sportsperson or sports coach. That is close to the freedom SW 18.3 is about to give you. What is linked to you is the length of their permission, not who they may work for. Under SW 36.1 a partner is granted permission ending on the same date as yours, and under SW 36.2 a child is granted permission ending on the same date as whichever parent has the earlier end date. So what would affect your family is anything that shortens your own permission, not a lawful change of employer once the new rule is in force. Checked against Appendix Skilled Worker on GOV.UK, 19 September 2026.

Will my new employer need a sponsor licence? No, and the rule proves it itself. SW 18.3 permits "any work (including self-employment and voluntary work)", and a self-employed person has no employer at all, so the amended condition cannot be running through sponsorship. Once your conditions are varied, your right to work comes from your own permission rather than from a Certificate of Sponsorship, so a new employer needs no licence and assigns you nothing. The catch is somewhere else, and it is the part to plan around. Your employer will never read Appendix Skilled Worker. They will run a Home Office online right to work check on your share code, and GOV.UK says plainly what that shows them: "the types of job you are allowed to do in the UK", and how long you can work here. The Home Office draft employer guide dated 11 September 2026 defines an eVisa as an online record of a person immigration status "and the conditions of their permission", tells employers to check any work restrictions to confirm the person is permitted to do the work in question, and counts someone whose leave carries restrictions on the type of work that can be conducted as a person they cannot lawfully employ. So what actually gets you hired is not the new paragraph in the Rules. It is the varied condition arriving on your own record, where an employer can see it. Until it does, a check on your share code will still show the sponsored restriction, and a careful employer will say no, because getting it wrong costs them a civil penalty. Nobody has published how the variation reaches your record or how long it takes. The Skilled Worker caseworker guidance does not mention SW 18.3, and nor does the employer guide: the in-force edition is dated 26 June 2025 and predates HC 584 entirely, and the 11 September 2026 draft mentions SW 18.3, conclusive grounds and the National Referral Mechanism zero times, counted on 20 September 2026. So before you hand in notice, get your own share code at gov.uk/prove-right-to-work and read what it says about the types of job you are allowed to do. If it still shows the sponsored restriction, the freedom has not yet reached the place your next employer will look. Checked against GOV.UK on 20 September 2026.

What happens to settlement if I use this to leave my sponsor? The five year clock keeps running, but the application at the end of it still needs a sponsor. Appendix Skilled Worker sets the qualifying period at SW 21.1 and SW 21.2 as five years of continuous permission on the Skilled Worker route, not five years of working for one employer, so time spent on varied conditions still counts towards it. The catch is at the other end. Under the sponsorship and salary requirement for settlement, SW 24.1 and SW 24.2, the sponsor on your most recent permission must still be licensed by the Home Office on the date of decision, and that sponsor must confirm that they still require you to work for them for the foreseeable future at the required salary. Nobody can give that confirmation if you are not working for a sponsor. So the practical reading, if settlement on this route matters to you, is that the new freedom is breathing space to find a decent sponsored job rather than a permanent alternative to having one. Appendix Skilled Worker carries no modern slavery or trafficking exception to SW 24, checked on GOV.UK on 19 September 2026. Other ways of staying in the UK, including the temporary permission covered above, are separate questions and are not governed by SW 24.

What if my Conclusive Grounds decision is negative? Roughly one in three is. A reconsideration route exists, but do not trust anyone who describes it to you as a settled process, because at the moment it is not. The Home Office statutory guidance page on GOV.UK, last updated on 18 September 2026 and checked again on 19 September 2026, carries a notice at the top saying the Home Office is currently reviewing its reconsideration policy. Version 4.8 of the guidance itself, published on 18 September 2026, says on its opening pages that following the court judgment in AYA (and others) the policy at paragraph 14.227, which covered people intended for removal to a country that has signed ECAT and the ECHR, has been quashed and is no longer being applied, and that the reconsideration paragraphs, 14.228 to 14.246, are now considered on an individual basis. The practical meaning for you is this: a negative decision is still worth challenging, and nobody can promise you a fixed route or a fixed timetable for challenging it. That is a strong argument for having proper representation from the start rather than after a refusal. The deadline matters more than anything else here. A request to reconsider a negative Reasonable Grounds or Conclusive Grounds decision is normally expected within thirty calendar days of that decision, and the Home Office reconsideration note published on 18 September 2026 calls it the thirty calendar day time limit. So if your refusal arrived this week, that clock, and not the exception described below, is the thing to act on. One thing did change on 18 September 2026, and it matters most if your thirty days have already run out. The same GOV.UK page now carries a separate reconsideration note setting out an interim approach, which the Home Office says applies while the review is being finalised and is subject to Ministerial agreement. Under it a Competent Authority may still consider a reconsideration request made outside the thirty day limit in two situations. The first is where there was a clear and obvious error, which the note defines narrowly as a clear failure to apply the decision making policy in line with the guidance, or a decision made on a mistaken understanding of the material facts, rather than a difference of interpretation or of subjective judgement. The second is where new or additional evidence is provided together with an explanation of the exceptional circumstances that prevented it being submitted in time, and that evidence has to be information not previously assessed which, seen alongside what was already there, could indicate that the person is a victim. Read it for what it is. It is an interim position published while the policy is still under review, so a missed deadline is not automatically the end of the matter, and equally it is not a second chance anyone should plan around. Checked again on 20 September 2026.

Will a criminal record stop me getting a Conclusive Grounds decision? Not by itself, and at the moment the relevant power is not being used at all. Section 63 of the Nationality and Borders Act 2022 lets the Home Office disqualify someone from the recovery period and its protections on public order grounds, which covers a foreign criminal who received a custodial sentence of 12 months or more. That is a disqualification from support, which is not the same thing as a decision that you were not trafficked. In January 2026 the High Court found the Home Secretary’s general approach to these decisions unlawful in R (ABW) v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2025] EWHC 3280 (Admin). The statutory guidance page now carries a plain notice that public order disqualification decisions have been paused since Tuesday 23 December 2025, and that the Home Office will update the page when it has more to say. Checked again on 16 September 2026 and still paused. Two cautions. A pause is not a repeal and can be lifted, and none of this changes SW 18.3, which turns on a positive Conclusive Grounds decision and nothing else. If you had a public order disqualification decision before 23 December 2025 and you need support under the Modern Slavery Victim Care Contract, the Home Office says you can email the Single Competent Authority, putting Public Order Disqualification Support Request in the subject line. The address is published in the Modern Slavery Victim Care Contract support section of the Home Office page Modern slavery: how to identify and support victims. Take it from there rather than from a copy of it anywhere else, so that you are writing to the address the Home Office is currently using.

What if I do not want to stay on a Skilled Worker visa at all? There is a separate route, and until recently it was blocked for people in exactly this position. Section 65 of the Nationality and Borders Act 2022 requires the Home Office to grant temporary permission to stay, often called VTS leave, to a confirmed victim where it is needed for recovery, to seek compensation or to help an investigation. But section 65(1)(b) applies that duty only to a person who, in the words of the Act, does not have leave to remain in the United Kingdom. A Skilled Worker has leave, so the duty did not apply, even where that leave tied the person to the employer who exploited them. Two recognised victims on Skilled Worker visas challenged this in R (PS and another) v Secretary of State for the Home Department, case number JR-2026-LDS-000012. The Home Office settled by sealed consent order in August 2026 and accepted that its caseworkers do have a discretion to vary a person’s existing leave so that VTS leave can then be considered. Now the limits, because they matter. This is a concession in one case, not a change to the Rules and not an entitlement, and it does not mean VTS leave will be granted. The published caseworker guidance, Temporary permission to stay: considerations for victims of human trafficking or slavery, version 11.0, published for Home Office staff on 18 September 2026, does not mention varying existing leave anywhere in it. The words vary, varied, varying and variation do not appear in that guidance at all, and nor does the phrase skilled worker. Version 11.0 was issued to add guidance on reconsideration, so this is a fresh edition that still leaves the point unwritten, checked 19 September 2026. So anyone who wants this has to ask for it and point the Home Office at the concession, which is work for a regulated adviser rather than something you can do on a form. Note too that this and SW 18.3 are two different doors. SW 18.3 keeps you a Skilled Worker with the work conditions lifted. VTS leave takes you off the sponsored route altogether. Neither one happens automatically.

Is it safe to report my employer? This deserves a fuller answer than it usually gets, because the honest one is mixed, and the mixed parts are the whole decision. Start with what the Home Office guidance actually provides for. A referral travels from a First Responder to a Competent Authority inside the Home Office. Nothing in the adult NRM guidance provides for telling your employer or your sponsor that you have been referred, and a sponsor is nowhere on the list of people a referral is shared with. That is not a promise of secrecy, and you should not accept one from anybody, but it is the right starting point.

Three things in that guidance matter before you decide, and they are the parts people are usually not told. First, you have to consent. A First Responder must explain what the NRM is, what support it carries and what the possible outcomes are, and you have to understand it and agree before the referral goes in. Second, saying no does not mean nothing is recorded. GOV.UK says that where an adult does not consent, a Duty to Notify referral should be completed using the same online process, and that adult cases who do not consent automatically become Duty to Notify referrals. The Home Office hears about it either way. What your consent changes is whether you get an NRM decision and the support that comes with it, not whether the department is told.

Third, and this is the one that catches people out, the police are told. The guidance says every NRM referral should be referred to the police, "either on the victim’s behalf if they give consent, or as a third-party referral if they don’t give consent", and that the Competent Authority does "not require consent from the victim to do so". The same guidance is just as clear that this puts you under no obligation to take part. The referral form records whether you are willing to engage with the police, and where you are not, they deal with the person who made the referral rather than with you.

So the useful question is not whether reporting is safe in general. It is what your own employer could see and do in your particular case, and that is a question for the free, named help listed above rather than for any website. Ring the Modern Slavery and Exploitation Helpline on 08000 121 700, or speak to Kalayaan or Migrant Help, before you act. Tell them how long your permission has left, because that single fact shifts the advice more than anything else you can tell them.

The verdict

This is a real and overdue protection, and it is narrower than the headlines suggest. It rewards coming forward, but the reward arrives at the end of a formal process, not the moment you decide to leave. If exploitation is happening to you, the useful step today is the referral and the evidence, not resigning. And if what you are facing is a bad employer rather than modern slavery, ACAS and employment law are the right tools, and they work.
