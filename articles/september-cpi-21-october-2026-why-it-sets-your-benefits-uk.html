UK Update

One number, published at 7am on 21 October, sets most benefit rates next April

The ONS publishes September 2026 inflation on 21 October. For millions of households that single figure, not the Budget a week later, decides what lands in the bank from next April. Here is the mechanism, in plain terms.

The short version

  • The release date is fixed. The ONS publishes Consumer price inflation, UK: September 2026 on 21 October 2026 at 7am.
  • September is the month that counts. Working age benefit uprating conventionally uses the September CPI figure, not the latest month or an annual average.
  • This year's uprating was 3.8%. Working age benefits rose by CPI at 3.8% for the financial year ending March 2027.
  • Universal Credit got more than CPI. The Universal Credit Act 2025 requires the standard allowance to rise by September CPI plus a further 2.3%, so it is not a straight inflation uprating.
  • The Budget does not set this. The Budget on 28 October comes a week after the number that actually drives the rates.

1. What is published on 21 October, and when

On Wednesday 21 October 2026 at 7am, the Office for National Statistics publishes a release called Consumer price inflation, UK: September 2026. It is on the ONS release calendar now, and it is free to read the moment it lands.

It contains the CPI annual rate for September, along with CPIH, RPI and the detailed breakdowns. For most people reading this, one line matters: the CPI annual rate for September 2026.

There is no announcement, no speech and no press conference. A statistics release goes up on a website at 7am, and within a few hours it has quietly decided the income of several million households for the following twelve months.

2. How one month's figure becomes next April's rates

The chain is short, and once you see it, the whole benefits calendar makes sense.

  1. The ONS publishes September CPI in mid to late October.
  2. The Secretary of State for Work and Pensions carries out the annual statutory review of benefit rates, using that September figure as the measure of price growth.
  3. The proposed rates are laid before Parliament, usually in an uprating order, and published on GOV.UK as the benefit and pension rates for the coming year.
  4. The new rates take effect from the following April.

So September 2026 CPI, published on 21 October 2026, is the input for the rates that start in April 2027. The figure published this October will not touch your payments for roughly six months.

One detail worth knowing before you read the release. The ONS publishes several inflation measures in the same bulletin, and they do not agree with each other. CPI is the headline measure and the one used for benefit uprating. CPIH is the same basket with owner occupier housing costs added, and usually reads slightly differently. RPI is an older measure, no longer designated as a national statistic, but still used in some rail fares and student loan calculations. On the morning, the figure that matters for benefits is the CPI annual rate for September, and it is easy to quote the wrong one by accident.

Running the same chain backwards explains this year. September 2025 CPI produced an uprating of 3.8%, which applied to working age benefits from April 2026, covering the financial year ending March 2027. That is the rate currently in your payments.

3. The exception almost nobody mentions: Universal Credit

This is the part that gets left out of most coverage, and it is the part that changes the answer.

For most working age benefits, and for Housing Benefit personal allowances, the uprating is straightforwardly CPI. For the Universal Credit standard allowance, it is not. The Universal Credit Act 2025 requires the Secretary of State to increase the standard allowance by at least the September CPI increase and then by a further amount on top, set at 2.3% for this year. The four standard allowance rates are legislated to rise above inflation in each year from 2026/27 through to 2029/30.

The practical effect is that if you read "benefits are going up by X%" and you are a Universal Credit claimant, the standard allowance part of your award went up by more than X. Your other elements, such as the housing element or child elements, generally did not.

That distinction is why two claimants can both read the same headline percentage and see two different numbers arrive.

4. A worked example: what 3.8% did to a real claim

Marcus is 34, single, lives in Sunderland and claims Universal Credit with no housing element because he lodges with family.

For 2026/27 the Universal Credit standard allowance for a single claimant aged 25 or over is £400.14 a month. That is the published rate in the GOV.UK benefit and pension rates for 2026 to 2027.

Over a year, that standard allowance alone is £400.14 multiplied by 12, which is £4,801.68.

Now the point of the example. Marcus saw the headline "benefits up 3.8%" and worked out that his allowance must have gone up by about £14 a month. In fact the standard allowance rose by CPI plus the additional 2.3% required by the Universal Credit Act 2025, so his increase was larger than the headline. Somebody on a different working age benefit, uprated by CPI alone, got the 3.8% and nothing more.

We are deliberately not publishing a precise pounds and pence increase for Marcus here, because the exact figure depends on which elements make up his award and we have not confirmed his element mix. The rate above is confirmed. The multiplication is confirmed. Anything beyond that would be a guess dressed up as a number, and that helps nobody.

5. What to watch for on the morning

If you want to know roughly what your April 2027 rates will look like, read one line of the ONS release on 21 October: the CPI annual rate for September 2026.

Then apply the two rules above. Most working age benefits and Housing Benefit personal allowances will move by roughly that figure. The Universal Credit standard allowance will move by that figure plus the legislated additional amount.

The confirmed rates themselves appear later, on the GOV.UK page called "Benefit and pension rates", published for each tax year. That page is the only figure you should plan around. Until it appears, any specific pounds and pence prediction for April 2027, including any you see the morning after the CPI release, is somebody's arithmetic rather than a published rate.

What you should not do is assume the Budget on 28 October will change it. The uprating mechanism is statutory and runs on its own track. A Chancellor can legislate to override it, and has done in the past, but the default is that the September number wins.

Frequently asked

Why September and not a more recent month? The annual uprating review needs a fixed reference point far enough ahead of April for the rates to be laid before Parliament and for systems to be updated. September has long been the conventional month used for that review.

Does this set the state pension too? No. The state pension uses the triple lock, which takes the highest of average weekly earnings growth, CPI, and 2.5%. CPI is only one of the three inputs.

When will I see the actual April 2027 rates? The proposed benefit and pension rates are normally published on GOV.UK in the weeks after the uprating review, ahead of the April start date.

Is 3.8% the figure for next April? No. 3.8% is the uprating that applied from April 2026, covering the financial year ending March 2027. The figure for April 2027 comes from the September 2026 CPI released on 21 October 2026.
