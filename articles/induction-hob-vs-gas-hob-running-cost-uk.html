An Induction Hob Will Not Cut Your Cooking Bill If You Have Gas. Buy It For Speed And Safety, Not Savings.

Induction hobs are sold on efficiency, and the efficiency claim is true: far more of the energy goes into the pan than with a gas flame. What the adverts leave out is that the energy comes from the grid at 26.32p a kilowatt hour while gas comes at 7.97p, a ratio of more than three to one, at the Ofgem cap for October to December 2026. We ran the sum both ways, found the one kind of household where induction genuinely saves money, and set out what the real reasons to switch are.

1. The 3.3 to 1 problem

Every comparison of induction and gas starts in the wrong place, with efficiency. The right place to start is the price of the two fuels, because that is the number the efficiency has to beat.

Under the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, Direct Debit customers pay an average of 26.32p per kilowatt hour for electricity and 7.97p for gas. Divide one by the other and you get 3.30. A kilowatt hour of heat from the grid costs three and a third times what a kilowatt hour of heat from the gas main costs, before either has reached a pan.

So the question is not "is induction more efficient than gas". It is. The question is whether it is 3.3 times more efficient, because that is what it would take for the two to cost the same per meal. If induction is twice as efficient as gas, gas is still cheaper by a wide margin. Everything else in this article is a footnote to that sum.

The ratio moves each quarter with the cap. It was 3.56 in the July to September 2026 period, when electricity was 26.11p and gas 7.33p. It has narrowed slightly because gas rose more than electricity this time, and because VAT came off electricity on 1 October. It has not come anywhere near 2.

2. What the efficiency figures actually say

There is no UK energy label for hobs and no Ofgem or Energy Saving Trust efficiency figure, so what follows is the range of figures that manufacturers and engineering references use, stated as ranges because that is what they are.

Induction heats the pan directly by magnetic field, so the losses are in the electronics and in the pan's own conduction. Manufacturers claim around 85 to 90 per cent of the electricity ends up as heat in the pan. Independent measurements tend to land a little lower, particularly with small pans on large rings, but 85 per cent is a fair working figure.

Gas heats the pan by flame, and a lot of the flame heats the kitchen. Figures from 40 to 55 per cent are commonly quoted; the upper end needs a pan that covers the burner and a flame that does not lick up the sides.

Ceramic and solid-plate electric hobs sit between the two, around 70 per cent, because the element heats a glass or iron plate first and the plate heats the pan.

Now do the sum. Take the most generous case for induction, 90 per cent, and the least generous for gas, 40 per cent: induction is 2.25 times as efficient. Take a fairer pairing, 85 against 50: 1.7 times. Neither is 3.3. On the fairer pairing, gas delivers a kilowatt hour of heat into the pan for about 16p, induction for about 31p.

The Energy Saving Trust's cooking comparison, updated 21 August 2025, puts a typical hob session at between 11p and 28p in Great Britain and does not distinguish induction from gas in that range, which tells you the difference per session is a matter of pence in either direction. It also makes the point that boiling water in a kettle first saves energy on a gas hob but not money, because the kettle's electricity costs more than the gas would. The same logic applies to the whole hob.

Against a ceramic hob, induction saves about £36 a year for the same family and about £15 for a lighter cook. That is the only positive saving in the table, and it is the subject of the next section.

4. When induction does save money, and what it costs to fit

The all-electric home. If you have no gas, the comparison is induction against ceramic, and induction wins by 15 percentage points of efficiency plus its faster response, which means less time with a ring on at full power waiting for a pan to catch up. A £300 induction hob replacing a ceramic one saves £15 to £36 a year on the figures above, a payback of eight to twenty years. It will not pay for itself on energy alone inside a typical hob life, but the gap is small enough that speed and cleaning decide it, and they usually decide it in induction's favour.

The gas home that is going gas-free anyway. If you are removing the gas supply, for a heat pump or because a new-build has none, the hob has to be electric and induction is the better electric hob. The saving to count is the gas standing charge, 29.68p a day under the October cap, which is £108 a year for having a gas meter at all. That only lands if the boiler and everything else on gas goes too; a hob alone does not get you there.

The cost of switching from gas. This is the part the sum usually forgets. An induction hob draws up to 7 kW on all rings, which needs its own circuit from the consumer unit, typically on a 32 amp breaker with 6 mm cable. If the kitchen has only a 13 amp socket where the hob goes, an electrician has to run a new circuit, and that is commonly £200 to £500 before the hob. The gas supply to the old hob then has to be capped by a Gas Safe engineer. Add pans: induction needs a magnetic base, and while most modern stainless and cast iron pans work, aluminium and copper ones do not. Budget for all of that and a £300 hob is a £600 to £1,000 job.

The non-money reasons, which are real. Induction boils a pan noticeably faster than gas at the same nominal power because so little heat escapes. The surface stays cool enough that spills do not bake on. There is no flame, no combustion products in the kitchen air, and no risk of a ring left on igniting a tea towel. Those are good reasons to choose it when you are buying a hob anyway. They are not reasons to replace a working gas hob, and nobody should tell you the bills will thank you for it.

5. Worked example: Dev and Hannah in Exeter

Three-bed terrace, mains gas, two adults and a toddler, gas hob from 2009, kitchen being refitted

The kitchen fitter has quoted for a gas hob or an induction hob in the new worktop and told them induction "pays for itself in energy". They cook on the hob most evenings, call it 1.2 kWh of useful heat a day.

Gas hob, our arithmetic. 1.2 kWh at 50 per cent efficiency is 2.4 kWh of gas a day, 876 kWh a year, £70 a year at 7.97p.

Induction hob. 1.2 kWh at 85 per cent is 1.41 kWh of electricity a day, 515 kWh a year, £136 a year at 26.32p.

Difference: induction costs £66 a year more. The fitter's claim is wrong for their house. There is no payback to calculate.

What decides it instead. The new circuit is already in the electrician's quote because the oven needs one, so the marginal cost of induction is about £150 in wiring plus two new pans. Hannah wants the cool surface with a toddler in the kitchen. They choose induction, knowingly, for about £66 a year more in running cost and a safer hob. That is a perfectly good decision. It is just not the decision the fitter described.

6. The honest verdict

If you have mains gas, do not replace a working gas hob with induction to save money, because it will cost you more to run, about £60 to £80 a year for a family that cooks daily, at the October 2026 cap. The efficiency advantage is real and it is nowhere near the 3.3 to 1 price gap between the fuels.

If you are buying a hob anyway, or your home has no gas, induction is the better hob for speed, safety and cleaning, and against a ceramic hob it even edges the running cost. Buy it for those reasons, budget for the dedicated circuit and the pans, and ignore anyone who tells you the bills will fall.

Frequently asked

Is induction cheaper to run than gas in the UK? No, not while electricity costs 3.3 times what gas costs per unit, which it does under the October 2026 cap at 26.32p against 7.97p. Induction is roughly 1.7 to 2.25 times as efficient as gas, which is not enough to close a 3.3 to 1 price gap.

How much does an induction hob cost to run per year? On our assumption of 1.5 kWh of useful heat into pans a day and 85 per cent efficiency, about 644 kWh a year, which is £170 at 26.32p. A gas hob doing the same cooking is about £87 to £109.

Is induction cheaper than a ceramic hob? Yes, by a little. Induction at about 85 per cent efficiency against ceramic at about 70 per cent saves £15 to £36 a year depending on how much you cook. That is the one comparison where induction wins on money.

Do I need an electrician to fit an induction hob? Usually. A full-size induction hob draws up to 7 kW and needs its own 32 amp circuit, not a plug socket. If the kitchen does not already have a cooker circuit in the right place, that is a new run of cable from the consumer unit, and the gas supply to the old hob must be capped by a Gas Safe engineer.
