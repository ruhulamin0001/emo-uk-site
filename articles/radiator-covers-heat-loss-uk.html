Everyone Has the Radiator Cover Arithmetic Wrong, Including the People Selling Radiators

The standard claim is that a radiator cover blocks 20% of your heat and costs you about £40 a year per radiator. That number is repeated everywhere and it does not hold up, because the heat a cover blocks does not leave your house. The true cost is a good deal smaller. The answer is still not to buy one, and here is the more interesting reason why.

The short version

  • The £40 a year per radiator figure does not stand up. It assumes the blocked heat is destroyed. It is not. It is still inside your house and it still ends up heating the room.
  • The real loss is the heat pushed into the wall behind. A cover raises the temperature of the wall pocket behind the radiator, and on an external wall some of that extra heat conducts outside. That is the only genuine loss, and it is a fraction of the headline claim.
  • A solid fronted cabinet is the one to avoid. It blocks radiant heat and restricts the convection current, so the room takes longer to warm and feels cooler at the same thermostat setting.
  • The measurable cost is comfort and response time, not pounds. Your thermostat still gets the room to temperature. It just works harder and longer to do it.
  • Radiator reflector foil is the cheap fix, and it addresses the actual loss. It costs a few pounds and goes behind the radiator, cover or no cover.
  • Buy a cover for looks or for child safety. Never for warmth, and never believe a saving claim attached to one.

1. Where the £40 a year claim comes from

Search for radiator covers and you will meet the same calculation on a dozen sites. It goes like this. A radiator is rated at 1,500 watts. A cover blocks 20% of the output, which is 300 watts. A UK heating season is about 2,000 hours. 300 watts for 2,000 hours is 600 kWh. Multiply by an energy price and you get a number in the tens of pounds, per radiator, per year.

The arithmetic is correct. The premise is not.

Before going further, it is worth noticing who publishes this calculation. On the results we looked at, the sources were radiator manufacturers, bathroom retailers, boiler comparison sites and insulation firms. Several of them sell radiators. A figure that makes covering your radiator sound expensive is a figure that sells radiators, and that is not a reason to dismiss it, but it is a reason to check it rather than repeat it. When we looked for a figure from a body with nothing to sell, we did not find one, and we are saying so rather than dressing up a retailer's number as an authority's.

2. Why the blocked heat does not disappear

A radiator does not consume energy. It is a heat exchanger. Hot water goes in at one end, cooler water comes out of the other, and the difference is heat that has left the water and entered your house.

Put a wooden box around it and ask where that heat goes. It does not get destroyed by the wood. It warms the wood, and the wood warms the air around it, and that air is in your living room. It slows down. It changes shape. It spreads out differently. But it is still in the same building, on the inside of the insulation, and a thermostat in that room will still eventually read 20C.

Heat blocked by a cover is not lost. It is delayed. The only heat you actually lose is the heat that leaves the building.

This is the same reason that leaving your oven door open after cooking does not waste energy in winter, and the same reason that an inefficient light bulb is very slightly less wasteful in January than in July. Anything that turns electricity or gas into heat inside your thermal envelope is heating your house, whether you meant it to or not.

So the £40 a year figure is measuring the wrong thing. It is measuring heat that did not come out of the front of the radiator, and then charging you for all of it as though it had gone out of the window.

3. What a cover genuinely costs you

There are three real costs and none of them is the headline number.

One. The wall behind gets hotter. This is the genuine energy loss and it is worth taking seriously. A cover creates a partly enclosed pocket of air around the radiator. That pocket runs hotter than the room. If the radiator is on an external wall, the temperature difference across that wall is now larger, so more heat conducts through it to the outside. Some of your blocked heat really does leave the building, and that share is the honest loss.

How big is it? It depends entirely on the wall. On a filled cavity wall or an insulated solid wall it is small. On an uninsulated solid wall it is meaningfully larger. Nobody can give you a single national figure for it honestly, and we are not going to invent one. What we can say is that it is a fraction of the heat the cover blocks, not all of it, which is why the standard calculation overstates the case.

Two. The room takes longer to warm up. This is real, measurable and the thing people actually notice. A solid fronted cover blocks the radiant heat entirely and slows the convection current that drives warm air up and across the ceiling. The room still reaches temperature. It reaches it later, and the boiler runs for longer to get there. That extra running has a cost, though it is much smaller than treating the whole 20% as wasted.

Three. The thermostat may be fooled. If your room thermostat or a thermostatic radiator valve sits inside or immediately above the cover, it is sitting in that pocket of hot air, and it will switch the heating off while the rest of the room is still cold. This is the failure mode that genuinely costs people comfort, and unlike the others it is entirely avoidable by moving the sensor.

5. Worked example: the Docherty living room in Stirling

A 1930s semi in Stirling with uninsulated solid walls at the front. The living room radiator sits under the bay window on the external wall, and there is a solid fronted wooden cabinet over it that came with the house. The room is always the last one to warm up.

What the standard calculation says. 1,800W radiator, 25% blocked, 450W, 2,000 hours, 900 kWh of heat. At 7.97p per kWh of gas and 88% boiler efficiency, that is £81 a year.

Why that is wrong. Most of those 900 kWh are still in the living room. The cabinet is not a hole in the wall.

What is actually happening. The room warms slowly because the cabinet is solid fronted, so the heating runs longer each evening. And the pocket behind the radiator sits hotter against an uninsulated solid wall, so a real but modest share of the heat conducts outside.

The fix that costs almost nothing. Reflector foil behind the radiator, about £10, which addresses the wall loss directly. Check the TRV is not shrouded by the cabinet. If the room still lags, take the solid front off the cabinet and fit a mesh panel, which is a £20 job.

The fix that costs £0 and is worth more. Turning the boiler flow temperature to 60C, worth about £68 a year across the whole house.

The Dochertys do not need to spend £81 a year on anything, because they were never losing £81 a year. What they had was a cold room, and the cure for a cold room is to stop the cabinet blocking the airflow, not to buy a new radiator.

Frequently asked

Should I take my radiator cover off? If it is solid fronted and the room is slow to warm, yes, or at least replace the front with a mesh or lattice panel. If it is open slatted and the room is comfortable, leave it. The saving from removing an open cover is small enough that it is not worth the argument with whoever likes the look of it.

Do covers help in a house with children? Yes, and this is the one genuinely good reason to fit one. A radiator running at 60C flow temperature is not dangerous, but at 75C or 80C a surface is hot enough to hurt a small child. A cover is a reasonable answer. So, incidentally, is turning the flow temperature down, which makes the radiator cooler to touch and saves gas at the same time.

What about those magnetic or thin decorative covers? They sit close to the radiator surface and do not enclose it, so they interfere with the convection current far less than a cabinet. They are the least harmful type. They still do not save you anything.

Is a radiator shelf different? Yes, and a shelf is generally harmless or slightly helpful. It deflects rising warm air out into the room rather than letting it run straight up a cold window. That is not a large effect either, but it is at least pointing the right way.

The honest verdict

Do not buy a radiator cover to save money, because it cannot save you money and nothing about it has a payback period.

Do not believe the £40 a year figure either. It comes from sources that sell radiators and it treats blocked heat as destroyed heat, which is not how a house works. The real cost of an open fronted cover is small, the real cost of a solid fronted cabinet is a colder feeling room and a boiler that runs longer, and the real loss in both cases is the extra heat pushed into an external wall.

If you want a cover because the radiator is ugly or because you have a toddler, buy one, buy an open fronted one, put reflector foil behind the radiator, and keep the thermostat out of the box. If you want a lower heating bill, this is the wrong page. Go and turn your boiler's flow temperature down instead.
