1. What is actually being sold

Three kinds of product sit under this heading. A prompt is a written instruction for an AI model, sold on the basis that it reliably produces a particular kind of output, and PromptBase is the marketplace built for that. A template is a pre-built structure in a tool other people already use: a Notion workspace, a Google Sheets tracker, a Canva layout, a set of Claude skills or project instructions. A pack is several of either bundled together. All three are digital files that cost nothing to reproduce, which is why the pitch sounds so good and why the market is so crowded.

The buyer's side is worth stating plainly. A prompt has value only while the model behaves the way the prompt assumes, and models change several times a year. A template has value while the tool keeps its features. Both decay, and the seller who stops updating is selling something that stops working, which the reviews will say. The honest framing is that you are selling a small piece of maintained software, not a file.

The pattern across all three is the same as everywhere else in online selling: the platform's own traffic is what you pay for. PromptBase's 0% and Gumroad's 10% apply to buyers you brought yourself. If you had an audience that could be sent a link, you would not need the marketplace, and the beginner does not have one, so the honest fee to plan around is the higher figure in each row.

4. The number nobody publishes

This is the section other guides skip. We looked for a public, dated figure for what a typical prompt or template seller earns: a marketplace disclosure, a survey with a stated method, a company filing, a creator's own published revenue page that could be opened and read. We did not find one that met that test, and we are not going to make one up. The figures that circulate are screenshots from individual sellers, which are unverifiable and, more importantly, are posted by the people it worked for. The person who listed twelve prompts and sold none does not post a screenshot.

Under the UK advertising rules, the CAP Code, claims about what people can earn from an opportunity have to be substantiated and have to reflect typical results rather than exceptional ones. A screenshot of one seller's best month fails both parts. So the responsible statement is: the typical outcome is unknown, the visible outcomes are the survivors, and the platforms, which do know, have chosen not to say. Plan on that basis.

5. What would have to be true for it to pay

Since the income is unknown, turn the question round. Decide what your time is worth, count the hours, and work out the sales that would cover them. Take ten hours a month: making two or three products, testing them, writing listings, answering buyer messages and updating for model changes. At the National Living Wage of GBP 12.71 that is GBP 127.10. At GBP 3.20 a sale it needs 40 sales a month to break even, or about 1.3 a day, every day, across your whole catalogue. At GBP 25 an hour, which is a modest freelance rate, it needs 79 sales a month.

6. A worked example in Wolverhampton

Aisha in Wolverhampton is a school administrator who has built a set of Claude project instructions and a matching spreadsheet for producing parent newsletters, because she does it every week. She lists the pack on Gumroad at GBP 4 and a shorter prompt version on PromptBase at the same price, spending about eight hours across a month on the products, the listing copy and two updates.

Month one: three sales on Gumroad direct, from people in a school office forum where she posted it, and one on PromptBase. She keeps GBP 9.69 and GBP 3.20, so GBP 12.89 for eight hours, or GBP 1.61 an hour. Month two: seven sales, GBP 22.40 or so, four hours of work. Month three: five sales. She is under the GBP 1,000 trading allowance by a mile, so there is nothing to tell HMRC, though she keeps a record of every sale as the allowance rules require, and the tax rules guide explains what changes if she ever passes GBP 1,000 in a tax year.

Her stop condition was written down before she listed anything: three months without a month of ten sales. She reached it. What she got for her twenty hours was about GBP 48, a working product she uses herself every week, and proof that a handful of other school offices would pay for it, which is the beginning of a much better idea: selling the setup as a service to schools directly, at a price that reflects the hours, rather than the file at GBP 4.

The honest verdict

Selling prompts and templates costs almost nothing to try and, for most people, pays almost nothing. The published fees leave about GBP 3.20 of a GBP 4 sale on any of the three platforms, ten hours a month needs about 40 sales to beat the minimum wage, and no platform or survey we could find says how many sellers get there. Do it as a cheap test of whether anyone wants your work, set a stop condition of three months without ten sales in a month, and treat the first person who buys as a lead for a service, not a customer for a file. As a plan to earn, this is not worth your time.

Frequently asked

How much do prompt sellers make? We do not know and neither does anyone publishing a figure without a source. PromptBase, Gumroad and Etsy do not publish seller income, and individual screenshots are unverifiable and come from the sellers it worked for. This article works from the published fees only.

Which platform has the lowest fees? On a GBP 4 sale they are within a few pence of each other: about GBP 3.20 kept on PromptBase's marketplace, GBP 3.23 on Gumroad direct, GBP 3.21 on Etsy. Gumroad Discover at 30% is the expensive route. Where the buyers are matters far more than the fee.

Do I need to tell HMRC? Not while your gross trading income is GBP 1,000 or less in a tax year, under the trading allowance, though you must keep records. Above GBP 1,000 you register for Self Assessment by the 5 October after the end of the tax year.

Do I need Claude Pro to write prompts? No. The free plan is enough to write and test prompts. Pro at USD 20 a month before tax adds usage and products such as Cowork and Claude Code, which matter for services, not for selling files.
