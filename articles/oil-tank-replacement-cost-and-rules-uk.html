The Oil Tank Question Nobody Asks Until the Ground Smells

A domestic heating oil tank is one of the few things in a garden that can generate a five-figure bill in a single weekend. The rules are less demanding than most people assume, which is precisely the problem, because the cheapest compliant option is not the one you want. Here is the arithmetic.

The short version

  • The Control of Pollution (Oil Storage) (England) Regulations 2001 do not apply to a container of 3,500 litres or less used wholly or mainly for a private dwelling. Almost every domestic tank falls under that line.
  • Building Regulations Approved Document J applies instead, and it is Part J, plus the OFTEC risk assessment, that drives whether you need a bund in practice.
  • A bund is a second skin. In practice most above-ground domestic tanks end up needing one, because of proximity to a drain, a watercourse, a borehole or a hard surface that oil could run across.
  • A replacement bunded plastic tank fitted typically runs £1,500 to £3,500, against £900 to £1,800 for single skin. The gap is a few hundred pounds of the total job.
  • A leak clean-up commonly runs to five figures, and many household policies limit or exclude oil pollution cover, which makes the bund one of the cheapest insurance products available.

1. Which rules actually apply to a domestic tank

There are two regimes and most confusion comes from people quoting the wrong one.

The Control of Pollution (Oil Storage) (England) Regulations 2001. These cover oil storage at industrial, commercial and institutional sites, and at domestic sites above a threshold. They expressly do not apply to oil stored wholly or mainly as part of a private dwelling where the container's capacity is 3,500 litres or less. Standard domestic tanks are typically 1,000 to 2,500 litres, so the overwhelming majority of homes sit outside these regulations entirely.

Building Regulations, Approved Document J. This is the one that actually governs your tank. It covers combustion appliances and fuel storage systems, and for oil it sets out the fire separation distances, the base requirements, and the pollution provisions for tanks of 3,500 litres or less serving a dwelling. Installing or replacing a tank is notifiable work.

Alongside those sits OFTEC, the industry body, whose technical standards and risk assessment method are what a competent installer will actually be working to. An OFTEC registered technician can self-certify the installation to building control, exactly as a Gas Safe engineer does for gas.

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own equivalents, and Northern Ireland in particular, where oil heating is far more common, has its own building control requirements. Check locally rather than assuming the English position transfers.

2. When a bund is required, and why the answer is usually yes

A bunded tank is a tank inside a tank. The outer skin holds at least 110% of the inner one's capacity, so a failure of the inner tank is contained rather than released.

Because the 2001 regulations do not bite below 3,500 litres, the requirement comes from the Part J risk assessment, and it is triggered by things that are extremely common in rural gardens:

  • The tank is within 10 metres of a watercourse, including a ditch, a stream or a pond.
  • The tank is within 50 metres of a borehole, spring or well used for drinking water.
  • Oil spilled from the tank could reach an open drain or a loose manhole cover.
  • The tank is on hard standing that extends beyond the base, so a spill would run rather than soak.
  • The vent or fill point is outside the bund, or the fill point cannot be seen from the tank.
  • The tank supplies a building other than a single family dwelling.

Read that list against an average rural garden and it is difficult to find a tank that clears all six. Which is why competent installers usually specify bunded as standard and why a quote offering single skin deserves the question: which of the six triggers have you assessed as not applying, and have you written that down.

3. How long a tank lasts and how to tell it is finished

Plastic tanks are generally reckoned on a ten to twenty year life, steel tanks somewhat longer where they have been maintained and much shorter where they have not. Insurers and OFTEC inspectors will often start asking questions at around fifteen years.

The warning signs are worth knowing because they are visible from a distance.

On plastic: bulging or deformation of the sides, a chalky bloom from ultraviolet degradation, star crazing around fittings, and any weeping at the outlet. Plastic that has gone brittle fails suddenly rather than gradually.

On steel: rust streaks below the seams, a soft patch anywhere on the underside, corrosion at the base where it meets the supports, and water in the bottom drawn off at the sludge valve.

On both: unexplained consumption. A tank that empties faster than the weather explains is either leaking or being siphoned, and both need investigating the same week.

Worked example: the Ellingham household, Fakenham

Detached house on oil, 1,360 litre single skin plastic tank installed in 2009, sitting on a concrete slab about seven metres from a field ditch.

The trigger. Their home insurer's renewal asked the tank's age for the first time. At seventeen years it was past what the insurer would treat as maintained, and oil pollution liability was capped in the small print at a figure well below any realistic clean-up.

The quotes. Like-for-like single skin, £1,420 fitted. Bunded 1,300 litre plastic with a new base and a relocated fill point, £2,380 fitted. Difference, £960.

The seven metres decided it. Inside ten metres of a watercourse, so a bund was indicated by the risk assessment regardless of preference. The installer, OFTEC registered, self-certified to building control at no extra charge.

Over an expected 18-year life, £2,380 is £132 a year. The old tank had cost them roughly £95 a year over its 17 years, which looked cheaper right up until the renewal letter arrived.

5. What actually happens when one leaks

The five-figure clean-up figure gets quoted so often that it has stopped meaning anything. Here is the sequence that produces it.

Kerosene does not sit still. It moves through soil, follows land drains, and in the wrong ground conditions reaches groundwater. It also moves through the fabric of a building, which is why the first sign for many households is not a wet patch outside but a persistent smell indoors that nobody can locate.

Once it is in the ground, the response is an environmental one rather than a plumbing one. A consultant investigates to establish the extent, which means trial pits and sampling. Contaminated soil is excavated and removed as controlled waste. Where a building is affected, sub-floor ventilation or a vapour barrier may be needed, and in bad cases floors come up. Where a watercourse is involved, the Environment Agency becomes a party to the process.

That is why the bill is large, and why it is so variable. Two identical tanks failing in two identical gardens can produce a £6,000 job and a £60,000 job depending entirely on what was downhill of them.

Report a spill on the Environment Agency incident hotline, 0800 80 70 60, and tell your insurer the same day. Do not attempt to dig it out yourself, and do not hose it down, which spreads it.

6. The honest verdict

Buy bunded. The whole-life difference is under ten pounds a year and it removes the only genuinely catastrophic financial risk attached to heating your house with oil.

Use an OFTEC registered installer so the work is self-certified to building control, and get the risk assessment in writing, because that document is what your insurer will want to see.

If your tank is over fifteen years old, check it this month. Bulging, chalking, rust at the seams or consumption the weather cannot explain are all reasons to replace now rather than after the ground starts to smell, because once oil is in the soil the tank is the cheapest part of the problem.

Frequently asked

Do I need building control approval to replace a tank? The work is notifiable under Building Regulations. An OFTEC registered technician can self-certify it, which is the normal and simplest route. A non-registered installer means submitting a building notice and having the council inspect.

Does house insurance cover an oil leak? Sometimes, often with a cap, and sometimes not at all. Third-party pollution liability, remediation of your own land, and the cost of the lost oil are three different things and may be treated differently. Read the policy wording before you need it.

Can I move the tank somewhere better while I am at it? Yes, and a replacement is the cheapest time to do it. Part J sets fire separation distances from buildings and boundaries, and moving further from a ditch or drain can change the risk assessment outcome in your favour.

What do I do with the old tank? It is the installer's job to drain, clean and remove it, and residues are controlled waste. Make sure removal and disposal are itemised in the quote rather than assumed.
