Affiliate Marketing in the UK for Beginners: The Honest Version

21 July 2026 · 6 min read · Checked July 2026

The short version
  • UK brands spent £1.8bn on affiliate and partner marketing in 2025 and it generated £20.7bn in revenue, a 15 to 1 return. The channel is real. That does not mean your share of it will be.
  • The UK is the third largest affiliate market in the world after the US and China, and the biggest in Europe, accounting for about 15% of global affiliate activity.
  • Amazon Associates UK pays roughly 1% to 12% depending on category. Most physical goods sit at 3% to 7%. Video games and consoles are 1%. Luxury beauty is the top rate.
  • You must disclose. The ASA and CMA require the commercial relationship to be upfront, prominent and unambiguous, before the reader engages with the content, not buried at the bottom.
  • The commonly quoted £35,000 average UK affiliate income is an average of people who already earn something. The median beginner earns nothing for months.

1. How the money actually moves

You send someone to a retailer with a tracked link. They buy. The retailer pays you a percentage. That is the whole model, and its simplicity is exactly why it attracts so much nonsense.

Three details matter more than the commission rate.

The cookie window. Amazon UK gives you 24 hours. Most networks give 30 days. That difference is enormous. On Amazon, if your reader adds the item to a basket and buys it four days later, you get nothing. On a 30 day network you still get paid.

Returns and cancellations. Commission is reversed when a customer returns the item. Fashion affiliates routinely see 25 to 40% of commission clawed back. Your dashboard number in week one is not your payment in week eight.

Payment terms. Networks typically pay 30 to 60 days after the month in which the sale validated. A January sale can land in your bank in April. Plan cash flow accordingly, which mostly means do not plan any.

3. The traffic maths nobody shows you

Here is the sum that gets left out of every course sales page.

A realistic affiliate article converts 1% to 3% of readers into clicks that lead to a purchase, and that is for genuine buying intent content. Informational content converts far lower.

Say you write a product review that ranks and pulls 1,000 visits a month. At a 2% purchase rate that is 20 sales. At an average order of £60 and a 4% Amazon commission, that is £48 a month. From an article that took you six hours to write and eight months to rank.

That is not a reason to quit. It is the reason the model only works at scale, or on high value products, or both. You need either fifty articles like that, or an article selling a £120 a year hosting plan at £60 a sale.

The argument for doing it anyway is what happens in year two. Those 96 articles keep earning without further work, and the ones that ranked keep climbing. Daniel's month 24 is far more likely to be £700 a month than £210, because the asset compounds and the marginal article gets easier. Affiliate income is not a wage, it is a slow build with a very unpleasant first year.

If you need money this month, this is the wrong thing to start. Something like a side hustle with immediate cash flow is a better fit, and you can build the site alongside it.

5. Networks worth joining in the UK

  • Amazon Associates UK. Easy to join, converts well, pays badly, 24 hour cookie. You must make three qualifying sales in 180 days or the account closes.
  • Awin. The biggest UK network by far. Thousands of British retailers, 30 day cookies, proper reporting. Some merchants approve instantly, most review your site. There is a refundable deposit to join as a publisher.
  • Impact and Partnerize. Where a lot of larger brands and SaaS companies now sit.
  • Direct programmes. Many hosting companies, software firms and UK retailers run their own. Rates are usually better than the networks because there is no middle layer.

Apply once you have a genuine site with real content. Applying with three thin posts and no traffic is how you collect rejections, and some networks remember.

6. The disclosure rules, and what happens if you ignore them

This is the part beginners get wrong and it is not optional.

The ASA enforces the CAP Code, and the CMA enforces consumer protection law. Their position is consistent: a commercial relationship must be upfront, prominent and unambiguous, and the reader must know before they engage with the content, not after.

In practice:

  • On a blog post, put the disclosure at the top, above the first affiliate link. Something plain: "This article contains affiliate links. We may earn a commission if you buy through them, at no extra cost to you."
  • On social, #ad or Ad at the start of the caption, in the first frame of a video, or in the audio. Not the eleventh hashtag. Not after the read more fold.
  • Gifted products count. Brand ambassador content counts. Whitelisted creator ads count.
  • Amazon additionally requires the exact phrase "As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases" in its programme terms.

The ASA cannot fine you directly, but it names non compliant advertisers publicly, refers persistent offenders to the CMA and to Trading Standards, and can have paid ads removed. The CMA can and does take enforcement action. More practically for a small site, undisclosed affiliate links are also a Google spam policy problem, and losing your rankings costs you far more than the disclosure ever would.

7. Tax on affiliate income

Affiliate commission is trading income. It is taxable.

  • There is a £1,000 trading allowance. Below that in a tax year, from all self-employed sources combined, you generally do not need to declare it.
  • Above £1,000 you must register for Self Assessment and file a return. Register by 5 October following the end of the tax year in which you crossed it.
  • Commission counts when it is earned and confirmed, not when the network pays you. That timing difference catches people at year end.
  • Payments in foreign currency convert at the rate on the date received. Keep the record.
  • You can deduct genuine costs: hosting, domain, themes, stock images, a proportion of broadband. Not your entire laptop if you also use it for Netflix.

This is general information rather than tax advice. If the numbers get significant, an accountant costs less than getting it wrong.

8. Five mistakes that kill beginner sites

  1. Reviewing things you have never touched. Google's reviews guidance explicitly rewards evidence of first hand use: your own photos, measurements, comparisons. Rewriting the manufacturer blurb is the single most common reason a review site never ranks.
  2. Chasing the highest commission instead of the best fit. A coffee blog pushing web hosting converts at nothing and damages trust.
  3. One network, one merchant. Amazon has cut its rates before. Build on a single programme and you have handed someone else the switch.
  4. Writing for the keyword rather than the buyer. "Best budget X UK 2026" with no actual recommendation is a page nobody links to and nobody converts on.
  5. Giving up at month six. Month six is when it looks pointless and is, statistically, roughly when it starts working.

Myths that cost people money

"Affiliate marketing is passive income." It becomes semi passive after a couple of years and a lot of unpaid work. In year one it is a job you pay to do. The word passive is doing enormous marketing work in that sentence.

"The average UK affiliate earns £35,000." That figure comes from surveys of people who identify as affiliate marketers, which excludes everyone who tried and earned nothing. Survivorship bias. The median beginner earns a two figure sum in year one, if that.

"You need a course." Most of what the £497 course teaches is on the Awin and Google documentation for free. What courses genuinely sell is structure and accountability. Decide whether that is worth £497 to you, but do not confuse it with secret information.

The honest verdict

This helps you if you already write about something you know properly, you can commit two years, and you have income from elsewhere in the meantime. It is a genuinely good long term asset. UK brands are putting £1.8bn a year into this channel and they need publishers.

This does not help you if you need money in the next six months, you do not enjoy writing, or you plan to build it on Amazon commissions alone. In those cases you will spend 400 hours to earn less than a fortnight of part time work would have paid you.

Nothing to buy here. Start free, on a £3 a month host and a £9 domain, and do not spend money on tools until something is earning.

Frequently asked

How long until I earn anything? Realistically three to eight months for the first commission, and twelve to twenty four months before it is meaningful. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

Do I need a limited company? No. Start as a sole trader. Revisit it if profits get into the tens of thousands.

Can I do it without a website? You can, on YouTube, Pinterest or a newsletter. The disclosure rules apply identically, and you are building on rented land. A site you own is the safer base.

Is it worth it in 2026 with AI search answering everything? Generic informational content is being hollowed out, that part is true. Content with first hand testing, real photographs and an actual opinion is holding up better, because a model cannot manufacture the experience of having used the thing.
